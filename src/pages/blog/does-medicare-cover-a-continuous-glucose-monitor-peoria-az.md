**Yes. Medicare Part B covers a continuous glucose monitor, or CGM, and its sensors for people with diabetes who use insulin of any kind or who have a history of problematic low blood sugar.** Your practitioner has to order it, and you pay {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount after the {{partB.deductible}} Part B deductible, as long as the supplier accepts assignment. A Medicare Supplement such as Plan G picks up that {{partB.coinsurance}}.

That has been the rule nationally since CMS loosened its coverage policy in April 2023. Before then, insulin users generally had to take three or more doses a day to qualify. Now one daily dose of long-acting insulin is enough.

This comes up often in my Peoria appointments because diabetes is common here: CDC's PLACES estimates put diagnosed diabetes at roughly one adult in ten across Maricopa County. The question I actually get is rarely "is it covered?" It is "why did my sensors stop coming?" Almost every time, the answer is in the paperwork below rather than in the plan.

## Who qualifies: insulin, or a history of lows

Medicare's coverage policy for glucose monitors, local coverage determination L33822, lists the conditions. You need all of these:

- **A diabetes diagnosis**, type 1 or type 2.
- **Training**, for you or a caregiver, on the specific CGM prescribed. The prescription itself counts as the record of that training.
- **A device used within its FDA-approved indications**, prescribed to improve your glucose control.
- **A visit before the first order**, in person or by Medicare-approved telehealth, within the six months before your practitioner writes the prescription.

Then you have to meet **one of two** clinical tests:

1. **You use insulin.** Any insulin, any number of doses. Pump, pen or vial makes no difference.
2. **You have a history of problematic hypoglycemia.** Either more than one level 2 low (glucose under 54 mg/dL) that kept happening after more than one attempt to adjust your medication or treatment plan, or one level 3 low, where your thinking or movement was impaired enough that someone else had to help you recover.

The second test is the one that surprises people. A Peoria retiree with type 2 diabetes on oral medication can qualify without ever starting insulin if their chart documents those lows. The chart has to show them, though. A remembered bad morning that never made it into the record does not count.

## The six-month visit that keeps the sensors coming

Coverage does not stop with the first order. **Your treating practitioner has to see you at least every six months** and record that you are using the CGM as prescribed. The visit can be in person or a Medicare-approved telehealth appointment.

The supplier checks for that visit before it ships, because Medicare looks for it within the six months before the date on each claim. If your endocrinologist books you seven months out, your next box of sensors can sit unshipped while the supplier waits for a new note. CGS, one of the contractors that process Medicare equipment claims, says plainly that there is no grace period.

The simplest fix is to book the next visit at the end of each one, and to tell the scheduler it has to fall inside six months. If you split the year between Peoria and a summer place, a telehealth visit with your Arizona doctor counts.

Two details are easy to miss. First, on Original Medicare the CGM is a **Part B** item, not a drug, so it does not count toward the {{partD.cap}} Part D cap and a stand-alone drug plan will not pay for it. Second, the {{partB.coinsurance}} on Original Medicare alone has no ceiling. Over years of sensors that adds up, which is one practical reason people with insulin-treated diabetes tend to value a Supplement. The [Plan G vs. Plan N comparison](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/) walks through that choice.

Insulin has its own protection. Under the Inflation Reduction Act, a month's supply of insulin covered by Part D costs no more than $35, and insulin used in a traditional pump under Part B has the same $35 monthly limit with no deductible.

## Advantage plans in Maricopa County: same coverage, different route

A Medicare Advantage plan has to cover a CGM for anyone who meets Original Medicare's conditions. Since 2024, federal rules at 42 CFR §422.101 bar Advantage plans from using internal coverage criteria stricter than Medicare's own published policy when that policy is fully set out, as L33822 is.

What a plan can still control is how you get the device. In practice that means:

- **A preferred brand.** Switching models can require an exception request from your practitioner.
- **A contracted supplier or a pharmacy route.** Sensors you used to get by mail may now come from a network pharmacy, or the reverse.
- **Prior authorization** before the first order, and sometimes again at renewal.

For someone with diabetes, the Maricopa County menu also includes a group of plans built around it. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, 20 of the county's 25 chronic-condition special needs plans for 2027 are designed for diabetes and heart conditions. A CGM is covered on all of them. What differs is the network, the drug list and the cost sharing. If you are weighing one, [this explainer on C-SNPs in Arizona](/blog/what-is-a-c-snp-medicare-plan-arizona-2027/) lists what they accept and how enrollment works. Whatever plan you are looking at, check its supplier list for your exact sensor before December 7, not after January 1.

## If you have type 2 diabetes and do not take insulin

If you manage type 2 diabetes with pills, a GLP-1 drug or diet, and your chart shows none of the qualifying lows, Medicare does not cover a CGM. That includes the over-the-counter sensors the FDA cleared in 2024. Medicare does still cover a standard fingerstick meter, strips and lancets.

There are two honest ways forward. If you start insulin, or your practitioner documents qualifying lows, you meet the rule from that point. If neither applies, some people pay cash for an over-the-counter sensor to learn how meals and exercise affect their numbers. That is a personal choice, not a coverage question, and it is worth discussing with your doctor before you spend the money.

## Arriving in north Peoria with a CGM from another plan

This is the version of the question I see most from Vistancia and the rest of 85383, and from people who just moved in from the Midwest or California. Their sensors came through an employer plan or a previous state's Advantage plan, and the first Medicare order stalls.

Medicare has no carry-over rule for a CGM, so it treats your first Medicare order as a new one. What makes that easy:

1. **Get your records before you lose touch** with the old practice: the diabetes diagnosis, your insulin prescription or the documented lows, and the most recent visit note.
2. **See your new Arizona doctor soon**, whether that is a primary care office along Lake Pleasant Parkway or an endocrinologist toward Arrowhead or Sun City. That visit is the one inside the six-month window, and the order comes from it.
3. **Choose the supplier after the order is written.** On Original Medicare, ask whether it is enrolled with Medicare, accepts assignment, and ships to Arizona. On an Advantage plan, ask the plan which supplier or pharmacy it uses for your model.

If the move is also changing your plan, [what moving to Arizona does to your Medicare](/blog/do-i-have-to-change-my-medicare-if-i-move-to-arizona/) covers the enrollment side. For local detail on doctors and hospitals across both ends of the city, see [Medicare help in Peoria](/service-area/peoria-az/).

## What changes for CGMs in 2028

CMS has already finalized a change to how Original Medicare buys these devices. In the home health and equipment rule published December 2, 2025, it moved CGMs and insulin pumps to a **monthly rental that bundles the device with its sensors and supplies**. It also created a national **remote item delivery** competitive bidding program, so a smaller group of contract suppliers will mail CGMs nationwide. Contracts are scheduled to take effect in 2028.

Three points from the rule matter for patients:

- **If you already own your device, you can keep using it** until it needs replacing or you choose to switch to a rental.
- **Contract suppliers have to provide the brand your practitioner orders.** The program is not supposed to force a model change.
- **Nothing changes for 2027.** Today's rules, suppliers and cost sharing stay in place through next year.

If your supplier changes in 2028, expect a letter. The coverage rules above, including the insulin-or-lows test and the six-month visit, stay the same.

## What to do before your next sensor order

- **Find your last diabetes visit date** and make sure the next one falls inside six months.
- **Ask your supplier** whether it is Medicare-enrolled and accepts assignment, and whether it has every note it needs on file.
- **If you are on an Advantage plan, or choosing one by December 7**, check the plan's preferred CGM brand and supplier for 2027 against the model you use now.
- **If you do not take insulin**, ask your practitioner whether your chart documents any qualifying lows before you assume you cannot get one.
