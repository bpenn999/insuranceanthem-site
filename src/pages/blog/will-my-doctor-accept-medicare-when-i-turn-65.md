**Almost certainly yes. Medicare.gov says most doctors, providers and suppliers accept assignment, and KFF's analysis of CMS data found that only about 1 percent of non-pediatric physicians have formally opted out of Medicare.** In Arizona, CMS's own opt-out list shows 1,283 providers as of its October 2026 update, and 117 of them are primary-care physicians. That is a small number for a state this size, but it is concentrated in the same places a lot of affluent retirees see their doctors. The question to ask is not whether your doctor "takes Medicare". Ask which of three kinds of Medicare doctor they are, and ask before your coverage starts.

This matters at 65 in a way it never did before. Until now your doctor has been checked against your employer plan. Medicare is a separate arrangement with its own rules, and the office's answer about your current insurance tells you nothing about this one. I meet people in north Peoria every year who assumed the two went together.

## The three kinds of Medicare doctor

Every doctor and practitioner in the country is in one of three positions with Medicare. The position decides what you pay, and whether your supplement pays anything at all.

The first group is most doctors. The second is uncommon and costs little with the right supplement; see [Plan G vs Plan N](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/) for how the excess charge plays out. The third group is the one that surprises people.

## What "opted out" actually means

A doctor who opts out has filed an affidavit with Medicare saying they will see Medicare patients only under a **private contract**. Federal rules spell out what the contract must say. Among other things, you accept full responsibility for the bill, you agree not to send a claim to Medicare, Medicare's limits on charges do not apply, and **Medigap plans do not pay** for what Medicare does not pay for (42 CFR 405.415). You have to sign it before you are treated, and it has to be renewed for each opt-out period.

Opt-out runs in two-year periods that renew on their own unless the doctor cancels. A doctor already in Medicare can opt out at the start of any calendar quarter (42 CFR 405.410). So a doctor who was in Medicare when you checked last spring may not be now. Check again in the three months before your coverage starts.

Two exceptions are worth knowing:

- **Emergencies and urgent care.** If an opted-out doctor treats you in an emergency or for urgent care and you have *not* signed a private contract with them, they must bill Medicare and can collect no more than the limiting charge (42 CFR 405.440).
- **What they order.** CMS's benefit policy manual says Medicare can pay for medically necessary services an opted-out physician orders, such as lab work, imaging or a hospital admission, as long as the lab, imaging center or hospital has not opted out itself (Chapter 15, §40.32). So the bloodwork your concierge internist sends to an outside lab can still be a Medicare claim. The visit itself is not.

**Medicare Advantage does not get around any of this.** An Advantage plan cannot pay an opted-out doctor either, except in the same emergency and urgent-care situations (42 CFR 405.425(a); Chapter 15, §40.37). An Advantage plan also adds its own network question on top, which is separate. [How Medicare Advantage networks work](/learn/medicare-advantage-networks/) covers that one.

## Concierge practices: two different questions

People mix up the membership fee and the Medicare status, and they are separate.

Some concierge practices stay enrolled in Medicare. They bill Medicare for your visits and charge an annual membership for things Medicare does not cover, such as same-day access or longer appointments. With one of those, your Medicare and your supplement work normally and the membership is an extra.

Others opt out completely. With those, the membership and every visit are private, and nothing goes to Medicare or your Medigap plan. KFF Health News has reported on patients who learned this only after the first bill. One thing works in your favour in both cases: your Part B covers the hospital, the specialists, the imaging and the labs, so you are not choosing between your doctor and Medicare. You are deciding whether one relationship is worth paying for privately on top.

## What to ask your doctor's office before your coverage starts

Call in the three months before your Medicare starts, and ask these three questions, in this order:

1. **"Do you accept Medicare assignment?"** A yes means the doctor participates, and you are done. Ask it in those words. "Do you take Medicare?" gets a yes from non-participating and some opted-out offices too.
2. **"If not, have you opted out, or do you bill Medicare as non-participating?"** Non-participating with Plan G costs you almost nothing extra. Opted out means a private contract.
3. **"Do existing patients who turn 65 stay on?"** Some practices close to *new* Medicare patients but keep the ones they already have. That is different from opting out, and it is worth knowing before you switch anything.

Then check it yourself. Medicare's [Care Compare](https://www.medicare.gov/care-compare/) shows whether a clinician accepts assignment, and CMS's opt-out dataset is searchable by name and NPI. If you are weighing an Advantage plan, the network question comes after this one. A doctor has to be in Medicare before an Advantage plan can pay them.

## Where this fits in the turning-65 sequence

The doctor check belongs early, before you choose between Medigap and Medicare Advantage, because the answer can decide that choice. A household whose doctors all accept assignment has the full range of options. A household with one opted-out concierge doctor they will keep regardless is often better served by Original Medicare, a supplement and a separate budget line for that doctor than by an Advantage plan that will not pay the doctor either.

Your Medigap open enrollment period, the six months that start with your Part B, is the one time in Arizona an insurer must sell you a supplement without health questions, since Arizona has no birthday rule.
