**Your ASRS non-Medicare plan ends when you become eligible for Medicare, and it does not switch over by itself.** To keep coverage through the Arizona State Retirement System you have to enroll in Medicare Part A and Part B, then apply online for one of the two ASRS Medicare plans before your Medicare start date. For 2027 both plans come from UnitedHealthcare: a Group Medicare Advantage HMO for Arizona residents at $131.83 a month per person, and a Group Medicare Advantage PPO that works nationwide at $234.30. The ASRS premium benefit comes off those prices. Those are the figures in the [ASRS 2027 Health Insurance Enrollment Guide](https://www.azasrs.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/2026-09/OE-Guide-2027-Web.pdf), and everything below comes from that guide unless I say otherwise.

The part that trips people up is in one sentence of the guide. If you are on an ASRS non-Medicare plan and don't switch to a Medicare plan when you become eligible, "your non-Medicare medical coverage will be terminated, and you will not be able to enroll in an ASRS Medicare medical plan until the next open enrollment period." ASRS open enrollment runs November 1 to 30, for coverage starting January 1. Miss your birthday month and you could wait most of a year.

## Who this applies to

ASRS covers most Arizona public employees: teachers and staff in public school districts, the universities and community colleges, the state, and most cities and counties. In north Peoria that includes many of the retired teachers from Peoria Unified and Deer Valley Unified I meet around Vistancia and Westbrook Village. Police and firefighters are usually in PSPRS instead. Their retirees can join the same ASRS health plans, but the guide sends them to the PSPRS benefits office for enrollment forms. Someone who retired under both systems should enroll through PSPRS, because that is how the guide says to get the full premium benefit.

If you are still working at 65 and covered through your own or your spouse's current job, this is not your turn yet. ASRS notes that people in that situation have likely delayed Part B, and the ASRS changes come when the job coverage ends. Our post on [Medicare at 65 while still working](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/) covers that order.

There are two details in the fine print. First, an application completed online or signed on the first of the month is not processed until the first of the following month, so don't leave it until April 1. Second, the online application asks for your Medicare number and your Part A and Part B effective dates. You can't finish it until Social Security has processed your Medicare enrollment, which is why ASRS tells you to start with Medicare three months out.

If your birthday falls on the first of the month, everything moves a month earlier. Medicare counts you as eligible from the first of the month before, as the ASRS guide also notes. Our post on [birthdays on the 1st](/blog/medicare-birthday-on-the-first-of-the-month/) works through the dates. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) will lay out your own months.

Drug costs are capped separately. Once you and others on your behalf have paid {{partD.cap}} toward covered Part D drugs in {{figYear}}, the plan pays the full cost of those drugs for the rest of the year. That is the federal cap, and the ASRS plans follow it.

**What is new for 2027 is mostly paperwork.** Starting January 1, 2027, ASRS splits its Medicare coverage into two linked plans: the Group Medicare Advantage plan for medical care and a separate Group Medicare Part D plan for prescriptions. ASRS calls it an administrative change and says benefits and monthly costs stay the same as 2026. You get two ID cards, mailed separately. The medical card keeps your 2026 member ID and the drug card has a new one. If you are turning 65 in 2027, expect two envelopes, and take both cards to your first pharmacy visit.

Even with the smaller benefit, most people's bill falls a long way at 65. The 2027 non-Medicare plans cost $826 a month (Choice EPO Basic) or $1,512 (Choice EPO Select) for a single retiree before the benefit. You do pick up the Part B premium, {{partB.premium}} a month in {{figYear}}, or more if your income puts you in an [IRMAA bracket](/blog/which-year-income-irmaa-when-i-turn-65/). That goes to Medicare, normally out of your Social Security, not to ASRS.

ASRS deducts the net premium from your pension each month. If the pension doesn't cover it, UnitedHealthcare bills you directly, and the guide says an unpaid direct bill ends your coverage until the next open enrollment.

## If your spouse is younger than you

Many households have one spouse turn 65 while the other is still on an ASRS non-Medicare plan. ASRS handles that with combination plans. One person on the Medicare HMO and one on Choice EPO Basic is $957.83 a month in 2027, and the premium benefit for that combination is up to $215 at 10 or more years. The HMO side of a combination plan is only available to Arizona residents. The PPO combinations work nationwide. When the younger spouse reaches 65, their own Medicare eligibility is the qualifying event, and the same deadline applies to them.

## Mayo Clinic, snowbirds and the HMO-or-PPO question

ASRS's Medicare page contains a detail that matters a great deal in the Valley. Mayo Clinic Arizona generally does not take Medicare Advantage, as our [Mayo Clinic post](/blog/does-mayo-clinic-arizona-accept-medicare-advantage/) explains. But ASRS says there is an arrangement between UnitedHealthcare and Mayo Clinic Arizona to accept members of the **ASRS and PSPRS Group Medicare Advantage PPO**. The HMO is not mentioned. ASRS adds that scheduling may depend on capacity.

So the choice usually comes down to this:

- **HMO:** the lower premium, Arizona only, a primary care doctor and referrals. It suits someone who lives here year-round and is happy with a UnitedHealthcare network doctor near home.
- **PPO:** about $102 a month more, no deductibles, any provider who accepts the plan nationwide, and the Mayo arrangement. It suits a part-year resident with a summer place out of state, or anyone who wants Mayo available.

Both plans cover emergency and urgent care abroad at the same copays as at home, but you pay the foreign bill up front and send the itemized receipt to UnitedHealthcare for reimbursement.

## Before you leave the ASRS plan

Some retirees look at the individual Medicare market at 65 instead: a Medicare Supplement with a drug plan, or an Advantage plan of their own. That is a legitimate choice. In Maricopa County there are dozens of individual Advantage plans, and your [Medigap open enrollment](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/) at 65 is the one time you can buy any Supplement without health questions. But be clear about how the ASRS rules work before you go:

1. **Joining another Medicare Advantage or Part D plan automatically takes you out of the ASRS plan.** The enrollment form says so in plain terms, because Medicare allows only one Advantage or drug plan at a time.
2. **Getting back in is limited.** The guide says voluntarily dropping coverage is not a qualifying life event. After you leave, the next door back is the November open enrollment, for a January start.
3. **The premium benefit is tied to ASRS coverage.** The guide describes it as paid to the insurer for ASRS health insurance, or to a former ASRS employer for that employer's non-subsidized plan. Don't assume it follows you to a plan you buy yourself. Ask ASRS first.
4. **Keep drug coverage without a gap.** ASRS warns that going 63 days or more without creditable drug coverage can bring a [Part D late enrollment penalty](/learn/late-enrollment-penalties/).

For someone with 10 or more years of service, the ASRS HMO nets $31.83 a month with a $4,250 cap. Any individual alternative has to beat that on your own doctors and prescriptions, not just on its premium.
