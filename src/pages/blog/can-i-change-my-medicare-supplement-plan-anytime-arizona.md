**Yes. You can apply to change your Medicare Supplement in any month of the year, and the new policy can start on the first of whichever month you pick. What Arizona does not give you is any guarantee the new insurer will say yes. Outside your one-time six-month Medigap open enrollment window, or a specific guaranteed issue right, an Arizona insurer can ask health questions, charge more for your answers, or decline you.** There is no birthday rule here and no annual window. The calendar does not decide a supplement switch in this state. Your health does.

I am writing this in the first week of October because this is the week the question arrives in volume. Rate renewal letters for next year have landed in Vistancia and Trilogy, and the Annual Notice of Change for Advantage plans has landed everywhere, and the two get read together. People in north Peoria who have had the same Plan G for ten years call to ask whether the open enrollment that starts October 15 is their chance to move it. It is not, and it is also not needed, because a supplement can be moved any time. The hard part is somewhere else entirely.

## Two questions that sound like one

"Can I change my Medicare Supplement plan?" is really two questions, and the answer to each is different.

**Can I apply?** Always. There is no enrollment period for Medicare Supplement plans. You can send an application to any company licensed in Arizona in any month, ask for any plan letter it sells, and choose a start date on the first of a coming month. Nothing on the federal or Arizona side stops you.

**Does the company have to accept me?** Only sometimes. Medicare.gov's own wording is that in most cases you will not have a right under federal law to switch Medigap policies unless you are within your six-month Medigap open enrollment period or are eligible under a specific guaranteed issue right. Outside those, there is no federal guarantee an insurer will sell you a policy, and if it does, it may cost more because of past or present health problems.

So the honest short answer is: you can apply any time, and in Arizona the answer you get back depends on what the application says about your health.

## What Arizona actually allows

A few states have added their own protections on top of the federal minimum, and that is where most of the confusion in this market comes from. The best known is the birthday rule, a window each year around your birthday when you can move to a plan with equal or lesser benefits without health questions. California started it. Nevada and Idaho, which send a lot of people to north Peoria, have versions of it. Arizona does not.

I am being specific about this because at least one page that ranks for the question tells readers Arizona has a thirty-day birthday window. It cites no statute, no bill and no Department of Insurance bulletin, because there is none. If you apply in your birthday month expecting no health questions, you will get health questions. Treat any claim of an Arizona birthday rule as wrong until the Arizona Department of Insurance and Financial Institutions says otherwise, and it has not.

One Arizona rule does exist and is worth knowing, because it explains the renewal letter. Arizona law lets an insurer file Medicare Supplement rates that include an early enrollment discount, a lower premium for people who buy at 65 that shrinks over the following years, and the insurer has to disclose that at the time you apply. A policy bought at 65 can therefore climb faster than the company's general rate increase alone, and that is often what people are reacting to when they call about switching.

## The October enrollment period does nothing for a supplement

This is the point to put on the refrigerator. The Annual Enrollment Period from October 15 to December 7 is for [Medicare Advantage](/medicare-advantage/) and [Part D](/part-d/) drug plans. Those plans redraw their networks, copays and drug lists each year, send an [Annual Notice of Change](/blog/medicare-annual-notice-of-change-letter-2027/) in September, and give you the fall to react.

A Medicare Supplement does none of that. Plan G in {{nextYear}} covers what Plan G covers in {{year}}. The only things that move are the Part B deductible, which Medicare announces in November and which Plan G leaves to you, and the premium, which the company sets on its own schedule. A supplement application sent in November is underwritten exactly like one sent in May. There is no deadline and no advantage to the season.

Where the fall matters for a supplement household is the drug plan sitting next to it. If your Part D plan is raising its premium or dropping a medication, that change has to be made between October 15 and December 7. The supplement decision runs on its own clock, and the two should not be confused into one December scramble. The [enrollment periods explainer](/learn/medicare-enrollment-periods/) lays out every window side by side.

## What a switch looks like when it is underwritten

Most Peoria switches are the third row of the table, and most of them succeed, because most of the people asking are in reasonable health and simply want a lower premium for the same letter. Here is how to do it without creating a gap.

**1. Apply before you cancel anything.** A Medicare Supplement is guaranteed renewable. The company cannot drop you for health or claims as long as the premium is paid. A declined application somewhere else does not touch it. So the order is fixed: apply, get the approval in writing, set the new policy's start date, and only then cancel the old one effective the day before. Cancel first and get declined and you are standing on Original Medicare alone with no protected way back.

**2. Expect the health application.** Questions about diagnoses and treatment in recent years, hospital stays, procedures already scheduled, height and weight, tobacco use, and the medications you take. The drug list tells an underwriter a great deal. Some companies decline outright for certain conditions, some charge a higher rate class, and some accept a condition one company would refuse, which is why one declined application is not the end of the question.

**3. Use the 30-day free look.** Medicare.gov is explicit. When the new policy arrives, you have 30 days to decide whether to keep it, and you will need to pay both premiums for the month you have both. That one month of double premium is the price of never being uncovered, and it is worth paying.

**4. Know the pre-existing condition rule.** If you have held your current supplement for six months or more, the new policy cannot impose a waiting period on conditions it covers. If you have held it for less than six months, the new company may make you wait for coverage of a pre-existing condition. For almost everyone reading this, the first case applies.

**5. Pick the letter you want at eighty.** Moving from Plan F to Plan G is the switch I see most, because F closed to people new to Medicare in 2020 and a closed pool ages and gets more expensive. Moving from G to N trims the premium further in exchange for small office and emergency copays and the Part B excess charge, which Arizona allows. I compared those two in [Plan G vs Plan N in Arizona](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/), and the [Plan G vs Plan N calculator](/tools/plan-g-vs-plan-n/) does the arithmetic with your own premiums. Whatever you choose, remember you are choosing under underwriting. Coming back up from N to G later means passing it again, older.

The [Medicare Supplement page](/medicare-supplement/) explains what Arizona underwriting actually asks and how the rate classes work.

## The Peoria version of this question

Peoria runs nearly thirty miles north to south, and the switching question shows up differently at each end.

**North Peoria, the 85383 corridor, Vistancia, Trilogy and Blackstone.** A large share of these households arrived in the last few years, and a noticeable number came from a birthday-rule state. If you bought your supplement in Nevada, California or Idaho, you had an annual chance to change it without health questions, and your agent back there may have told you to wait for your birthday. That right stayed at the state line. The policy itself travels fine, because Original Medicare has no network and a supplement pays wherever Medicare pays, but the window to change it did not come with you. If a lower premium is available here for the same letter, the application is an Arizona application, underwritten under Arizona rules.

The same end of the city holds the seasonal residents, and for them timing is practical rather than legal. Apply while you are here for the winter, when the paperwork, the phone interview and the new card are all in one place. A new policy can start on the first of any month, so there is no reason to let a summer away decide the date.

**The 85345 core and Westbrook Village.** Households here have often had Plan F, or the Plan G that replaced it, for fifteen or twenty years, and the letter that prompts the call is a rate renewal. The honest answer depends almost entirely on health. Someone healthy at seventy-eight is routinely approved for the same letter at a lower premium with a different company. Someone with a recent cardiac history or a scheduled joint replacement usually is not, and for them the right move is to keep the policy they have, because it cannot be taken away. Either way, the answer comes from quotes and a candid look at the health questions, not from a date on the calendar.

Across both ends, [Peoria](/service-area/peoria-az/) sits in Maricopa County, and the supplement premium you are quoted depends on your ZIP, your age, your tobacco status and the company's rate history here. Comparing one neighbor's premium to another's inside the same community association, which happens constantly, mostly tells you about the two companies and the two ages, not about the plan.

## The short version

- **You can apply to change a Medicare Supplement in any month.** There is no enrollment period for it, and October 15 to December 7 is for Advantage and Part D, not for a supplement.
- **Arizona has no birthday rule and no annual window.** Fifteen states do as of July 2026. A page that says Arizona has a thirty-day window is wrong.
- **Without health questions, there are two routes:** your one-time six-month open enrollment window at 65, or a federal guaranteed issue right with a 63-day clock.
- **Every other switch is underwritten.** Apply first, get approved in writing, use the 30-day free look, and cancel the old policy last.
- **A supplement cannot be cancelled on you for health.** If the switch is declined, you keep what you have.
