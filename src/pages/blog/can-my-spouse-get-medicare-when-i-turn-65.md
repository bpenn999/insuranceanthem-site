**No. Medicare is individual coverage, and your enrollment does nothing for a spouse who is under 65.** There is no family plan, no dependent tier and no spousal premium. Your spouse becomes eligible on their own 65th birthday, or earlier only through their own disability. The one thing your record does for them is premium-free Part A when they get there, if they never earned the quarters themselves.

I hear this question in the same breath as "when do I sign up," and it is more common in north Peoria than almost anywhere I work. The couples arriving in Vistancia and Trilogy from a career in another state are often a few years apart in age, and the one turning 65 is usually the one whose job carried the family coverage. So the real question underneath is not whether Medicare covers your spouse. It is who does, for how long, and what that decision does to your own Part B timing, your tax return and the health savings account you have been funding for a decade. That is a planning problem, and it has a good answer if you start it before your birthday month rather than after.

## Why Medicare does not cover your spouse

Medicare.gov describes eligibility in one line: you are first eligible to sign up for Part A and Part B starting three months before you turn 65 and ending three months after the month you turn 65. The word in that sentence is *you*. Every person enrolls on their own record, gets their own Medicare number, pays their own Part B premium and picks their own plan. Marriage does not move anyone's eligibility date.

The only ways under 65 are the same for a spouse as for anyone else: 24 months of Social Security Disability Insurance, a diagnosis of ALS, or permanent kidney failure requiring dialysis or a transplant. None of those are planning tools.

If you were born on the first of a month, your own dates shift a month earlier, and the arithmetic in [Do you automatically get Medicare when you turn 65?](/blog/do-you-automatically-get-medicare-when-you-turn-65/) applies to you, not to your spouse. Their window opens around their own birthday, years from now, and the [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) will build it for either of you.

## What your work record does do for a spouse

Here marriage matters, and it is worth understanding because it cuts both ways in a two-career household.

Premium-free Part A requires about ten years, 40 quarters, of Medicare-covered work. Medicare.gov puts the test as whether "they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes long enough while working." A spouse who stayed home, worked overseas, or spent a career in a job outside the Social Security system can still get Part A at no premium at 65 on the other spouse's record, provided three things are true:

- **The working spouse is at least 62.** Social Security's operations manual states that Part A eligibility for the 65-or-over spouse of a worker begins with the month the insured worker turns 62, and that neither of you has to be drawing a monthly benefit for this to apply.
- **The working spouse has the 40 quarters.**
- **You have been married at least a year** when the application goes in. Divorced spouses can qualify on a former spouse's record after a marriage of ten years or more, and surviving spouses have their own shorter rule.

Without that, the {{year}} Part A premium is {{partA.premiumIfUninsured}} a month with fewer than 30 quarters, and 45 percent less with 30 to 39, which is real money over a retirement. The [Part A premium checker](/tools/part-a-premium/) runs the quarters test for either spouse.

Part B is different. Everyone pays the Part B premium, work history or not, and here is the detail higher-income couples in 85383 should plan around: **IRMAA is charged per person, but it is set by your joint return.** While only one of you is on Medicare, one surcharge is owed. When the second spouse enrolls, the same tax return produces a second one. The [IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) shows where a couple lands, and [IRMAA explained](/learn/irmaa-explained/) covers the two-year lookback that makes a final salary year follow you into retirement.

Here is where the decision actually lives. Every household I meet in north Peoria lands in one of these, and the right one depends on who is still working and where.

**You keep working.** This is the common case in Vistancia, where a lot of the recent arrivals still consult or run a business from home. You can enroll in Part A, or both parts, and remain an active employee, and your spouse stays on the group plan exactly as before. If the employer has 20 or more employees, you can also delay Part B without a penalty until the job ends. If it is your own small company with fewer than 20, take Part B at 65, because the group plan pays second for you and often pays very little. I went through that test, and the owner-operator trap in the older part of Peoria, in [Do I need to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I'm still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/)

**Your spouse keeps working.** The reverse case, and often the cleanest. If the younger spouse has a group plan through a current job, they stay on it, and you can be covered on it too. Medicare.gov's rule reads "you (or your spouse)," so coverage from a working spouse's employer of 20 or more lets you delay Part B just as your own job would. When their employment ends, both of your clocks start on the same day, and that is a busier eight months than anyone expects.

**Retiree coverage.** Some employers, and most public pensions, carry a spouse on retiree medical. Read the plan document for two things: whether the spouse keeps coverage until their own 65, and whether the plan requires you to enroll in Part B to stay on it. Retiree coverage never lets you delay Part B, so for you the answer is to enroll on time regardless.

**COBRA, and the 36-month rule.** When there is no retiree coverage, COBRA is the bridge, and the length of the bridge depends on a date most people never think about. The ordinary COBRA period after a retirement is 18 months. But the federal regulation sets the spouse's maximum at the *later* of 18 months from your termination or **36 months from the date you became entitled to Medicare**. Retire within 18 months after your Part A begins and your spouse's COBRA runs from your Medicare start date, not your last day of work.

Put real dates on it. You turn 65 in March 2027 and your Part A starts March 1. You retire at the end of December 2027, nine months later. Your spouse's COBRA can run through February 2030, 36 months from March 2027, instead of ending in June 2029 under the plain 18-month rule. That is eight more months of the plan you know, with the same doctors, while your spouse works toward their own birthday. Retire in October 2028 instead, 19 months after Medicare started, and the extension is gone.

Two cautions. COBRA is the full premium plus an administrative charge, so it is a bridge, not a bargain. And it does nothing for *you*. COBRA is not current-employment coverage, so it does not let you delay Part B, and Medicare.gov is blunt that waiting until COBRA ends to sign up is the wrong move. [Can I keep COBRA instead of Medicare at 65?](/blog/can-i-keep-cobra-instead-of-medicare-at-65/) covers that side of the house.

If none of these four apply, a spouse buys coverage as an individual until 65, and that is a different market from Medicare and not one I work in. A broker who does is the person to ask, and the sooner the better, since the group coverage ends on a date and not on a feeling.

## The HSA move most couples miss

If the younger spouse is still working and the household has a family high-deductible plan, there is a planning point here worth real money in a tax bracket that makes it matter.

The month your Medicare starts, your own HSA contribution limit drops to zero. IRS Publication 969 is plain about that. What it also says is that each spouse who is an eligible individual opens a separate HSA, and the married-couple rules only apply when both spouses are eligible. So when you enroll in Medicare and your under-65 spouse keeps the family HDHP through their own job, your spouse can keep funding **their own** HSA, up to the family limit, which is $8,750 for 2026, plus a $1,000 catch-up if they are 55 or older. The money is theirs, and it can still pay your Medicare premiums and out-of-pocket costs tax-free once you are 65, because an HSA can reimburse a spouse's qualified medical expenses.

The trap is on your side. Enroll in Part A after 65 and it can be backdated up to six months, which turns contributions you already made into excess contributions. That look-back, and how to stop in time, is in [Can I contribute to an HSA while on Medicare?](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) Read it before you file anything with Social Security.

## When your spouse's turn comes

A few years from now the younger spouse gets their own seven-month window, their own Part B start date, and their own **six-month Medigap open enrollment period** that begins the month they are 65 and enrolled in Part B. Inside it, any Medicare Supplement sold in Arizona must take them without health questions. After it, Arizona carriers can underwrite, and Arizona has no birthday rule to give anyone a yearly do-over. It happens once per person, and the fact that you used yours well does not help your spouse use theirs.

Two local notes for a Peoria couple planning that far ahead. First, the plans on offer are set by county and pulled by ZIP, and ZIPs on the Peoria, Glendale and Sun City seam are stitched tightly enough that neighbors a mile apart see different line-ups. Price your own ZIP both times. Second, it is entirely normal for two spouses in the same house to end up on different structures: one on a Supplement because they winter in another state, the other on a Medicare Advantage plan because every doctor they see is in Arrowhead or Sun City and inside the network. Same mailbox, different answer, and that is fine. ## What to do this month if you turn 65 in early 2027 and your spouse is younger

1. **Find out who carries the family coverage after your birthday month.** If it is your current job, confirm in writing that dependents stay on the plan when an employee enrolls in Medicare, and ask for the employee count. If it is your spouse's job, do the same there.
2. **Write down your Medicare start date before you pick a retirement date.** If retirement is coming within a year or two, the 36-month COBRA rule rewards leaving within 18 months of the day Part A begins. That is a conversation with your benefits office and your spouse, not a Medicare form, but the Medicare date drives it.
3. **Run the quarters test for both of you.** If either spouse is short of 40, the other spouse's record at 62 or older is the fix, and the [Part A premium checker](/tools/part-a-premium/) shows which rule applies.
4. **Decide your own Part B timing on the current-employment rule, not on your spouse's age.** Group coverage from a current job of 20 or more, yours or theirs, lets you wait. Retiree coverage and COBRA do not.
5. **Stop your HSA contributions in time, and keep your spouse's going.** Six months before Part A if you are enrolling late, the month before if you are enrolling at 65.
6. **Put the second IRMAA on the household budget.** Same joint return, two surcharges once you are both enrolled. If a Roth conversion is planned, the year before either enrollment is the one to discuss with your tax advisor, and [How do I appeal IRMAA after retirement?](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/) covers the appeal when income drops.
