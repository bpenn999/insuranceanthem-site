**Yes, you can skip Medicare Part B at 65. It is voluntary, and nobody fines you for turning it down. But if you are retired and have no group coverage from a current job, skipping it means paying 10% more of the standard Part B premium for each full 12 months you were eligible and went without it, for as long as you have Part B, and you can only sign up again from January 1 to March 31.** Meanwhile, nothing pays your doctor, outpatient or emergency-room bills, and you cannot buy a Medicare Supplement or join a Medicare Advantage plan, because both require Part B.

The question usually comes from someone healthy. A man in Vistancia who still hikes the trails above Lake Pleasant three mornings a week, sees a doctor once a year and has plenty saved, looks at a {{figYear}} Part B premium of {{partB.premium}} a month, or a good deal more with IRMAA, and asks whether he could just pay his own bills for a few years. It is a fair question, and one worth answering with the actual trade-offs.

## What Part B pays for, and what you would pay instead

Part A covers inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing after a qualifying admission, hospice and some home health. Almost everything else Medicare covers comes from Part B:

- Doctor visits, specialists and annual wellness visits
- Emergency-room visits where you are not formally admitted
- Outpatient surgery, imaging such as CT and MRI scans, and lab work
- Chemotherapy and other drugs given in a clinic or infusion center
- Ambulance rides, durable medical equipment and outpatient physical therapy
- Hospital **observation stays**, which feel like an admission but are billed as outpatient care ([how observation status works](/blog/does-medicare-cover-hospital-observation-stay/))

That last item is the one that surprises people. Two nights in a hospital bed under observation in Sun City or Arrowhead is a Part B claim, not a Part A one. Without Part B you are a self-pay patient for it.

Paying cash is not the same as paying Medicare's price, either. The Medicare-approved amounts, and the rule that keeps a doctor who does not take assignment within 15% of them, are protections that come with Part B coverage. Without it you are billed whatever the practice or the hospital outpatient department charges, and Original Medicare's own design already has no yearly out-of-pocket cap even with Part B. Without it, one cancer diagnosis, one heart procedure or one bad fall on a trail can cost more than a decade of premiums.

## The doors that close when you skip Part B

This is the part people do not see coming, because it has nothing to do with the premium.

**No Medicare Supplement.** A Medigap policy pays the gaps in Original Medicare, and most of those gaps are Part B coinsurance. Federal rules require both Part A and Part B before an insurer can sell you one.

**No Medicare Advantage.** Every Advantage plan requires Part A and Part B. According to CMS's {{figYear}} plan landscape files, Maricopa County has 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage from 13 companies. A Peoria retiree without Part B can join none of them.

**Your Medigap window waits.** The six-month Medigap open enrollment, when an insurer cannot price a policy on your health, starts the first month you are both 65 and enrolled in Part B. Skipping Part B does not use that window up, but it does mean you have no supplement until you return. Arizona has no birthday rule offering a second chance later, so that window matters more here than in some states ([when the window runs](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/)).

What stays open: a standalone **Part D drug plan**. Under [42 CFR §423.30](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/423.30) you qualify with Part A or Part B, so Part A plus a drug plan is a legal combination. Anyone weighing this should at least keep drug coverage, because Part D has a separate penalty of its own.

## What it costs to come back later

Two things happen when a retiree who declined Part B changes their mind.

**You wait for the calendar.** Without a Special Enrollment Period, the only way in is the General Enrollment Period, January 1 through March 31 each year. Part B then starts the first of the following month. Decide you want it in August and you are uninsured for outpatient care until at least February.

**You may pay a surcharge for life.** The penalty in [42 CFR §408.22](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/408.22) is 10% of the standard premium for each full 12 months you could have had Part B and did not. Take someone whose 65th birthday falls in March 2027. Their first window closes at the end of June 2027. If they sign up in January 2030, the count runs from July 2027 through January 2030, 31 months, which is two full years. Their Part B premium carries a 20% surcharge from then on. Sign up in January 2028 instead and the gap is seven months, so there is no surcharge at all, but there were seven months with no Part B coverage.

If you think the window has already closed on you, [what to do if you missed it](/blog/what-happens-if-i-miss-my-medicare-enrollment-at-65/) covers the General Enrollment Period in more detail.

## "My IRMAA is high, so I'll skip Part B": run the numbers first

This is a version of the question I hear from higher-income households in north Peoria, and it deserves a straight answer. In {{figYear}}, a single filer whose income reached {{irmaa.singleStart}} two years earlier pays more than the standard premium, and at the top bracket Part B alone is {{irmaa.t5.partB}} a month.

Three facts change the math:

1. **The penalty is figured on the standard premium**, not your income-adjusted one. Skipping Part B does not dodge anything permanently; IRMAA, if you still owe it later, is added on top of the surcharged amount.
2. **IRMAA looks back two years and can be revised.** If your income was high because you were still working, retiring is a life-changing event, and Form SSA-44 asks Social Security to use your current, lower income instead. [How to appeal IRMAA after retirement](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/) walks through it.
3. **The gap is the real cost.** A wealthy household can absorb a few years of premiums. What it cannot predict is the year a diagnosis arrives, and nothing buys Part B coverage back retroactively.

If income is the worry, the [IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) shows which bracket your return lands in, and a tax advisor is the right person for planning Roth conversions or the timing of a home sale around it.

## How to say no to Part B properly

If you are in the delay column above, or you have weighed all of this and still want to decline, do it in the right way so nothing is billed by mistake.

- **Enrolled automatically** because you already draw Social Security: your Medicare card arrives about three months before you turn 65 with instructions for declining Part B. Follow them and tell Social Security before the start date on the card.
- **Applying yourself**: on the Social Security application you can ask for Part A only. Premium-free Part A costs nothing to have, and you generally want it.
- **Part B already started**: file Form CMS-1763 with Social Security. Under [42 CFR §407.27](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/407.27) coverage ends on the last day of the month after the month you file. Social Security usually wants a short interview first, and that is a good moment to confirm your other coverage really counts.

Keep proof of the coverage you are relying on. If you later use a Special Enrollment Period, Social Security will ask your employer to confirm the dates.
