**Short-term care insurance is worth it for some people on Medicare, and the deciding factor is what you already carry on top of Medicare.** Original Medicare pays for skilled nursing or rehab after a qualifying hospital stay for up to 100 days: days 1 to 20 at no daily cost after the Part A deductible, days 21 to 100 at {{partA.snf}} a day, and nothing after that. It does not pay for custodial care, meaning help bathing, dressing or getting around, when that is the only care you need. A short-term care policy pays a set cash benefit for roughly a year or less, at home or in a facility, whether Medicare pays or not.

So the honest answer splits in two. If you have a Medicare Supplement like Plan G, the Medicare-covered part of a rehab stay is already paid in full, and the policy would only cover the custodial gap. If you are on a Medicare Advantage plan, you have daily copays up to your plan's out-of-pocket maximum *plus* that same custodial gap, and the case for the policy is stronger.

I am an Anthem-based advisor and much of this conversation happens in north Peoria, around Vistancia, Trilogy and the Lake Pleasant Parkway corridor, usually after a neighbor's hip or knee surgery. The rest of this page is how I walk someone through it.

## What a short-term care policy actually pays

Short-term care, sometimes called recovery care, is an indemnity policy. You pick a daily or monthly benefit amount and a benefit period when you buy it. When you qualify, usually because you need help with daily activities or are recovering from an illness or injury, the policy pays that amount in cash. It does not care what the bill says.

According to LTC News, the common designs look like this:

- **Benefit period:** a year or less, with 360 days the usual example. A few designs run longer.
- **Waiting period** (the insurer calls it the elimination period): 0, 20 or 100 days are the common choices. Home care often starts at day zero.
- **Where it pays:** at home, and in rehab centers, assisted living, memory care and nursing homes, depending on the policy.
- **Underwriting:** about ten health questions, sometimes a prescription check. It is sold to people well into their 80s.

That last point is the product's real niche. A traditional long-term care policy has full medical underwriting, and many insurers stop accepting applicants around age 79. Short-term care is often what is still available when the bigger policy is not.

It is not a Medicare Supplement, it is not a substitute for long-term care insurance, and it is not "short-term health insurance," which is an unrelated product sold to people under 65.

## Where Medicare stops: the three gaps

Every short-term care conversation is about the space between what Medicare pays and what a recovery actually costs. There are three gaps worth knowing.

**Gap one: days 21 to 100 in a skilled nursing facility.** Medicare pays only if you were admitted to the hospital as an inpatient for at least three days in a row, and you need daily skilled nursing or therapy. Nights spent under observation do not count toward the three. If you qualify, you pay {{partA.snf}} a day from day 21. A full 80 days at that rate is {{partA.snfTotal}}.

**Gap two: anything after day 100, or the day Medicare decides you no longer need skilled care.** Medicare's coverage ends when your care stops being skilled, which can happen long before day 100. From that day, a facility bills you directly.

**Gap three: help at home that is not skilled.** Medicare's home health benefit covers part-time skilled nursing and therapy for someone who is homebound. Medicare.gov describes "part-time or intermittent" as up to 8 hours a day combined, and a maximum of 28 hours a week. It does not pay for 24-hour care, and it does not pay for a caregiver to help with bathing, meals or getting around when that is the only help you need. At the Arizona median of {{care.az.homeCareHourly}} an hour, according to the {{care.surveyYear}} CareScout survey, a few weeks of daytime help after a fall adds up quickly.

For an Advantage member, the ceiling on that middle row is the plan's in-network maximum out-of-pocket. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, the general Medicare Advantage plans sold in Maricopa County carry in-network maximums from **$2,000 to $8,900** for 2027, up from a top of $7,900 in 2026. A recovery in the second half of the year, on a plan near the top of that range, can use most of it. A short-term care benefit paid in cash covers those copays without touching savings.

For a Plan G household, the same recovery costs only the Part B deductible of {{partB.deductible}} if it has not been met yet. If a policy is worth buying there, it is for the custodial help, not for the nursing facility.

## When it tends to make sense, and when it does not

From what I see in practice, short-term care earns its premium in a handful of situations:

- **You are on Medicare Advantage** and a high out-of-pocket year would hurt.
- **You are 79 or older** and were declined for, or priced out of, a traditional long-term care policy, but still want something.
- **You have a long-term care policy with a 90-day waiting period.** A short-term care policy with no waiting period can cover those first three months.
- **One spouse would be the caregiver**, and that is not realistic. A Trilogy household where one partner has had back surgery cannot also be the other's hands after a knee replacement.

It tends not to make sense when:

- **You have Plan G and substantial savings.** A few weeks of home help at Arizona prices may cost less than several years of premiums.
- **Your real worry is years of care**, for example dementia. A year of benefits will not solve that. That is a long-term care question, and the [long-term care piece](/blog/is-long-term-care-insurance-worth-it-arizona/) on this site is the place to start.
- **The premium would strain your monthly budget.** A policy you let lapse at 82 has paid for nothing.

## The north Peoria version

Two local details make this question come up more often north of the Loop 303.

The first is distance. A skilled nursing stay for someone in 85383 is often a drive to the Arrowhead or Sun City area, and a spouse making that drive daily for three weeks is part of the cost of a recovery even if no bill shows it. Cash from a policy can pay for a ride service or a few hours of help at home so the visiting is manageable.

The second is how people moved here. Many households in Vistancia and Blackstone arrived from out of state in their late 60s and chose a Medicare Advantage plan because it looked like the employer coverage they were used to. If that describes you, look up your plan's skilled nursing copays and its maximum out-of-pocket for 2027 in the Evidence of Coverage. If those two numbers would make you uncomfortable, that is a reason to consider either a short-term care policy or a change of plan. The [Peoria service-area page](/service-area/peoria-az/) has more on the local plan picture.

## What to check before you buy

If you decide to look at a policy, compare these five things side by side, not just the premium:

1. **The daily or monthly benefit** against what care actually costs here. The [cost of care tool](/tools/cost-of-care/) uses Arizona medians.
2. **The waiting period**, and whether it is different for home care and facility care.
3. **The benefit period**, and whether it renews after you recover.
4. **What triggers a claim.** Some policies pay after a hospital stay, others when you need help with a set number of daily activities.
5. **Whether premiums can rise**, and how. Policies differ here and the rules are in the outline of coverage.

Two things to do before any of that: check your own Medicare coverage, because the answer depends on it, and ask a tax advisor how premiums and benefits are treated for you. The [reference page on what Medicare does not pay for](/learn/what-medicare-does-not-pay-for/) is a good companion read.

## The short version

- Medicare pays skilled nursing for up to 100 days after a qualifying hospital stay, with {{partA.snf}} a day from day 21, and nothing for custodial care.
- Plan G pays that daily coinsurance in full, so a Plan G household only needs to think about custodial help.
- An Advantage household has daily copays up to a maximum that runs to $8,900 in Maricopa County for 2027, plus the custodial gap.
- Short-term care pays cash for about a year or less, is easier to qualify for than long-term care, and is not a replacement for it.
