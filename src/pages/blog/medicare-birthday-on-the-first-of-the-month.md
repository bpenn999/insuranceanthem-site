**If your birthday is on the 1st, Medicare can start on the first day of the month before your birthday month.** Medicare.gov puts it in one line: "If your birthday is on the first of the month, coverage starts the month before you turn 65." The reason is a Social Security rule that you reach an age the day before your birthday. So a March 1, 2027 birthday is a February Medicare birthday, and everything that hangs off it, the sign-up window, the Medigap window and the Part B penalty deadline, moves back a month with it.

This one matters right now for anyone born **January 1, 1962**. Federal rules count you as 65 on December 31, 2026, so Medicare can start December 1, 2026, if you sign up by November 30. It is easy to assume you have until after New Year's, and you do not, not if you want the earliest start.

## Why a 1st birthday counts as the month before

The rule is not a Medicare quirk. It sits in the Social Security regulations, at [20 CFR §404.2(c)(4)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/20/404.2): an individual "attains a given age on the first moment of the day preceding" their birthday. Born on March 1, you are legally 65 from the first moment of February 28.

Medicare's sign-up window is built on that date. Under [42 CFR §407.14](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/407.14), the initial enrollment period is seven months long and centred on the month you first meet the age requirement. For a March 1 birthday that month is February, so the window runs November through May instead of December through June.

Only the 1st moves you. Born on the 2nd, you are 65 on the 1st of your own birthday month, and the normal calendar applies.

## Every 2027 date if your 65th birthday is on the 1st

This is the full year for anyone born on the first of a month in 1962. "Sign up by" is the last day to enroll and still have coverage start on the earliest possible date.

Missing the "sign up by" date costs you nothing but time. Under [42 CFR §407.25](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/407.25), anyone who enrolls in the last four months of the window gets coverage the month after they sign up. The penalty only starts if the whole seven months go by without Part B and you have no qualifying job-based coverage. If you would rather not count months, the [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) does it from your birth date and handles the 1st automatically.

## What else moves a month earlier

People usually ask about the start date and stop there. Four other dates move with it.

- **Your Medigap open enrollment.** The six months when no Medicare Supplement company can ask health questions begin the first month you have Part B and are 65 or older. For a 1st birthday with an early Part B start, that window opens and closes a month sooner. Arizona has no birthday rule and no anytime switching right, so this is the one guaranteed window most people ever get. If you are considering a Supplement, [apply ahead of your start date](/blog/how-early-can-i-apply-for-a-medicare-supplement-plan/) so both begin on the same day.
- **Your Part B penalty deadline.** The window closes a month earlier too. Count from the "Medicare counts you as 65" column, not your birthday.
- **Your first Medicare Advantage or Part D choice.** The plan-choice window is tied to your Part A and Part B start, so it opens three months before the earlier date.
- **Your HSA.** If you are still contributing to a health savings account through work, contributions generally have to stop with the month your Medicare starts. For a 1st birthday that is the month before your birthday, which catches people who planned to contribute through it. [How HSAs and Medicare overlap](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) covers the details, and a tax advisor should confirm your own numbers.

If you are still working and covered by a large employer's plan, none of this forces you onto Part B. You can delay without a penalty and use a special enrollment period later. The 1st-of-the-month shift matters most for people who are retiring, self-employed, or on a small employer's plan.

## Born January 1, 1962: the December decision

This group has the most unusual choice of the year, and it lands in the middle of open enrollment.

Sign up by November 30 and Part A and Part B start **December 1, 2026**. That gives you one month of Medicare on 2026 terms before the {{figYear}} figures take over in January. Three things follow from that.

1. **December is billed at 2026 rates.** Your first Part B premium is the 2026 amount. From January it becomes {{partB.premium}} a month.
2. **You could meet two Part B deductibles in five weeks.** The Part B deductible is per calendar year. A visit or test in December counts toward the 2026 deductible, and January starts the {{partB.deductible}} {{figYear}} deductible from zero. If you are scheduling elective care, that is worth knowing.
3. **A December Medicare Advantage or Part D start means a 2026 plan.** It renews into its 2027 version on January 1. You can still compare 2027 plans before December 7, and the rules for someone who is [new to Medicare during open enrollment](/blog/turning-65-during-medicare-open-enrollment/) apply to you.

Sign up in December instead and coverage starts January 1, 2027, with no 2026 month at all. Neither choice is wrong. The question is whether you have other coverage running through December. If an employer plan or COBRA ends November 30, the December 1 start closes the gap. If you are covered through December, January may be simpler. Your Medigap clock still counts from December either way.
