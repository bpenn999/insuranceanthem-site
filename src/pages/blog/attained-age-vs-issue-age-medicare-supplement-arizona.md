**An issue-age Medicare Supplement usually costs more at 65 and less later, because its price never goes up for your birthday. An attained-age policy starts cheaper and climbs every year you age.** Medicare.gov's own example shows the trade: an attained-age policy at $120 a month, rising $6 a year, overtakes a $145 issue-age policy at age 70. If you are 65 and expect to keep your Medigap policy into your 80s, issue-age, where it is offered, usually comes out ahead. If you expect to drop it within a few years, attained-age usually wins.

Most of the 65th-birthday Medigap conversations I have in north Peoria right now are with Vistancia and Trilogy households, and many of them have been handed a spreadsheet of Plan G quotes sorted from cheapest to most expensive. That sort order is the problem. The cheapest line at 65 is often the attained-age one, and the spreadsheet does not show what it will cost at 75.

## The three ways a Medigap policy is priced

Every Plan G pays the same benefits whichever company sells it. Federal law standardizes the letters. What isn't standardized is how the company sets the price, and [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/medigap/basics/costs) names three methods:

- **Community-rated** (also called no-age-rated). Everyone with the policy pays the same premium, whatever their age.
- **Issue-age-rated** (also called entry-age-rated). The premium is based on your age on the day you buy. Buy at 65 and you are priced as a 65-year-old for as long as you keep the policy.
- **Attained-age-rated.** The premium is based on how old you are now, so it goes up every year as you age.

All three can go up for inflation, claims and other factors. The difference is whether age is an *extra* reason your premium rises. With attained-age pricing it is, every year. With the other two methods, your birthday has no effect on the price.

Three things come out of that table:

1. **The monthly lines cross at 70.** From then on, the attained-age policy costs more every month.
2. **Total cost crosses at 74.** By the end of age 74, the issue-age buyer has paid less in total, even after paying more for the first five years.
3. **By 84 the gap is $7,680** in this example, and the attained-age premium is still rising.

The exact ages will differ with real quotes. The shape almost never does: attained-age is cheaper at the start and more expensive later.

## The Arizona detail that makes this a one-time choice

Arizona is not a state that requires one pricing method. Its Medicare Supplement statute, [A.R.S. §20-1133](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/20/01133.htm), names attained-age rating directly, so attained-age policies are sold here alongside other methods. The statute also lets an insurer file an **early enrollment discount** that "shall diminish over a period of time", and says that discount is not an attained-age structure. Insurers have to tell applicants how the discount shrinks.

In practice that gives you a fourth thing to check. A policy can look cheap at 65 partly because a new-enrollee discount is built into the first few years. That is legitimate, but the discount is temporary, and the quote should say how fast it phases out.

The rule that makes the choice stick is the one Arizona doesn't have. **Arizona has no birthday rule and no anytime right to switch Medicare Supplement policies without health questions.** Your federal [Medigap open enrollment](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/) runs six months from the month your Part B starts at 65 or older. During that window an insurer can't use medical underwriting: it can't turn you down or charge you more because of your health. After it ends, a new insurer can usually ask medical questions and say no.

So picture the person who chose attained-age at 65 and wants out at 73, when the premium has climbed past the issue-age price they turned down. If their health has changed, they may not be able to move. And if they can, the issue-age policy is now priced at age 73, not 65. The cheaper start was really a long-term choice, and they didn't realise it at the time.

## When attained-age is the right call

Attained-age is not a trap for everyone. It can make sense if:

- **You expect to keep the policy only a few years.** For example, you plan to move to a community-rated state, or you are bridging to retiree coverage.
- **Cash flow at 65 matters more than total cost at 80.** That can be the case in the first years of retirement, before Social Security or a pension starts.
- **You are buying later than 65.** At 72, an issue-age policy is priced on 72, so the early-years advantage of issue-age is smaller.
- **The only issue-age policy you are offered costs much more** than the attained-age options. If the crossover lands in your mid-80s, the case for issue-age weakens.

For most households buying at 65 who expect Original Medicare plus a supplement to be their coverage for life, the long run favours paying more now for a price that does not rise with age. That is especially true for the 85383 retirees I talk to who bought in Peoria to stay.

## How to compare quotes before you sign

Medicare.gov's advice is to compare the same plan letter across companies and to ask each company how it prices. Here is how I'd do it at a kitchen table:

1. **Write the rating method next to every quote:** community, issue-age or attained-age. If the quote doesn't say, ask. It's a fair question and the insurer should answer it.
2. **Ask whether an early enrollment discount is built in,** and how many years it takes to phase out.
3. **Ask for the insurer's rate increase history** on that policy in Arizona for the past few years. Past increases don't promise future ones, but a company that has raised rates steeply before may do so again.
4. **Price the policy at 75 and 80,** not just 65. For attained-age, the insurer's age-banded rate table shows the step-ups.
5. **Check the discounts Medicare.gov lists:** household or married, non-smoker, annual pay and automatic payment. A household discount can change which quote is cheapest for a couple.
6. **Decide inside the six months.** Everything above is only a free choice while that window is open. If you are still weighing a supplement against Medicare Advantage, start with [the side-by-side](/learn/medicare-advantage-vs-medigap/).
