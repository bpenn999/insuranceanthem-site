**Yes. Medicare premiums are a deductible medical expense, but only if you itemize, and only the part of your total medical and dental costs that is more than 7.5 percent of your adjusted gross income.** [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) names Part B and Part D premiums as medical expenses, and Part A premiums for people who buy Part A because they do not get it premium-free. Medigap and Medicare Advantage premiums count as medical insurance too. Premiums withheld from Social Security count the same as premiums you pay by check.

That is the national answer. Two things make it different here. Arizona lets you itemize on the state return even when you take the standard deduction federally. And the retirees I work with in North Peoria, around Vistancia, Trilogy and the Lake Pleasant Parkway and Happy Valley corridor, usually have incomes high enough that the 7.5 percent floor matters more than the premiums. This is education, not tax advice: your own return is a question for your tax advisor.

## Why the 7.5 percent floor stops most people

The floor rises with your income, and so does IRMAA, but not at the same speed.

Take a married couple in Vistancia with $300,000 of adjusted gross income. Their floor is $22,500: nothing below that line helps them. If that income puts them in the second IRMAA tier, each pays {{irmaa.t2.partB}} a month for Part B in {{figYear}}. Two Part B premiums at that level come to about $10,000 for the year. Add a Medigap plan and a drug plan for each of them and they are still usually short of $22,500 before anyone has seen a doctor.

Then the itemized total has to beat the standard deduction, which for a married couple filing jointly is $32,200 for 2026, plus an extra amount for each spouse 65 or older. Medical costs above the floor, plus state and local taxes, mortgage interest and charitable gifts, all have to add up past that number before itemizing helps at all.

So for most households I meet, the premiums alone change nothing. The years when they do matter look like this:

- **A big care year.** A long rehab stay, major dental work, hearing aids, or a parent's or spouse's memory-care costs can push the total well past the floor. Qualified long-term care services count as medical expenses in IRS rules, and so do qualified long-term care insurance premiums, up to the age-based limit.
- **A low-income year.** The year after you stop working, before required distributions start, your AGI may be low enough that the floor is small.
- **A big charitable year.** If you are itemizing anyway because of a large gift, every medical dollar above the floor now counts.

The new federal deduction for people 65 and older, available for tax years 2025 through 2028, is separate from all of this. It does not require itemizing, but it [phases out](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/new-and-enhanced-deductions-for-individuals) starting at $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income on a joint return ($75,000 single), so many households paying IRMAA get little or none of it.

## The Arizona rule worth knowing

This is the part most national articles leave out. The Arizona Department of Revenue says [you may claim itemized deductions on an Arizona return even if you take the standard deduction on your federal return](https://azdor.gov/individuals/income-tax-filing-assistance/deductions-and-exemptions). You prepare a federal Schedule A anyway, attach it to the Arizona return, and add an Arizona Schedule A if any line differs.

In practice, that means a year with heavy medical costs can help on the state return even when it falls short federally. Be realistic about the size of it, though. Arizona's income tax is a flat [2.5 percent](https://azdor.gov/forms/individual-income-tax-highlights), so $10,000 of extra Arizona deductions saves about $250. That is worth claiming if your preparer is already doing the work. It is not a reason to choose one Medicare plan over another.

Arizona also does not tax Social Security benefits, so the premiums withheld from your benefit were never part of Arizona taxable income in the first place.

## Two routes that work better than itemizing

**An HSA you built before Medicare.** Once you are on Medicare you cannot add new money to a health savings account, but you can spend what is already there. [IRS Publication 969](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969) lets you take tax-free distributions for Medicare premiums once you are 65, Part B and Part D included. Medigap premiums are the exception. That route has no floor and no itemizing test. You cannot deduct a premium and also pay it tax-free from the HSA, so pick one. The [HSA and Medicare post](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) covers the six-month look-back that catches people as they enroll.

**Self-employment income.** If you still have consulting or business income, Medicare premiums can count toward the self-employed health insurance deduction on [Form 7206](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i7206). It comes off before adjusted gross income, so you get it without itemizing and it lowers the MAGI that sets IRMAA two years later. The [self-employed at 65 post](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-im-self-employed/) goes through the conditions.

## Where IRMAA fits

A medical deduction on Schedule A does **not** lower IRMAA. Social Security sets IRMAA from modified adjusted gross income, and itemized deductions come after AGI. That is why the itemizing question and the IRMAA question rarely meet. Things that move AGI itself, such as the timing of a [Roth conversion](/blog/does-a-roth-conversion-affect-medicare-premiums/) or the [sale of a house](/blog/does-selling-my-house-affect-medicare-premiums/), are what change the premium. The [IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) shows which tier a given income lands in, and the [IRMAA explainer](/learn/irmaa-explained/) covers the two-year lookback.

## What to pull together this winter

1. **Your SSA-1099**, which arrives in January and lists the Medicare premiums withheld from your benefits in {{year}}.
2. **Premium statements** for any Medigap, Part D or Medicare Advantage plan you paid directly.
3. **Receipts for care Medicare did not pay for**: dental, hearing aids, glasses, deductibles and copays.
4. **A note of any HSA distributions**, so nothing is counted twice.
5. **A question for your preparer:** "Would itemizing on the Arizona return alone help this year?"
