**Usually, yes. If you have Medicare Part A and your employer's drug coverage is not creditable, you are building a Part D late enrollment penalty from the day your Part D enrollment window closes: 1% of the national base beneficiary premium for every full month without creditable coverage, added to your drug premium for life once you do sign up.** The employer plan still pays your prescriptions. It just does not count as protection. And for 2027 the test got harder: CMS now requires an employer plan to be designed to pay, on average, **73% of members' drug costs** to pass the simplified test, up from the 60% most plans used for years.

That is why this question has jumped in north Peoria this fall. Plenty of people in Vistancia and the 85383 corridor are 65, still working, and keeping a company plan instead of moving to Medicare. Most of them took premium-free Part A at 65, because it costs nothing. Some of them opened a one-page notice from HR in September or early October that said, for the first time, that their prescription coverage is "non-creditable." Most of those notices went into a drawer.

## What "creditable" means, and why the line moved

Creditable drug coverage is coverage expected to pay, on average, at least as much as Medicare's standard Part D benefit. If what you have meets that bar, you can delay Part D with no penalty for as long as you keep it. If it does not, Medicare treats you as if you had no drug coverage at all for penalty purposes.

The bar moved because Part D got richer. Since 2025 the standard benefit has an annual out-of-pocket cap, {{partD.cap}} this year and $2,400 in 2027, and plans now carry far more of the cost of expensive drugs. An employer plan that comfortably matched the old Part D can fall short of the new one without a single change to its own design.

For years, employers that did not hire an actuary could use a shortcut CMS published in 2009: cover brand and generic drugs, give reasonable pharmacy access, and be designed to pay at least 60% of members' drug costs. CMS's final rule of April 6, 2026 retires that shortcut. For 2027, the only shortcut left requires **73%**. CMS's own modeling in the rule projects the figure keeps rising, to about 75% by 2030. So a plan that passes this year can fail next year, and the notice is worth reading every fall, not once.

## How to find out where you stand

You should not have to guess. Federal rules at 42 CFR §423.56 require your plan to send a creditable coverage notice at four points: before your Part D initial enrollment period, before the annual enrollment period that opens October 15, whenever a change affects the plan's status, and **any time you ask**. CMS tells employers to send it before October 15 every year.

So the first step is simple: find this year's notice, or email HR and ask for the plan's 2027 creditable coverage determination in writing. If your employer offers more than one medical plan, ask about each one. It is common for the richer plan on the menu to pass and the high-deductible plan to fail.

High-deductible plans deserve a specific word. In the rule, CMS noted that plans with higher deductibles, including high-deductible health plans, "might have appeared less likely" to meet the test, though covering maintenance drugs before the deductible can help. The health savings account attached to the plan is not drug coverage and does not count either way. If you are on a high-deductible plan at 65, read the notice before you assume anything.

## What the penalty costs at the 2027 rate

Medicare's formula: 1% of the national base beneficiary premium, multiplied by the number of full months you went without creditable coverage, rounded to the nearest ten cents, added every month for as long as you have Medicare drug coverage. CMS set the base at **$41.33 for 2027**, up from $38.99 in 2026.

## Three ways to answer a non-creditable notice

**1. Keep the work plan and add a stand-alone Part D plan.** This is the usual answer for someone still working. Medicare does not make you drop employer coverage to join a drug plan, and you need Part A or Part B to enroll, which most people already have. Your prescriptions still run through the employer plan the way they always have; the Part D plan is there mainly to keep the penalty clock stopped. Before you enroll, ask HR in writing whether adding Part D changes anything about your group coverage. For active employees it usually does not.

**2. Move to a creditable option at your employer's next open enrollment.** If the company's PPO passes and its high-deductible plan does not, switching solves the problem without a second premium. It may also cost more each paycheck, and it ends health savings account contributions, so price both sides.

**3. Leave the employer plan and move to Medicare.** If you were planning to retire within the year anyway, this can be the cleanest route. Leaving employer coverage gives you a Special Enrollment Period of two full months after the month the coverage ends to join a drug plan. That window covers the switch. It does not erase penalty months already counted while the plan was non-creditable. The order of events is laid out in [how to sign up for Part B when you retire after 65](/blog/how-do-i-sign-up-for-medicare-part-b-when-i-retire-after-65/).

## If your plan just lost creditable status

This is the 2027 situation, and Medicare has a specific rule for it. If your coverage was creditable and changes so that it no longer is, you get a Special Enrollment Period lasting **two full months after the month you lose creditable coverage, or two full months after you are notified, whichever is later**.

In practice, for a calendar-year plan that turns non-creditable on January 1, 2027, that window runs through the end of March. But there is a cleaner route open right now: the annual enrollment period runs until **December 7**, 29 days from today. Join a stand-alone drug plan by then and it starts January 1, the same day your work plan's status changes, with no uncovered month in between.

One more protection people rarely hear about. If you were never properly told your coverage was not creditable, 42 CFR §423.56(g) lets you apply to CMS to have that coverage treated as creditable for penalty purposes. It is a remedy, not a strategy. It works far better when you can show the notice never came than when it is sitting in a drawer.

## The health savings account wrinkle

Some still-working 65-year-olds have not taken Part A at all, because enrolling in any part of Medicare ends their health savings account contributions. Part D requires Part A or Part B, so they cannot simply add a drug plan. When you do apply, Social Security generally backdates premium-free Part A up to six months, which has its own contribution traps.

If that is you and your high-deductible plan's drug coverage is non-creditable, sort out the order with Social Security and your tax advisor before you sign anything. ## Before December 7, in order

1. **Find the notice.** If you cannot, request the 2027 creditable coverage determination from HR in writing.
2. **If it says creditable,** file it with your tax records. Years from now it is your proof if a plan ever charges you a penalty.
3. **If it says non-creditable and you have Part A,** pick a stand-alone Part D plan by December 7 for a January 1 start. Run your actual prescriptions through [Medicare's Plan Finder](https://www.medicare.gov/plan-compare/); the [2027 Arizona Part D premiums](/blog/medicare-part-d-plans-arizona-2027/) are a useful starting list.
4. **If you are turning 65 in the next few months,** your Part D window runs alongside your Part B window. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) builds both from your birthday.
5. **Ask again next fall.** The 73% bar becomes 75% within a few years.
