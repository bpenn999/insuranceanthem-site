**High-deductible Plan G has a $3,050 deductible in 2027, up from $2,950 in 2026, according to CMS's October 2026 announcement.** You pay Medicare-covered costs, including the Part B deductible, until your total for the year reaches $3,050. After that the policy pays exactly what standard Plan G pays for the rest of the calendar year. It is open to anyone new to Medicare on or after January 1, 2020, which takes in everyone turning 65 in 2027. Whether it is worth it depends on two things: could you write a $3,050 check in a bad year without noticing, and are you content to keep this plan for life? In Arizona, switching later usually means answering health questions.

I get asked about it most by people turning 65 in Vistancia, Blackstone and the rest of the 85383 corridor. They are healthy and used to a high-deductible employer plan with an HSA, and they look at the gap between the two premiums and wonder why anyone pays full price. That is a fair question, and the answer is not "never." But the deductible is the least important part of the decision. What matters is a rule you only meet once.

## How the $3,050 actually works

The high-deductible version is standard Plan G with one change: nothing is paid until you have spent the deductible yourself. Medicare.gov's chart puts it as paying "Medicare-covered costs (coinsurance, copayments and deductibles)" up to the amount "before your policy pays anything."

Three details matter in practice:

- **The Part B deductible counts.** Standard Plan G leaves you the Part B deductible ({{partB.deductible}} in {{year}}; CMS announces the 2027 figure in November). On the high-deductible version it is simply the first dollars toward $3,050.
- **The Part A hospital deductible counts too.** One inpatient stay puts {{partA.deductible}} (the {{year}} figure) toward the total, so a year with a hospital admission and a few weeks of follow-up care can reach $3,050 quickly. From there you pay nothing more for covered services.
- **It resets every January 1, and the figure moves.** CMS raises it each year by the change in the consumer price index, as section 1882(p)(11) of the Social Security Act requires. The 2027 notice applied a 3.4 percent increase and rounded to the nearest $10.

What it does not include: your premium, and prescription drugs. Plan G has never covered drugs, so you still need a Part D plan, which has its own out-of-pocket cap ({{partD.cap}} in {{year}}). For a household that likes to know its worst case, the arithmetic is unusually clean. Add the deductible, the Part D cap and a year of premiums, and nothing Medicare covers can cost you more than that.

One number from those files puts the deductible in context. **41 of the 52 general Advantage plans in Maricopa for 2027 have an in-network out-of-pocket maximum above $3,050**, and every PPO in the county does. So the high-deductible supplement's worst year is lower than most Advantage plans' worst year, and it applies with any doctor who takes Medicare, not only those in a network. The Advantage plan usually has no premium; the supplement always has one. That is the trade. ## When the high-deductible version is the right call

I would put it on the table for a 65-year-old who fits most of this list:

- **You rarely see a doctor.** An annual wellness visit and a screening or two. Medicare covers most preventive services at no cost to you, so those bills mostly never reach the deductible.
- **You could pay $3,050 in a bad year without changing your plans.** If that sum would mean selling an investment or skipping a trip, the lower premium is not worth it.
- **You have an HSA balance from your working years.** That money cannot pay the Medigap premium, but it can pay every dollar of the deductible tax-free. More on that below.
- **You split the year between Peoria and another state.** Like standard G, it works with any provider who takes Medicare anywhere in the country, with no network to leave behind when you drive north in May.
- **You know what you want at 80.** That last one is the subject of the next section.

It is a weaker fit if you already have a specialist you see every few months, a joint you expect to have replaced, or a diagnosis that will need regular imaging. Those years land on the deductible every January, and the premium saving shrinks to nothing or goes negative.

## The rule that decides it in Arizona

For six months starting the month you are 65 and enrolled in Part B, every Medicare Supplement sold in Arizona must take you at standard rates, with no health questions. That window is the [Medigap open enrollment period](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/), and it happens once.

After it closes, moving from the high-deductible version to standard Plan G is a new application, and the insurer can ask about your health and decline you or charge more. **Arizona has no birthday rule and no annual switching window for Medicare Supplements.** If you search this question you will find pages saying otherwise, but they are describing other states. So the realistic way to think about it is not "high-deductible now, standard later if I need it." The person who needs to switch is, almost by definition, the person the underwriter turns down.

There are exceptions. Some guaranteed-issue situations, such as an insurer going out of business or a move out of an Advantage plan during your first year, let you buy again without health questions. But none of them exist to fix a deductible you wish you had not chosen.

So choose the version you expect to want in your late seventies, when the premium on any supplement will be higher and your health history longer. For some households that is still the high-deductible version: they would rather pay the deductible than the premium every year for the rest of their lives, and they can afford the bad years. For others, paying more each month and having nothing to track is the point. Both are reasonable. The mistake is choosing on the premium alone and assuming you can switch later.

## How to run the numbers on a real quote

You do not need a spreadsheet. With quotes for both versions from the same insurer, at your age and ZIP, do this:

1. **Find the annual premium gap.** Standard G's monthly premium minus the high-deductible premium, times 12.
2. **Find the extra a bad year costs you.** Standard G already leaves you the Part B deductible, so the high-deductible version's added exposure in a bad year is $3,050 minus that, a little under $2,800 at the 2026 Part B deductible.
3. **Compare them.** If the yearly saving is, say, half of that extra exposure, you come out ahead unless a bad year arrives more often than every other year. The bigger the gap, the more bad years you can absorb.

Then look at each insurer's rate history for the past several years, not just this year's price. A low starting premium that rises steeply is worth less than it looks. Not every company that sells Plan G in Arizona sells the high-deductible version, so this is a question for each quote. The [Plan G vs. Plan N comparison](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/) uses the same logic for the other common choice at 65.

## Paying the deductible from an HSA

This is where the high-deductible version suits people coming off an employer HDHP. IRS Publication 969 lets HSA money pay Medicare premiums once you are 65, "other than premiums for a Medicare supplemental policy, such as Medigap." So the supplement premium comes out of ordinary money. Everything inside the deductible, from the hospital deductible and Part B coinsurance to office bills, is a qualified medical expense, and the HSA can pay it tax-free for the rest of your life.

In effect a well-funded HSA covers the bad years, and the lower premium is the saving. Two cautions. You can no longer contribute once Medicare starts, and if you enroll after 65, Part A is backdated up to six months. [Can I contribute to an HSA while on Medicare?](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) explains the timing. And nothing about the supplement changes IRMAA, which is set from your tax return, not your plan choice.

## What to do before your window opens

If you turn 65 in the first half of 2027:

1. **Find your Medigap window dates.** The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) builds them from your birthday. The window starts with your Part B, so a later Part B start because you are still working pushes it back too.
2. **Write down your doctors and the next five years of care you can foresee.** That list, more than the premium, decides whether a $3,050 deductible is likely to be reached.
3. **Get both quotes from the same insurers** and run the three steps above.
4. **Decide inside the window.** If a supplement is the plan, most insurers will take the application shortly before Part B begins, so the policy can start the same day.
