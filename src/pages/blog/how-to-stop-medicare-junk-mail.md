**You can cut most of it off with three opt-outs: OptOutPrescreen.com for prescreened insurance offers (five years online, or permanently by signed form), DMAchoice.org for most other promotional mail ($6, good for 10 years), and the National Do Not Call Registry for sales calls, which the FTC says should stop within 31 days.** What none of them stop is mail from a company you have already given your details to, and that is where most of the phone calls come from.

The other half of the answer is that some of it is legal and some of it is not. Federal rules let Medicare Advantage plans write to you and email you. They do not let plans or their agents cold-call you, text you or turn up on your doorstep. Knowing which is which tells you what to bin, what to report, and which envelopes in the pile you actually need to open.

## Why it starts around 64

Your date of birth sits on marketing lists that insurers and agencies buy, and 64 is when you become worth writing to. The mail usually ramps up about six months before your birthday month, peaks during your enrollment window, and spikes again every October when the fall enrollment period opens.

There is a local reason it feels heavier here. Maricopa County is a crowded market. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, there are 45 general Medicare Advantage drug plans in the county from 13 organizations, before you count the special needs plans and the nine stand-alone drug plans sold across Arizona. Every one of those plans, and the call centers and agencies that sell them, has a reason to find a 64-year-old in 85383.

None of that is a reason to feel hunted. It does mean the mailbox is a sales channel for about a year, and it pays to decide in advance what gets opened.

Do all three in one sitting. It takes about twenty minutes, and if you are married, register both names. The mail does not stop overnight. Pieces already printed keep arriving for weeks, so judge it after a couple of months, not after a couple of days.

## The calls usually start with something you filled in

This is the part most people miss. Under 42 CFR 422.2264, Medicare Advantage plans may contact you uninvited "by conventional mail and other print media" or by email, as long as every email has an opt-out. The same rule says they may not use "telephone solicitation (that is, cold calling), robocalls, text messages, or voicemail messages" unless you asked, may not go door to door, and may not send you direct messages on social media.

So when the phone starts ringing four times a day, it is usually because permission was given somewhere. The usual culprits:

- **A postage-paid reply card** that asked you to "confirm your benefits" or "request your benefits guide." Mailing it back is a request for contact.
- **An online quote form.** Many sites that compare plans pass your details to several call centers at once. One form can mean a dozen callers.
- **A television ad's toll-free number**, where calling in is treated as asking to be called back.

The Do Not Call Registry does not cancel permission you gave. If a company you contacted keeps calling, tell that caller plainly to put you on their internal do-not-call list, and note the date and the company's name. The rule also requires plans to tell the people they contact, at least once a year in writing, how to opt out of future calls about plan business, so look for that notice and use it.

A caller who says they are from "Medicare" is the one to be most careful with. Medicare.gov says "Medicare will never call you to sell you anything or visit you at your home." If someone asks for your Medicare Number or Social Security number on a call you did not place, hang up and call 1-800-MEDICARE yourself.

## The envelopes you must not throw away

The cost of an aggressive purge is binning the one letter that matters. NCOA's advice is the simplest rule there is: open anything from CMS, the Department of Health and Human Services or the Social Security Administration. In practice, for someone turning 65 in early 2027, that pile includes:

- **Your Medicare card.** It comes from CMS in a plain envelope that looks a lot like junk. People throw it away every year. [When will I get my Medicare card?](/blog/when-will-i-get-my-medicare-card/) covers what it looks like and when it arrives.
- **Letters from Social Security** about your enrollment, your premium, or an income-related surcharge. For the household incomes common in Vistancia and Blackstone, the surcharge letter, IRMAA, is a real one. The {{year}} surcharge starts above {{irmaa.singleStart}} of income for a single filer, and that letter has appeal rights with deadlines attached.
- **Your employer's creditable coverage notice** for drug coverage, if you are still working. It is the document that protects you from the Part D late penalty later. It usually arrives with your benefits paperwork, not from Medicare.
- **Once you join a plan, everything from that plan,** including the Annual Notice of Change each fall. This year that letter mattered more than usual: several Maricopa plans are ending on December 31, and the [plans ending in Arizona for 2027](/blog/medicare-advantage-plans-ending-arizona-2027/) post lists them.

A quick test for everything else: government mail names the Social Security Administration or the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, not a "Medicare Benefits Center" or "Senior Services Department." Bulk-rate postage marked "PRSRT STD" is almost always marketing. And no official letter will ask you to mail back a card to "keep your benefits."

## The north Peoria problem: cluster boxes and forwarded mail

Two things make this worse at the north end of the city. Most newer neighborhoods along the Happy Valley corridor get their mail in shared cluster boxes, often a walk from the house, so a week of Medicare mailers piles up fast and the one envelope with your new Medicare Number sits in the same stack. And a good share of 85383 arrived recently from another state, with mail still forwarding from the old address. A card that goes to the old house, or gets lost in a forwarding gap, is a card someone else can read.

Two cheap fixes help. Update your address with Social Security as soon as you move, not just with the Postal Service, because that is the address CMS uses. And if you are expecting your card, check the box every day for a couple of weeks rather than letting it fill up. If you are a part-year resident with a second home, decide which address is your Medicare address and keep it consistent; [Which state do I sign up for Medicare in if I live in two states?](/blog/which-state-do-i-sign-up-for-medicare-if-i-live-in-two-states/) walks through that choice.

## What to do this month if you turn 65 in 2027

1. **Run the three opt-outs** above, for both spouses.
2. **Stop filling in reply cards and quote forms.** If you want help comparing plans, pick one person to work with on purpose rather than letting a form pick twelve for you.
3. **Set aside a folder** for anything from Social Security, CMS or your employer's benefits office, and open those first.
4. **Write down your real dates.** The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) shows your own seven-month window from your birth date, so the deadlines on the mailers stop sounding like yours.
5. **Report the pushy ones.** A plan or agent that cold-calls you, texts you uninvited or shows up at the door is breaking a federal marketing rule. Note the name and date and report it to 1-800-MEDICARE.
