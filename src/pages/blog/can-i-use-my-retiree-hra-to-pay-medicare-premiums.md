**Usually, yes. A retiree health reimbursement arrangement (HRA) can pay you back for Medicare Part B and Part D premiums, Medigap premiums and Medicare Advantage premiums, and many also repay copays and deductibles, as long as your employer's plan document lists them.** You buy the coverage yourself, pay the bill, then send the HRA a claim. What the account cannot do is stand in for the coverage. It is not Medicare, it does not let you put off Part B, and it is not a drug plan. From **January 1, 2027**, under CMS's final rule of April 6, 2026, an HRA no longer even has to send you the Part D notice that used to say so.

I get this question most from north Peoria. Vistancia, Trilogy and the 85383 corridor are full of people who retired from large companies and public employers, and more of those employers now end the group retiree plan at 65. The retiree gets a letter, an annual dollar amount and a phone number for a benefits administrator, and is told to "choose your own Medicare plan." That letter tends to arrive in the same months as the Medicare card, which is a lot of paper to sort out at once.

## What an HRA is, and why employers switched to one

A retiree HRA is an account your former employer funds, often a set amount each year, that reimburses health costs you pay yourself. It is not insurance. There is no network, no claims processing for your doctor and no drug formulary. You pick an individual Medicare plan on the open market, and the HRA repays some or all of what it costs you.

Employers like it because the cost is capped at the deposit, and the plan stops depending on whatever group carrier they used to buy coverage from. Retirees often come out about even, or ahead, because the individual Medicare market is competitive and a group retiree plan was frequently priced for a younger, mixed population. Whether you come out ahead depends on what the account repays and what you choose to buy with it.

Retiree-only arrangements also sit outside some rules that limit what an employer can reimburse for active workers. That is why a retiree account can repay Part B and Medigap premiums when the same employer generally could not simply hand an active employee money for a Medicare premium.

Two practical points that rarely make it into the letter. First, most retiree HRAs pay you back after you pay. Part B is billed by Medicare or taken from your Social Security check, and your Medigap or drug plan bills you monthly, so you front the money and claim it. Many administrators let you set up recurring premium claims so you are not filing twelve times a year. Second, if your HRA repays Part B, ask whether it also repays an IRMAA surcharge. For a household above {{irmaa.jointStart}} of joint income two years back, that line can be worth more than the base premium difference between two plans.

## What it cannot do: Part B, Part D and the notice that is going away

**It does not let you delay Part B.** Medicare lets someone covered by a current employer's group plan put off Part B without a penalty. A retiree HRA is not coverage from current work, so that Special Enrollment Period does not apply. Once you are 65 and retired, sign up for Part B in your initial enrollment period. Most retiree HRAs are written on the assumption that you have Part A and Part B anyway; Nevada's plan document requires Part B outright. The longer version of that rule, for any retiree plan, is in [do I need Medicare Part B if I have retiree insurance](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-part-b-if-i-have-retiree-insurance/).

**It is not drug coverage.** This is where the 2027 change matters. For years, account-based plans fell under the same rule as group drug plans: send Medicare-eligible members a yearly notice saying whether the coverage is "creditable" for Part D. CMS's Contract Year 2027 final rule ends that requirement for HRAs and individual coverage HRAs, for coverage beginning January 1, 2027. CMS's reason was simple: these accounts do not offer prescription drug coverage directly.

That is sensible housekeeping, and it removes one confusing notice. It also removes the one piece of paper that told some retirees, in writing, that the account did not protect them from the Part D penalty. From 2027 the HRA is quiet on the subject. Medicare is not. Go 63 days or more after your Part D window without creditable drug coverage, and a penalty of 1% of the national base premium for every uncovered month is added to your drug plan for as long as you have it. The fix is simple: when Medicare starts, enroll in a stand-alone Part D plan or an Advantage plan that includes drugs, and let the HRA pay you back for it.

## The Maricopa County math: why an HRA tilts toward Medigap

This is where a reimbursement account changes the decision, not just the paperwork. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, **39 of the 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage in Maricopa County carry a $0 premium for 2027.** Arizona's nine stand-alone Part D plans run from $5.30 to $191.50 a month, and **none is $0 for 2027**, down from four this year.

So consider a retiree whose HRA repays premiums only, not copays:

- On a $0-premium Advantage plan, the account has little to repay apart from Part B, if it covers Part B. Much of the deposit may go unused, and whether that money rolls forward or disappears depends on your plan.
- On Medigap plus a Part D plan, there are two monthly premiums to claim. The account can cover much or all of the cost that normally makes Medigap the more expensive route, and in return you get a plan that works with any provider in the country that takes Medicare, no referrals and predictable costs.

That is not a reason to pick Medigap, and it is not a recommendation of any plan. It is a reason to run the comparison on what you will actually pay after the HRA, not on the sticker premium. For a household that winters in Peoria and summers in another state, the national reach of Medigap is usually the bigger point anyway. The trade-offs are set out on the [Medicare Advantage versus Medigap](/learn/medicare-advantage-vs-medigap/) page.

## Time it with your 65th birthday, not with the HRA letter

The order of events matters more than the account balance.

1. **Part B in your initial enrollment period.** It runs for seven months around your birthday month. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) builds your dates from your birthday.
2. **Medigap in the six months after Part B starts.** That is your one Medigap open enrollment window: insurers cannot refuse you or charge more for health history. Arizona has no birthday rule to give you a second chance later, so if the HRA math points to Medigap, this is when it is easiest to buy.
3. **A drug plan with the same start date.** Stand-alone Part D alongside Medigap, or drugs built into an Advantage plan.
4. **Then the HRA claims.** Set up recurring premium reimbursement with the administrator once the policies are issued.

If your plan requires you to buy through a designated exchange, step 2 and step 3 happen there. That does not change your Medicare rights. It does mean you should check which insurers and plans the exchange actually offers in Maricopa County before your Medigap window starts, not after.

One more date for this year. If you are already on Medicare and your employer is moving you to an HRA on January 1, the annual enrollment period runs until **December 7**, 28 days from today. Ask HR whether leaving the group plan counts as losing employer coverage, which can open a Special Enrollment Period, and whether a Medigap guaranteed-issue right applies. Get the answer in writing before you cancel anything.

## Before your first claim, in order

- **Get the plan document,** not just the welcome letter. You want the eligible-expense list, the rollover rule and any "must enroll through" requirement.
- **Ask four questions in writing:** Does it repay Part B? The IRMAA surcharge? Copays and deductibles, or premiums only? What happens to the balance for a surviving spouse?
- **Sign up for Part B and a drug plan on Medicare's schedule,** whatever the HRA letter says about its own start date.
- **Compare plans on net cost after reimbursement.** Run your prescriptions through [Medicare's Plan Finder](https://www.medicare.gov/plan-compare/) and check the [2027 Arizona Part D premiums](/blog/medicare-part-d-plans-arizona-2027/).
- **Keep the tax side with your tax advisor.** HRA reimbursements are generally not income, which also keeps them out of the figure Social Security uses for IRMAA, but your own plan's treatment is theirs to confirm.
