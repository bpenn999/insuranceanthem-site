  - q: Do you pay IRMAA if you have a Medicare Advantage plan?

You keep paying the Part B premium in a Medicare Advantage plan, so the Part B surcharge comes with it, and the drug coverage inside the plan carries the Part D surcharge.   - q: Does a $0 premium Medicare Advantage plan avoid IRMAA?
    a: No. The $0 is the plan's own premium. IRMAA is charged by Medicare on top of the Part B premium and on top of whatever the plan charges for drug coverage, even when that plan premium is zero. A single filer in the first 2026 tier in a $0 plan still pays $284.10 a month for Part B and a $14.50 Part D surcharge.
  - q: Is IRMAA paid to Medicare or to my plan?
    a: To Medicare, through Social Security. Both surcharges are normally withheld from your Social Security benefit, whatever way you pay the plan premium. If you are not drawing Social Security yet, or the benefit is too small, Medicare bills you directly. Your plan never collects it.
  - q: What happens if you don't pay the Part D IRMAA?
    a: Medicare ends your drug coverage. Under the federal rule, a directly billed Part D surcharge has a grace period that runs to the end of the third month after the billing month, and Social Security's manual says CMS then tells the plan to cancel coverage. In a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage, that is the plan you rely on, so a direct bill from Medicare is one to pay on time.
  - q: Do Medicare Advantage plans without drug coverage have IRMAA?
    a: Only the Part B piece. If your plan has no drug coverage and you have no other Medicare drug plan, there is no Part D surcharge to charge. Otherwise you risk a permanent late-enrollment penalty, and you generally cannot add a separate drug plan to an Advantage plan that leaves drugs out.
  - q: How is IRMAA calculated for 2027?

Social Security mails the determination late in the year, and CMS announces the 2027 dollar amounts with the Part B premium, usually in November. If your income dropped since 2025 because of a life-changing event such as retirement, you can ask Social Security to use a newer year with Form SSA-44.

**Yes. A Medicare Advantage plan does not take you out of IRMAA. You still pay the Part B premium in an Advantage plan, so the income-related Part B surcharge comes with it: {{irmaa.t1.partB}} to {{irmaa.t5.partB}} a month in {{year}} instead of {{partB.premium}}, once a single filer's {{irmaaTaxYear}} income passes {{irmaa.singleStart}} ({{irmaa.jointStart}} filing jointly). If the plan includes drug coverage, as most do, the Part D surcharge of {{irmaa.t1.partD}} to {{irmaa.t5.partD}} a month applies as well. A $0 plan premium changes neither.**

I hear the opposite assumption every fall in north Peoria. A couple in Vistancia or Trilogy sells a house in the Midwest, takes a big IRA distribution to cover the move, or does a Roth conversion in the first year of retirement, and two years later a letter from Social Security raises their Part B. When the Annual Enrollment Period opens, someone at the table says, "We'll take one of the $0 Advantage plans and get out of that." It is a reasonable guess, and it is wrong. IRMAA is charged by Medicare on your income, not by the plan on its price, and the plan you pick has almost nothing to do with it.

## Which IRMAA charge follows which kind of coverage

Social Security's operations manual (POMS HI 01101.001) lists who pays an income-related adjustment: anyone with Part B, Part B plus a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage, Part B plus a stand-alone drug plan, or a drug plan alone, whose income is over the threshold for their filing status. Lay that against the setups people actually carry and the pattern is simple. Part B triggers one charge, drug coverage triggers the other, and the label on the card does not matter.

The two rows that surprise people are the first and the last. The $0 plan still carries both surcharges, because the Part D surcharge is added "in addition to" the plan's drug premium, whatever that premium is. And a retiree group plan paid for by a former employer still leaves the surcharge with you. Social Security's manual says the Part D amount is withheld from your benefit "regardless of how a beneficiary pays their PDP or MA-PD premium."

## How it is billed, and the bill not to ignore

You never pay IRMAA to the plan. Social Security withholds both surcharges from your monthly benefit, next to the Part B premium. If you are not collecting Social Security yet, which is common for anyone holding out to 70 for the larger check, or the benefit is too small to cover it, Medicare bills you directly instead.

That direct bill matters more than people think. Under 42 CFR §423.44(e), a directly billed Part D surcharge has a grace period ending on the last day of the third month after the billing month. After that, CMS ends the drug coverage, and Social Security's manual says CMS "tells the PDP or MA-PD to cancel coverage." If your drug coverage sits inside your Advantage plan, that is the plan your doctors and prescriptions run through. Getting it back requires paying everything owed within three months of the disenrollment. If you have delayed Social Security, put the Medicare bill on autopay.

## The Advantage plans with no drug coverage

There is one setup with no Part D surcharge: an Advantage plan without drug coverage. On the CMS 2027 landscape file, Maricopa County has 52 general Medicare Advantage plans, and 7 of them leave drugs out. They are built for veterans who fill prescriptions through the VA and military retirees on TRICARE, both of which count as creditable drug coverage.

For anyone else, choosing one of those plans to avoid IRMAA is a bad trade. Medicare generally does not let you add a separate drug plan to an Advantage plan that excludes drugs, and going without creditable drug coverage builds a [Part D late-enrollment penalty](/learn/late-enrollment-penalties/) that you pay for as long as you have drug coverage. I wrote about that choice for people with no prescriptions in [do I need Part D if I don't take prescriptions](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-part-d-if-i-dont-take-prescriptions/), and for TRICARE households in [the TRICARE For Life post](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-part-b-if-i-have-tricare-for-life/).

## Should IRMAA change your Advantage vs. Medigap decision?

On its own, no. Look back at the first table: Advantage with drugs and Medigap with a drug plan carry the same two surcharges at the same tier. IRMAA is a cost you carry into whichever door you choose, so the real decision rests on everything else.

For the higher-income retirees I see across north Peoria, that usually comes down to three things. The first is whether the specialists you use at Arrowhead, in Sun City or at Mayo Clinic Arizona are in a given network for 2027. The second is how many months you spend away from Arizona. The third is whether you would rather pay a predictable Medigap premium or carry an out-of-pocket cap that runs as high as $8,900 on the 2027 Maricopa list. [Medicare Advantage vs. Medigap](/learn/medicare-advantage-vs-medigap/) covers the general trade. What IRMAA adds is a reason to look past the advertised $0, because your Medicare bill is never $0 at these income levels.

If your 2026 Advantage plan is one of the Maricopa plans being discontinued, the same logic holds. A forced switch does not raise or lower your surcharge. If you move to Medigap with a stand-alone drug plan, the Part D surcharge simply follows the new drug plan. The steps and deadlines for that switch are in [my Advantage plan is being discontinued](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/).

## What changes for 2027

Two things move each year, and neither is set yet.

- **The tax year.** 2027 IRMAA is based on your **2025** return. If 2025 held a home sale, a Roth conversion or a final year of salary, expect the letter from Social Security late this year. The [Roth conversion post](/blog/does-a-roth-conversion-affect-medicare-premiums/) and the [home-sale post](/blog/does-selling-my-house-affect-medicare-premiums/) cover those two triggers.
- **The dollar amounts and income brackets.** CMS announces them with the 2027 Part B premium, usually in November; the 2026 figures came out on November 14, 2025. Until then, every figure on this page is the {{year}} schedule.

If your income has dropped since 2025 because you retired, cut your hours or lost a spouse, you do not have to wait it out. Form SSA-44 asks Social Security to use a more recent year, and it works the same whichever plan you choose. [How to appeal IRMAA after retirement](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/) walks through it. For whether the conversion or the sale itself made sense, talk to your tax advisor; my part is the Medicare side.

## What I'd check before December 7

1. **Find your Social Security letter** and note your tier and which tax year it used.
2. **Price plans on the full cost**: the plan premium plus your Part B premium plus your Part D surcharge, not the plan premium alone.
3. **If you do not draw Social Security yet**, confirm how Medicare is billing you and set up automatic payment.
4. **If your income fell after a qualifying event**, file Form SSA-44 now instead of waiting for 2027 to start.
5. **Choose the plan for networks, travel and the out-of-pocket cap**, since the surcharge is the same whichever way you go.
