**Medicare uses your income from two years earlier. If you turn 65 in 2027, Social Security sets your first Part B and Part D premiums from your 2025 federal tax return, the year you turned 63.** Its own manual says it uses "the most recent tax information that IRS is able to provide," generally from two years before the premium year and never more than three. For {{year}}, a surcharge starts above {{irmaa.singleStart}} of modified adjusted gross income for a single filer and {{irmaa.jointStart}} for a married couple filing jointly. The 2027 lines have not been published yet.

That answer is the same in Peoria as anywhere else; IRMAA is federal. What is local is who it lands on. In north Peoria, in Vistancia, Blackstone and the 85383 corridor, I meet a lot of people whose last full working year falls in exactly the wrong spot: a final bonus, a deferred-compensation payout, or the sale of a house in Illinois or Minnesota before the move. All of it sits on the return that prices their first year of Medicare.

The same two-year lookback then runs every year for the rest of your life. Your second year on Medicare uses the year you turned 64, your third uses the year you turned 65, and so on. That is why the most expensive Medicare years for a high earner are often the first two: they are priced from working years.

## What "income" means here

IRMAA does not look at your paycheck or your net worth. It uses modified adjusted gross income, which Social Security defines as two numbers off your Form 1040 added together:

- **Adjusted gross income** (line 11), plus
- **Tax-exempt interest** (line 2a).

The second line surprises people. Municipal bond interest that never shows up in your tax bill still counts toward IRMAA. In the households I sit with in Trilogy at Vistancia and around the Lake Pleasant Parkway and Happy Valley Road corner, a muni ladder is common, and it gets added back.

Things that commonly push a 63-year-old's return over the line:

- The last year of salary, plus a bonus or severance paid on the way out
- Deferred compensation or a pension lump sum paid at separation
- Stock options exercised or restricted stock vesting before retirement
- A gain on selling the old house above the home-sale exclusion (see [does selling my house affect Medicare premiums](/blog/does-selling-my-house-affect-medicare-premiums/))
- A Roth conversion done "while income is low" that turned out not to be low enough (see [does a Roth conversion affect Medicare premiums](/blog/does-a-roth-conversion-affect-medicare-premiums/))

## When Social Security actually checks, and the extension trap

This is the part almost no one explains. Social Security does not look at your income on your birthday. Under its operating manual, it asks the IRS at one of two points:

- **If you are already drawing Social Security**, about two months before your Medicare starts.
- **If you are not drawing Social Security yet**, which is most people turning 65 now, when you actively sign up for Part B, or when Medicare tells Social Security that you have joined a drug plan or an Advantage plan with drug coverage.

At that point the IRS sends the most recent year it has on file. If it does not have the two-years-back return, it sends the three-years-back return, but only if that one is over the threshold. When Social Security uses the older year, it corrects the premium once the newer return becomes available.

Here is where that matters for a January–March 2027 birthday. Many households with complicated returns, such as K-1s, a home sale or option exercises, file on extension, and a 2025 return filed in October 2026 may not be on the IRS's file yet when you sign up for Part B this fall. If your 2024 income was over the line, you can be billed on 2024 first and adjusted later. If 2024 was your peak earning year, expect a first letter that looks too high, and expect a correction afterwards rather than having to fight it. Keep a copy of the filed 2025 return where you can find it.

## What IRMAA attaches to, whichever plan you pick

Two separate surcharges, two separate triggers:

- **Part B surcharge.** Owed once you have Part B. In {{year}}, the first tier takes the Part B premium from {{partB.premium}} to {{irmaa.t1.partB}} a month, and the top tier reaches {{irmaa.t5.partB}}.
- **Part D surcharge.** Owed once you have any Medicare drug coverage, whether that is a stand-alone drug plan alongside a supplement or a Medicare Advantage plan with drugs built in. In {{year}} it runs from {{irmaa.t1.partD}} to {{irmaa.t5.partD}} a month on top of the plan's own premium.

Choosing an Advantage plan does not make either one go away. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, 67 of the 85 Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage offered in Maricopa County for 2027 carry a $0 premium, counting special needs plans (39 of the 45 general plans). A higher-income Peoria household on one of them still pays the Part B surcharge and, if the plan includes drugs, the Part D surcharge. A Part B giveback plan reduces the standard premium only. It never touches the income-related part.

Surcharges are per person. A married couple filing jointly is measured against the joint threshold, and each spouse who has Medicare pays their own surcharge. A younger spouse who is not on Medicare yet pays nothing until they enroll.

## If your income has already dropped: you can ask in year one

There is a myth that new enrollees have to sit through their first year of IRMAA. They do not. Social Security's manual lets any beneficiary ask for a new initial determination using a more recent year if a life-changing event caused a significant drop in income, and it puts no waiting period on that request. Stopping work is the event that applies to most people turning 65. You give an estimate of the current year's income, usually on Form SSA-44, and attest that it is true.

What does not qualify on its own: a home sale, a Roth conversion, or a one-time capital gain that simply will not repeat. Those are real income in the year they happened, and the surcharge for that year usually stands.

The mechanics of the form, the eight events and the evidence are in [how do I appeal IRMAA after retirement](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/). For the brackets themselves, see [IRMAA explained](/learn/irmaa-explained/), and the [IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) will show which tier a given income lands in.

## Turning 65 in 2028? Your year closes December 31

If you were born in 1963, the return that will price your first Medicare premium is your 2026 return, and 2026 has about six weeks left in it.

That is the only point in this whole process where a decision can still change the outcome. A Roth conversion, a large IRA distribution, harvesting a gain or taking a bonus in December rather than January all land on the 2026 return. I am not the person to tell you whether any of those is right for your household. A conversion that triggers one year of IRMAA can still be the right call over twenty years. But your tax advisor should see the Medicare consequence before the year closes, not after the letter arrives.

If you turn 65 in 2027, the year is already closed. The useful work now is getting the enrollment right and knowing what the first letter is likely to say.

## What to do this month if you turn 65 in early 2027

1. **Find your 2025 return** and look at line 11 and line 2a. Add them. That is the number Social Security will most likely use.
2. **If you filed on extension**, note whether 2024 was higher. If it was, expect a high first bill and a later correction.
3. **If you have stopped working, or will by January**, plan to file for a new initial determination as soon as your premium notice arrives. Don't wait a year.
4. **Line up your enrollment dates** with the [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/). Signing up for Part B is what triggers the income check for most people who are not drawing Social Security yet.
5. **If you are not drawing Social Security yet**, read [how to pay your Medicare premium if you're not on Social Security](/blog/how-do-i-pay-my-medicare-premium-if-im-not-on-social-security/). The first bill covers several months at once, and with a surcharge it is larger than people expect.
