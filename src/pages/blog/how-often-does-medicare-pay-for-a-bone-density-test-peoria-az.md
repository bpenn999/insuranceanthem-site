**Medicare Part B pays for a bone density test once every 24 months for people in five risk groups, and more often when a doctor documents a medical reason. If the doctor and the facility accept assignment, you pay nothing for the test: no Part B deductible and no {{partB.coinsurance}} coinsurance.** The test is usually a DEXA scan of the hip and spine, and the two-year limit is counted by calendar month, so a scan any day in March 2025 can be repeated from March 1, 2027.

That is the national rule, the same in Peoria as in any other county. Where people trip over it is in the details. One is the exact month the clock resets. Another is that a man needs a reason other than age. A third is that an Advantage plan wants the scan done in its network. In north Peoria, where a lot of Vistancia and Trilogy residents arrived from another state with their records in a folder somewhere, the most common problem I see is simply not knowing when the last scan was.

## Who Medicare covers for a bone density test

Federal regulation (42 CFR 410.31) lists five groups. You need to fit at least one, and your treating doctor or nurse practitioner has to order the test after evaluating you:

1. **A woman whose doctor determines she is estrogen-deficient and at clinical risk for osteoporosis**, based on her history and other findings. This is the door almost every woman past menopause walks through. Taking estrogen does not rule it out; CMS's manual says a woman on hormone therapy can still qualify if the doctor documents why.
2. **Anyone with vertebral abnormalities on an X-ray** that suggest osteoporosis, osteopenia or a vertebral fracture.
3. **Anyone on steroid therapy, or about to start it,** at an average of 5 mg of prednisone a day or more for more than three months.
4. **Anyone with primary hyperparathyroidism.**
5. **Anyone being monitored on an FDA-approved osteoporosis drug**, to see whether it is working. This one has to be a DEXA scan of the spine and hip, not a heel or wrist device.

Age alone is not on the list. Turning 65 does not make you eligible on its own, and that matters most for men. A 70-year-old man with no steroid history, no X-ray finding and no parathyroid diagnosis has no door to walk through, however sensible a baseline scan sounds.

## How the 24-month clock really works

Medicare's frequency rule is written as "at least 23 months have passed since the month the last covered measurement was performed." In practice that means the test is covered again from the first day of the same calendar month two years later. The day of the month does not matter.

Book a day early, in the 23rd month instead of the 24th, and the claim can be denied as too frequent. When a facility expects that, it should hand you an Advance Beneficiary Notice to sign before the scan, which tells you the bill may become yours. If you are handed one, that is your cue to ask the front desk for the date of your last scan and move the appointment.

**The two exceptions.** Medicare will pay sooner when the doctor writes down why. CMS gives two examples: monitoring someone on steroids for more than three months, and a confirmatory baseline DEXA when the first measurement was taken on a different type of machine (a heel ultrasound at a health fair, for instance). A confirmatory scan is not covered if the first one was already a DEXA.

## The records problem for people who moved to Peoria

The 24-month rule follows you, not your doctor. If you had a DEXA in Ohio or Washington in spring 2025 and Medicare paid for it, that scan counts against the clock in Arizona even though your new primary care office in 85383 has no record of it. Medicare's claims system knows the date. Your new doctor may not.

Two things help. First, your Medicare.gov account lists past claims, so you can find the date of your last bone density test before the appointment rather than after the denial. Second, ask the old imaging center to send the report itself. A follow-up DEXA is far more useful when the radiologist can compare it to the last one, and the comparison is the main reason to repeat the test in the first place.

According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, Maricopa County has 52 general Medicare Advantage plans for 2027, not counting special needs plans. Every one of them has to cover the bone density test on Medicare's terms, so the scan is not a reason to pick one plan over another. What differs is which imaging centers are in each plan's network and whether an HMO wants the order routed through your primary care doctor. If the scan is scheduled for January and you are changing plans on January 1, check that the facility is in the new plan's network, not the old one. Your doctor chooses the scan. Your plan's network decides where it can be done for $0.

## After the scan: when the result is osteoporosis

The test itself costs nothing. Treatment is where the bills start, and the drug is paid through a different part of Medicare depending on its form:

- **Pills taken at home**, such as weekly or monthly tablets, are Part D drugs. They are covered by your stand-alone drug plan or the drug coverage inside your Advantage plan, at whatever tier the formulary puts them.
- **An injection given in a doctor's office** is generally billed under Part B, so on Original Medicare you pay {{partB.coinsurance}} after the Part B deductible, and a Medigap plan picks up some or all of that share depending on the plan letter.
- **Medicare's home health injection benefit** is narrow. Part B covers an injectable osteoporosis drug given by a home health nurse for a woman who qualifies for home health care, has a fracture her doctor certifies is related to postmenopausal osteoporosis and cannot give herself the shot (and has no family member or caregiver able or willing to). The nurse visit costs nothing; the drug is up to {{partB.coinsurance}} after the deductible.

If you are already on one of these drugs, that is the fifth qualifying group above, and the follow-up DEXA to check whether it is working is covered on the same 24-month schedule, or sooner if your doctor documents a reason.

## A quick checklist before you book

- **Find the date of your last scan**, from your Medicare.gov claims or the old imaging center, and book in or after the same month two years on.
- **Make sure the order says why.** The diagnosis on the order is what places you in one of the five groups. For a man, that matters more than anything else.
- **Ask whether the facility accepts Medicare assignment**, or on an Advantage plan, whether it is in network for the plan you will have on the date of the scan.
- **Do not sign an Advance Beneficiary Notice without asking why it is there.** It usually means the date is early or the reason is missing, and both can be fixed before the scan rather than after.
