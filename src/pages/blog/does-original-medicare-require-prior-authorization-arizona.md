**Yes, for 12 specific outpatient services. Arizona is one of six states in a CMS pilot called WISeR, and since January 15, 2026, people on Original Medicare here have needed approval before Medicare pays for things like epidural steroid injections, knee arthroscopy for osteoarthritis, cervical fusion and implanted nerve stimulators.** Everything else in Original Medicare works the way it always has: no referrals, no network and no approval for an office visit, an MRI or a hospital stay. The pilot runs through December 31, 2031, and it does not touch Medicare Advantage, which has its own, much longer lists.

That answer matters most to a particular kind of Peoria retiree: the one who chose Original Medicare and a supplement precisely so nobody would stand between them and their surgeon. For almost everything, that is still how it works. For a back injection at a pain clinic near Arrowhead or a knee scope in Sun City, there is now a review step, and it is worth knowing what it is before you pick a 2027 plan.

## The 12 services on Arizona's list

Noridian, the Medicare contractor that processes claims for Arizona doctors, publishes the list for this state. Skin substitutes, which appear on the national version of the model, are not on Noridian's Arizona list.

CMS has said two more, deep brain stimulation and image-guided lumbar decompression for spinal stenosis, were pushed back to a future year, and that the list can grow. Inpatient-only procedures and emergency care are excluded, as is any service where waiting would put the patient at serious risk.

This sits alongside an older, national program. Since 2020, Original Medicare has also required approval for a handful of procedures done in hospital outpatient departments, such as eyelid surgery, vein ablation and some Botox injections. That one applies in every state; my post on [prior authorization for Botox](/blog/does-medicare-require-prior-authorization-for-botox-2027/) covers it.

## Who files it, and how long it takes

You do not file anything. The doctor's office or the facility does, either straight to the Arizona reviewer, a company called Zyter, or through Noridian, which forwards it within a day. According to Noridian:

- **A standard request is decided within three days,** and an expedited one within two when your health is at risk.
- **An approval is good for 120 days,** so a scheduled injection series can be covered by one request.
- **The office can skip the request,** and some do. Then Medicare holds the claim after the service and has it reviewed before paying. The care is the same either way. What changes is when anyone finds out whether Medicare will pay.
- **A "no" on the request cannot itself be appealed.** The doctor can ask for a peer-to-peer conversation with the reviewer or resubmit with more records. If the service goes ahead and the claim is denied, the ordinary Medicare appeal process applies.

The rule underneath does not change. CMS and Noridian both say WISeR does not alter what Medicare covers, only when the coverage rules are checked. And if the office expects Medicare to refuse, it has to give you an Advance Beneficiary Notice before the procedure. That signature is what makes you responsible for the bill, so read it rather than signing it with the rest of the clipboard.

## Why it shows up more in Arizona than elsewhere

KFF looked at how often traditional Medicare patients used the services on the WISeR list in 2024. Across the six states, about 207,500 people did. Arizona had the highest rate of the six, 43 of every 1,000 people on traditional Medicare, against 32 per 1,000 nationally.

That fits what I see in the Valley. Spine and joint care is a big part of retirement here, and the pain-management and orthopedic practices around Arrowhead, Sun City and north Peoria do a lot of exactly these procedures. If you have had an epidural steroid injection in the last few years, the odds are good you will meet this process again.

## Does a Medicare Supplement get you out of it?

No, and it never claimed to. A Medigap plan has no approval process of its own. It pays its share of whatever Original Medicare approves: under Plan G, everything after the {{partB.deductible}} Part B deductible in {{figYear}}. When Medicare approves a WISeR service, a Plan G member's bill for it is close to nothing. When Medicare does not, the supplement has nothing to pay against.

The real comparison is scale. Under Original Medicare in Arizona, the list is 12 services, it is published, and the reviewer has three days. A Medicare Advantage plan sets its own list, which typically reaches into imaging, specialist referrals, skilled nursing, home health and Part B drugs. KFF counted 52.8 million prior authorization requests to Advantage insurers in 2024, about 1.7 per enrollee, with 7.7% denied in full or in part. Only 11.5% of those denials were appealed, and 80.7% of the appeals were overturned at least partly.

So a supplement still means far fewer approvals. It no longer means none.

## Is WISeR going away in 2027?

Not as things stand. Congress has tried twice to stop it:

- **The Senate vote.** After the Government Accountability Office found in May 2026 that the model counts as a rule, Senate Democrats used the Congressional Review Act to try to cancel it. The Senate defeated that resolution on July 16, 2026.
- **The spending bill.** In June 2026 the House Appropriations Committee added language to its fiscal 2027 Labor-HHS bill that would bar CMS from spending money on WISeR. That bill has not become law, and the language still has to survive negotiations with the Senate.

Until one of those changes, plan as if the model will be in place for all of 2027. If it ends, Original Medicare simply goes back to its old, shorter list.

## What to weigh in north Peoria before December 7

A forced plan change, a new Medigap application or a spine procedure on the calendar all collide this time of year. A few questions sort them out:

1. **Is a procedure on the list already scheduled for January?** If you are on Original Medicare, ask the clinic whether it is sending the request or leaving the claim to be reviewed afterward. If you are moving to an Advantage plan on January 1, your new plan's own approval governs instead, and the request has to be made again.
2. **Is your Advantage plan ending December 31?** Several Maricopa plans are. If you are thinking about going back to Original Medicare with a supplement, the guaranteed-issue window that follows a plan's discontinuation is your best chance, because Arizona has no Medigap birthday rule. Outside that window the application is medically underwritten, and recent spine or joint treatment is the kind of history it asks about. [Can I switch from Medicare Advantage to Medigap in Arizona?](/blog/can-i-switch-from-medicare-advantage-to-medigap-in-arizona/) walks through when that door is open.
3. **Do you live in one state and see doctors in another?** WISeR follows where the service is given. A winter resident in Vistancia or Trilogy who has a procedure done back home in a state outside the pilot will not see it there.
4. **Which clinic, and how does it bill?** From the Lake Pleasant Parkway and Happy Valley corridor, pain management usually means a drive to Arrowhead or Sun City. Practices that do these procedures every week know the WISeR routine. A smaller office may not, and that is worth a phone call.

## The short version

- Original Medicare in Arizona now needs prior approval for 12 outpatient services, mostly spine, joint, nerve-stimulator and bladder procedures, through a CMS pilot that runs through 2031.
- The doctor files, decisions take about three days, and an approval lasts 120 days.
- A Medigap plan follows Medicare's decision. It adds no approvals, and it does not override a denial.
- Medicare Advantage is outside the pilot and uses its own, far broader lists.
- Congress has not stopped the model, so it is part of the 2027 picture.
