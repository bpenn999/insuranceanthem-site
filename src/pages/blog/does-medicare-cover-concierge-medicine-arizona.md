**No. Medicare does not pay a concierge doctor's membership fee, in Arizona or anywhere else.** Medicare.gov puts it plainly: you pay all costs for non-covered services, including the membership fee for concierge care. What Medicare still pays depends on one thing, the doctor's Medicare status. If the concierge doctor still participates in Medicare, your covered visits, tests and wellness visit are billed to Medicare as usual. If the doctor has opted out, Medicare pays nothing for that doctor's care, and neither does a Medigap policy or a Medicare Advantage plan, except in an emergency.

So the fee is the part everyone asks about, and the opt-out is the part that decides the bill. The rest of this post is about telling the two apart before you sign anything.

## Why north Peoria retirees keep asking

Most of the people who ask me about concierge care live in [Peoria's](/service-area/peoria-az/) north end, in Vistancia, Trilogy and the 85383 corridor off Lake Pleasant Parkway. Many moved from another state, left behind a primary-care doctor they had seen for twenty years, and found that the new-patient wait at a Valley practice ran months. A practice that caps its patient list and answers the phone on a Saturday is appealing, especially when you have already been through one search for a doctor after a move.

The question I hear is almost always "will Medicare cover it?" The honest answer is that the yearly fee is never covered, but most of what happens inside the exam room can be, if the practice is set up the right way.

## The three ways a doctor can work with Medicare

The concierge practices most retirees come across are in the first group. They keep billing Medicare for the visit and charge a separate yearly fee for what Medicare does not pay for: longer appointments, same-day access, a direct line to the doctor. Medicare.gov notes that a doctor who accepts assignment cannot charge you extra for Medicare-covered services, so the fee has to be for something other than the covered visit itself.

The third group is the one to watch for. A doctor who opts out has filed an affidavit with Medicare, and the opt-out lasts at least two years and covers every Medicare patient they see. They cannot opt out for some patients and keep billing Medicare for others.

## What the opt-out list shows for Arizona

CMS publishes every opted-out provider in its Opt Out Affidavits dataset. I pulled the September 2026 release (last updated October 21, 2026) and counted the Arizona rows:

- **1,293 Arizona clinicians** are on the list. Most are not primary-care doctors. The largest groups are mental health counselors (245), oral surgeons (157) and clinical psychologists (146).
- **118 are primary-care physicians**, listed as family practice, internal medicine, general practice or geriatric medicine.
- **By city, Scottsdale has 305 listings and Peoria has 13.** Glendale shows 148, but 136 of those sit in one ZIP code, 85308 near Arrowhead, and most of them are oral surgeons and dentists rather than primary-care practices.

Two things follow from that. First, a doctor who has opted out of Medicare is the exception, not the rule, even among the Valley's concierge practices. Second, it is common enough in Scottsdale that a Peoria household following a friend's recommendation across town should check before the first appointment. A name on the list means Medicare will not pay that doctor, full stop.

## How Medigap and Medicare Advantage fit in

**With a Medicare Supplement,** a concierge doctor who participates in Medicare works like any other doctor. Medicare pays its share, your Medigap plan pays its share, and the fee is separate. On a Plan G you would owe the yearly Part B deductible ({{partB.deductible}} in {{year}}) and very little else for covered care. Plan G also covers the up-to-15% excess charge a non-participating doctor can add, which matters in Arizona because the state does not ban those charges. The [Plan G vs. Plan N calculator](/tools/plan-g-vs-plan-n/) shows the difference.

If the doctor has opted out, the Supplement is no help. Federal rules require the private contract to tell you, in writing, that Medigap plans do not pay for those services.

**With a Medicare Advantage plan,** the network comes first. Of the 52 Medicare Advantage plans CMS lists for Maricopa County in 2027 that are not special needs plans, 36 are HMOs or HMO-POS plans and 16 are PPOs, according to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files. An HMO pays nothing for routine care from a doctor outside its network, so a concierge doctor who isn't contracted with your plan means paying the fee and paying for the visits. A PPO may pay at its out-of-network rate. Federal rule [42 CFR §422.220](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/422.220) bars any Advantage plan from paying an opted-out doctor for basic benefits, with an exception for emergency or urgently needed care when no private contract was signed.

So the combination that keeps the cost predictable is a concierge doctor who participates in Medicare, paired with a Medigap plan, with the fee as a known line in the budget. Any other combination needs a closer look at the paperwork.

## What the private contract has to say

If a practice hands you a "private contract" rather than a membership agreement, the doctor has opted out. Federal rule [42 CFR §405.415](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/405.415) spells out what that contract must contain, and it is worth reading for those clauses alone. You agree to:

- **pay the doctor's charges in full** for every service, with no Medicare limit on what they bill;
- **not submit a claim to Medicare**, and not ask the doctor to submit one;
- **accept that Medicare will not pay**, even for services Medicare would normally cover;
- **understand that Medigap plans will not pay** either.

The contract also has to show the start and end dates of the doctor's two-year opt-out period, and it cannot be signed while you need emergency or urgent care.

## Questions to ask before you sign

Write these down and ask them on the phone, before the practice sends any paperwork:

1. **Does the doctor participate in Medicare, accept Medicare without assignment, or opt out?** Get the answer in writing.
2. **Exactly what does the yearly fee cover?** A list of services, not a description of the experience.
3. **Which lab, imaging and hospital systems does the practice send patients to?** In the northwest Valley that usually means Banner, HonorHealth or Abrazo facilities around Arrowhead and Sun City, and each of those bills Medicare or your plan separately from the practice.
4. **If I have a Medicare Advantage plan, which plans is the doctor contracted with for 2027?** Ask by plan name and plan ID, since contracts change every January.
5. **What happens to the fee if I leave or move partway through the year?** Refund terms vary by practice.

Whether the fee counts as a deductible medical expense on your tax return is a question for your tax advisor, not for me or the practice.

Open enrollment runs through December 7, 2026. If you are weighing a concierge doctor for 2027, decide that before you pick a plan, not after. A concierge doctor who bills Medicare usually pairs most easily with Original Medicare and a Supplement, because there is no network to match.
