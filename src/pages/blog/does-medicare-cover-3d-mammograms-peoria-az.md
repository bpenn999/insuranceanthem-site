**Yes. Medicare covers 3D mammograms, which CMS calls digital breast tomosynthesis, as an add-on to the standard mammogram. A 3D screening mammogram is covered once every 12 months for women 40 and older, and you pay nothing if the facility accepts assignment: no Part B deductible and no coinsurance. A 3D diagnostic mammogram is covered whenever it is medically necessary, and you pay {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount after the Part B deductible ({{partB.deductible}} in {{year}}).**

That is the national rule from Medicare.gov and the federal regulation, and it applies the same way at an imaging center off Lake Pleasant Parkway as anywhere else in the country. The 3D part does not change the price; the word in front of "mammogram" does. Almost every surprise bill I hear about from women in Peoria comes from a visit that started as a screening and ended up partly diagnostic.

## Screening or diagnostic: the word that sets the bill

Federal regulation, 42 CFR 410.34, draws the line in two sentences:

- **A screening mammogram** is for a woman *without* signs or symptoms of breast disease, to catch cancer early. No doctor's order is required for Medicare to pay, though most facilities still want the name of your doctor so the report has somewhere to go.
- **A diagnostic mammogram** is for a man *or* a woman *with* signs or symptoms of breast disease, a personal history of breast cancer, or a personal history of a biopsy-proven benign breast condition. It has to be ordered by a medical doctor or doctor of osteopathy.

Screening is a preventive service, so Medicare waives the deductible and coinsurance. Diagnostic is ordinary Part B care, so it does not. Nothing about the machine, the room or the technologist tells you which one you are getting. The order and the reason on it decide.

## How Medicare pays for the 3D part

CMS's claims manual (Chapter 18, §20.2.1) sets up 3D imaging as a second line on the same claim, never a stand-alone test:

- **3D screening** is billed with code 77063, and it is paid only alongside the regular screening mammogram, code 77067. Medicare's manual is explicit that there is no Part B deductible or coinsurance for screening mammography, so the 3D add-on rides on the same $0.
- **3D diagnostic** is billed with code G0279, paid only alongside a diagnostic mammogram, codes 77065 or 77066, with the usual Part B share.

One consequence matters for scheduling. If the regular screening fails Medicare's frequency check because it is too soon, CMS's manual tells the claims system to reject *both* lines, the 2D and the 3D together. The federal rule is that at least 11 full months must pass after the month of your last screening. A mammogram any day in October 2026 can be repeated any day from October 1, 2027, regardless of the date in October. Book it for late September and the whole claim can come back denied.

## The callback that turns a $0 visit into a bill

Here is the situation that produces most of the calls. You go in for your yearly 3D screening. The radiologist reads it while you are still there, sees an area worth a closer look, and orders extra views. CMS allows this: under §20.6 of the claims manual, a radiologist interpreting a screening mammogram can order and read additional films during the same visit, and **Medicare pays for both the screening and the diagnostic mammogram**. The facility flags the second one with a modifier so CMS can track it.

What that means on your statement:

1. **The screening portion stays at $0.** It was a screening when you walked in, and it is billed that way.
2. **The extra views are diagnostic.** They carry the Part B deductible and {{partB.coinsurance}} coinsurance on Original Medicare. If you have not yet met the deductible that year, a large share of the first diagnostic bill can be deductible.
3. **Anything after that is diagnostic too**: a breast ultrasound the same afternoon, a return visit for magnified views, a biopsy. Each is its own Part B claim.

Most callbacks end with a normal result. The point is not to worry about the callback, but to know the second statement is coming and why it is not an error.

## After breast cancer, every mammogram is diagnostic

The definition above includes "a personal history of breast cancer." That puts a woman who has finished treatment, even years ago, in the diagnostic category for her yearly mammogram, with no symptoms at all. In practice she pays the Part B share every year on Original Medicare, or the plan's diagnostic imaging copay on an Advantage plan, while her neighbor with no history pays nothing.

Facilities do not all code survivors' follow-up the same way, so ask when you book: "Will this be billed as screening or diagnostic?" If it is diagnostic, that is one more reason a Medigap plan or an Advantage plan's out-of-pocket limit matters for someone with a cancer history. Our post on [Medicare and cancer treatment in Peoria](/blog/does-medicare-cover-cancer-treatment-peoria-az/) covers the bigger costs that come with an active diagnosis.

## Dense breasts and the extra ultrasound

Since September 10, 2024, an FDA rule under the Mammography Quality Standards Act has required every mammography facility in the country to tell you, in the plain-language summary of your results, whether your breast tissue is dense, with set wording that dense tissue can hide cancer and that other imaging may help. If you have had a mammogram since then, your results letter carried that paragraph.

The FDA rule requires the notice. It does not create a Medicare benefit. **Medicare's screening benefit is the mammogram itself.** There is no separate screening benefit for a breast ultrasound or MRI, so when a doctor orders one because of dense tissue, it is paid as a diagnostic test: deductible and {{partB.coinsurance}} on Original Medicare, the plan's copay on Advantage. Ask your doctor whether the extra test is worth it for you. That is a medical question, not a coverage one, but it helps to know the bill will not be $0.

**On a Maricopa County Advantage plan**, the screening line is the same everywhere. Federal rule 42 CFR 422.100(k) bars an Advantage plan from charging any deductible, copay or coinsurance for an in-network Medicare-covered preventive service, and CMS's 2027 landscape files list 52 general Advantage plans in Maricopa County for next year, every one bound by it. What varies from plan to plan is the diagnostic row: the copay for diagnostic imaging, whether an MRI needs prior authorization, and which imaging centers in Peoria, Glendale and Sun City are in the network. Those numbers are in each plan's Evidence of Coverage, not on the landscape file, and they are worth reading if you have a cancer history or a callback in your past.

## The Maricopa number behind the reminder

The CDC's PLACES estimates put mammography use among Maricopa County women aged 50 to 74 at **75.0%** (2022 data), meaning a mammogram within the past two years. Put the other way, roughly one woman in four in that age group was not screened in the window, even though for most of them the test would have cost nothing. In north Peoria the reason is often a move: a Vistancia or Trilogy newcomer whose last mammogram was in Illinois or Washington, whose new doctor in 85383 has no report on file, and who loses track of the date.

The 12-month clock follows you across state lines, the way every Medicare frequency limit does, because Medicare's claims system holds the date of your last paid screening. Your Medicare.gov account lists it under past claims. Having the old images sent to the new facility matters just as much: radiologists compare this year's pictures with earlier ones, and a comparison is one of the things that keeps a harmless spot from becoming a callback.

## Five questions for the imaging center before you go

1. **"Do you accept Medicare assignment?"** On Original Medicare, that is what makes the screening $0. On an Advantage plan, ask instead whether they are in network for the plan you will have *on the day of the visit*. If you are switching plans for January 1, 2027, a January appointment needs the new plan's network.
2. **"Is the 3D part included in the screening?"** At a facility that offers tomosynthesis to Medicare patients it should be billed as the 77063 add-on, at no cost to you for a screening.
3. **"Will this be billed as screening or diagnostic?"** This matters most if you have had breast cancer, a benign biopsy, or a symptom you mentioned to your doctor.
4. **"When was my last screening?"** If they cannot tell you, check Medicare.gov. A visit before the 12-month mark can get the whole claim denied.
5. **"If I need extra views, will you do them today?"** Doing them the same day saves a second trip, and you will know the second bill is coming.
