**Yes, Medicare covers podiatry when it is medically necessary. Part B pays 80% of the Medicare-approved amount for a podiatrist to diagnose and treat a foot injury or disease, and you pay {{partB.coinsurance}} after the {{partB.deductible}} deductible.** Routine nail trimming and callus care are the exception: Medicare excludes them unless a documented condition, most often diabetes with nerve or circulation damage, makes it risky for anyone but a professional to do them.

That second sentence is where most of the confusion lives. A neighbor in Trilogy at Vistancia gets her toenails trimmed every couple of months and Medicare pays; her husband goes to the same office and gets a bill. Nobody made a mistake. She has diabetic neuropathy on her chart and he does not, and Medicare draws its line exactly there.

## The covered list and the excluded list

Medicare's own policy manual splits foot care into two piles. The covered pile is anything that treats a problem. The excluded pile is upkeep.

One wrinkle worth knowing: routine care becomes covered when it is part of treating something else. If the podiatrist trims a nail because it sits over an infection or an ulcer they are treating that day, the trim rides along with the covered treatment.

## When toenail trimming is covered

Medicare makes three exceptions to the routine-care exclusion, and each one has its own paperwork.

**A systemic condition that puts your feet at risk.** The manual's reasoning is practical: if diabetes, peripheral artery disease or a neuropathy has left you with poor circulation or numb feet, a nick from home nail clippers can turn into an ulcer. The conditions that most commonly qualify include diabetes, hardening of the arteries in the legs, chronic thrombophlebitis, and peripheral neuropathy linked to diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cancer, multiple sclerosis, certain drugs, alcohol or vitamin deficiency.

For most of those conditions there is a second requirement: you have to be **under the active care of an MD or DO** who documents the condition. In Arizona that means your primary care doctor or endocrinologist, not just the podiatrist, has seen you for it recently. Contractors generally look for a visit within the six months before the foot care.

**Findings in the foot itself.** Medicare's contractors presume the care is necessary when the podiatrist documents certain signs. A single non-traumatic amputation counts on its own. So do two findings such as missing foot pulses or advanced skin and nail changes, or one of those plus two symptoms such as claudication (leg pain when walking), cold feet, swelling, burning or tingling. The podiatrist records these on every claim, which is why your first visit is longer than the follow-ups.

**Thick, fungal toenails.** Treating mycotic nails can be covered even without a systemic disease, if the podiatrist documents the fungus and that the thickened nail causes marked trouble walking, pain or a secondary infection. Cosmetic discoloration alone does not qualify.

How often covered trimming can repeat is set by the regional Medicare contractor, not by Medicare.gov. Arizona falls under Noridian's JF region, and its rules set a minimum gap between covered routine visits. If your podiatrist's office asks you to wait a few more days before booking, that is usually why.

## The diabetic foot exam every six months

People with diabetes get a separate benefit. If you have a documented diagnosis of **diabetic sensory neuropathy with loss of protective sensation**, meaning you can no longer feel small cuts, burns or pressure, Medicare covers a foot evaluation **no more often than every six months**. The condition is that you have not seen a foot care specialist for some other reason in between.

The visit covers:

- your history
- an inspection of the whole foot, including between the toes
- a test of protective sensation, usually with a thin nylon filament
- a check of foot structure, circulation and skin
- whether you need special footwear
- education on daily foot care

Treatment during that visit, such as caring for a small wound, debriding corns and calluses, or trimming and debriding nails, is part of the same benefit. The diagnosis has to be established and written down first, and the primary care doctor is expected to have ruled out other causes of the neuropathy.

## Diabetic shoes and inserts

Medicare excludes most shoes and foot supports, but therapeutic shoes for diabetics are written into the law as an exception. If you have diabetes and severe diabetes-related foot disease, Part B covers, each calendar year:

- **either** one pair of custom-molded shoes with inserts **or** one pair of extra-depth shoes, and
- two or three additional pairs of inserts, depending on which shoe you chose.

Three people have to be involved. The doctor treating your diabetes certifies the need. A podiatrist or other qualified doctor writes the order. A podiatrist, orthotist, pedorthist or other qualified supplier fits and supplies them. All of them have to be enrolled in Medicare, and the supplier should accept assignment, or it can charge you more than the {{partB.coinsurance}} coinsurance.

Because the benefit resets on January 1, someone who qualifies and has not used it this year has until December 31 to order 2026's pair.

## What you pay in Peoria

On Original Medicare, a covered podiatry visit works like any other Part B service. You pay the first {{partB.deductible}} of Part B costs in the year, then {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount. In a hospital outpatient clinic there can be a facility copayment too. If the podiatrist suggests something Medicare does not cover, or wants to see you more often than Medicare allows, the office should ask you to sign an Advance Beneficiary Notice before the service. Read it: it means you will pay the full bill if Medicare says no.

A **Medicare Supplement** picks up most of the rest. Plan G covers the Part B coinsurance in full, leaving only the annual deductible. Plan N covers it too, with up to a $20 copay for some office visits. Neither plan turns routine nail care into a covered service, though: a supplement pays its share only of what Medicare approves.

A **Medicare Advantage plan** has to cover everything above. Two things differ:

- **Routine foot care as an extra.** Some plans in Maricopa County add a set number of routine podiatry visits a year on top of Medicare's rules. This is a plan benefit, not Medicare's, so it changes from plan to plan and year to year.
- **The route to the podiatrist.** Many HMOs want a referral from your primary care doctor first. I wrote up how that works plan type by plan type in [the referral post for Peoria](/blog/do-i-need-a-referral-medicare-advantage-peoria-az/).

## Why feet matter more in the Valley

Two things about living in north Peoria make this more than a paperwork question.

First, **summer pavement**. Concrete, asphalt and pool decks in the Valley get hot enough in June through September to burn skin quickly. For someone with neuropathy who cannot feel heat, a barefoot walk to the mailbox or around the pool at a Vistancia or Blackstone home can cause a burn they do not notice until it is a wound. That is exactly the patient the six-month exam and the shoe benefit were written for.

Second, **the drive to the specialist**. Podiatry offices are spread along the Loop 101 corridor, around Arrowhead and toward Sun City. For someone in the 85383 corridor off Lake Pleasant Parkway, the closest in-network podiatrist on one plan can be a short hop, and on another it can be across the Peoria–Glendale line. If you see a podiatrist regularly, put that practice's name on the list you check against every plan, the same way you would a cardiologist.

## What to check before December 7

Open enrollment runs to December 7, and coverage changes on January 1, 2027. If foot care is part of your year, these are the questions to settle now:

1. **Is your podiatrist in the 2027 network?** Networks are set per plan per year. Call the podiatry office and ask which 2027 Medicare Advantage plans they will accept, not just which carriers.
2. **Is your plan ending?** Several Maricopa County plans were discontinued for 2027. If yours is one of them, ask the podiatrist whether the plans you are considering need a new referral or prior authorization. The [discontinued-plan guide for Peoria](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/) has the dates.
3. **Does the routine foot care benefit change?** If you rely on a plan's extra routine visits, compare the 2027 Annual Notice of Change against this year's. The [Annual Notice of Change post](/blog/medicare-annual-notice-of-change-letter-2027/) shows where to look.
4. **Is your diabetic diagnosis on paper?** If you have diabetes and numbness in your feet but have never had it written down as loss of protective sensation, ask your primary care doctor about it. Without that, the six-month exam and covered trimming are not available, whatever plan you pick.
