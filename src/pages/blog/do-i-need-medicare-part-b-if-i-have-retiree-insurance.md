**Yes, almost always. Retiree insurance is not a substitute for Medicare Part B, and it does not let you delay Part B without a penalty.** Once you are 65 and retired, Medicare pays first and the retiree plan pays second, which means the plan is built on the assumption that Part B is already there. Skip it and the plan can refuse the share Medicare would have paid, and the late-enrollment penalty is 10% of the premium for every full 12 months you went without. The real exception is a federal retiree keeping a federal employee plan.

I get this question in north Peoria more than anywhere else I work. A lot of the people who have moved into Vistancia and the 85383 corridor over the past few years arrived with a retiree plan from a career in Illinois, Michigan, Minnesota or Washington: a manufacturer, a utility, a school district, a union trust. It is good coverage, they have had it for years, and the standard Part B premium of {{partB.premium}} a month in {{year}} looks like money spent on something they already have. It is not the same thing, and the difference shows up on the first large claim.

## Why does retiree coverage not let me skip Part B?

Medicare lets you put off Part B without a penalty in one situation: you or your spouse are **still working**, and the health plan comes from that current job. Medicare.gov is direct about what falls outside it. Its working-past-65 page says that if your coverage is not available to everyone at the company, and it gives retiree coverage as the example, you should sign up for Medicare when you turn 65 to avoid the monthly Part B late-enrollment penalty.

Retiree coverage fails that test however generous it is, because it comes from a job that has ended. That is the whole distinction, and it is the same reason [COBRA does not protect you](/blog/can-i-keep-cobra-instead-of-medicare-at-65/) either. If you are still on the payroll at 65, you are in a different conversation, and I wrote that one up separately: [do I have to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I am still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/)

## Is retiree insurance primary or secondary to Medicare?

Secondary. Medicare.gov's who-pays-first rules put it in one line for a retired person with group coverage from a former employer: Medicare pays first, and the group health plan pays second. The 20-employee rule that people half-remember only applies to someone actively working. Once you have retired, the size of the company stops mattering.

Paying second has a practical meaning. On a doctor's bill, Part B normally pays 80% of the approved amount after the {{partB.deductible}} annual deductible, and the retiree plan works on what is left. If you never enrolled in Part B, there is no first payment for the plan to follow. Medicare.gov's wording is that retiree coverage "might not pay your medical costs during any period when you were eligible for Medicare" and did not sign up, and that you may need both Part A and Part B to get full benefits from the plan.

So the usual outcome of skipping Part B is not that the retiree plan quietly steps up and pays everything. It is that the plan pays its 20% and you are handed the other 80%.

## What does skipping Part B cost if I guess wrong?

Two things, and the second lasts longer than the first.

**A gap.** There is no Special Enrollment Period for someone who simply had a retiree plan. If you pass your seven-month [Initial Enrollment Period](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) without enrolling, the next door is the General Enrollment Period, January 1 through March 31, with Part B starting the month after you sign up. Someone who realizes the mistake in May is looking at most of a year with a plan that is paying second to coverage that does not exist.

**A permanent surcharge.** The Part B late-enrollment penalty is 10% of the standard premium for each full 12 months you were eligible and not enrolled. Two years late is 20% more, and it stays on the premium for as long as you have Part B. It is also recalculated each year against the new standard premium, so it grows with it. The mechanics, and the separate Part D penalty, are laid out in the [late-enrollment penalties explainer](/learn/late-enrollment-penalties/).

The rule above is Medicare's side. Each kind of retiree plan then adds its own, and this is where it pays to read the plan document rather than a neighbor's experience.

A few of those rows deserve a sentence more.

**Arizona public retirees.** If your pension is from the Arizona State Retirement System, its retiree health program tells members who reach 65 and become eligible for Medicare to enroll in Parts A and B first and choose a Medicare-age plan after that. The retirement systems of other states work much the same way, and they tend to run on their own short enrollment deadlines. Call the retirement system three or four months before your birthday, not the week of it.

**Federal retirees.** This is the one group for whom Part B is a real choice. A Federal Employees Health Benefits plan continues to cover a retiree who declines Part B, so the question is arithmetic: the premium, plus any income surcharge, against what your particular plan waives or pays back when Medicare is primary. Some federal plans make Part B worth having and some do not. The penalty still applies if you decline and change your mind later.

**Postal retirees.** The newer Postal Service Health Benefits program is stricter than the federal one. Annuitants who retired after January 1, 2025 are generally required to enroll in Part B once they are entitled to Part A, and so are their covered family members. Those who retired on or before that date, and employees who were already 64 by then, are among the exceptions. If you are postal, do not borrow a federal neighbor's answer.

**Military retirees.** TRICARE For Life does not work without Part B. I covered it in [do I need Part B if I have TRICARE For Life?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-part-b-if-i-have-tricare-for-life/)

## Can I lose my retiree coverage by joining a Medicare plan?

Yes, and this is the mistake I most want people to avoid in October and November.

Many retiree plans turn into a group Medicare plan at 65, either a group Medicare Advantage plan or a supplement the employer arranges, with drug coverage attached. You can generally be in only one Medicare Advantage plan or one Part D plan at a time. So when a retiree answers a fall mailer and enrolls in an individual plan sold in Maricopa County, Medicare moves them out of the group plan automatically. Medicare.gov's advice is to find out first whether you could lose your retiree coverage by joining a plan the employer does not offer.

Often you can, and often it is one-way. Plenty of employers do not let a retiree back in after leaving, and a spouse covered under the same retiree plan can lose coverage on the same day. The Annual Enrollment Period runs October 15 through December 7, the mail is heavy, and Maricopa County has one of the longest plan menus in the country. A $0-premium plan on that menu still has copays, a network and an out-of-pocket maximum, and it may be replacing an employer benefit you cannot get back.

None of this means the retiree plan is always the better choice. Employers raise retiree contributions, narrow the benefits, or end the plan, and some retiree plans cost more than comparable coverage bought on your own. It means the comparison should be made on purpose, with the plan document open, rather than by returning a postcard.

## What about a Medigap policy alongside a retiree plan?

Usually you do not need one. A retiree plan paying second to Medicare is already doing the job a [Medicare Supplement](/medicare-supplement/) would do, and paying for both is rarely useful.

There is a timing detail worth knowing, though. Your six-month Medigap open enrollment starts the month you are 65 and enrolled in Part B, and it runs whether or not you use it. If you rely on a retiree plan through those six months, that window closes behind you. Arizona has no annual birthday window to reopen it, so [the six months matter](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/).

The protection that remains is narrower. If a retiree plan that pays after Medicare later ends, federal rules give you a guaranteed right to buy certain Medigap plans without health questions, and you generally have to apply no later than 63 days after the coverage ends. That right is tied to the plan ending. Whether it reaches someone who drops a retiree plan by choice is a question to settle with the insurer before you cancel anything, not after.

For a household with the means to choose, this is the real decision at 65: stay with an employer benefit that could change at the employer's discretion, or use the one window in which a supplement cannot turn you down.

## What about the income surcharge on Part B?

Higher-income retirees pay more than the standard premium through IRMAA, which is based on the tax return from two years earlier. For {{year}} it begins above {{irmaa.singleStart}} for a single filer and {{irmaa.jointStart}} for a couple filing jointly, and the [IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) will show where you land.

For most retiree plans the surcharge changes nothing, because Part B is required either way. For a federal retiree it is part of the arithmetic. And if the income that put you in a higher bracket came from your last working year, retirement is one of the life-changing events Social Security accepts for a reduction request. I walk through that in [how to appeal IRMAA after retirement](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/). Your tax advisor should be part of that conversation.

## What should I do before I turn 65?

Four things, in this order.

1. **Call the benefits administrator, not the insurance company.** Ask three questions. What happens to this plan when I turn 65? Does it require Part A and Part B? If I enroll in a Medicare plan you do not offer, do I lose this coverage, does my spouse, and can either of us come back?
2. **Get the answers in writing.** A plan document or an email will do. People change jobs in benefits departments, and you may need this in ten years.
3. **Enroll in Part A and Part B in the three months before your birthday month**, so both start the first day of that month and the retiree plan has something to pay second to. If you are already drawing Social Security, enrollment is automatic and the card arrives about three months ahead. Do not send back the form declining Part B.
4. **Ask for the drug coverage notice.** If the retiree plan's prescription coverage is creditable, you do not need a separate Part D plan, and joining one can cost you the retiree plan. Keep the notice each year.
