**Usually yes. Medicare.gov's own guidance is that if you are self-employed, or your health insurance is not employer group health plan coverage, you should sign up for Medicare when you turn 65 to avoid the Part B late enrollment penalty.** That penalty is 10 percent of the standard Part B premium, currently {{partB.premium}} a month, for each full 12-month period you went without Part B when you could have had it, and it lasts as long as you have Part B. The one common exception is a business owner who is covered by their own company's group health plan when that company has 20 or more employees.

That is the standard answer, and for most self-employed people it settles the matter. Where I see people in north Peoria go wrong is in the middle: the consultant who bought a policy on their own and assumes it works like an employer plan, or the owner of an eight-person firm who assumes the company plan lets them wait. Neither does. Here is how to tell which group you are in, and the tax point most self-employed people find out about a year too late.

## Which kind of self-employed are you?

The question Medicare asks is not whether you work. It is whether your health coverage comes from a group health plan, and whether that plan belongs to an employer big enough to pay first.

Premium-free Part A is a separate decision. Most people take it at 65 regardless, because it costs nothing. The exception is anyone still putting money into a health savings account through their business, for the reason in [the HSA post](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/).

## The $400 test almost nobody has heard of

CMS's definition of current employment, at 42 CFR 411.104, specifically includes "the employer (including a self-employed person)". So an owner on their own company's plan can be covered by virtue of current employment, which is what the Part B Special Enrollment Period depends on.

There is a catch written into the same rule. A self-employed person only counts as having group coverage by virtue of current employment in a given tax year **if their net earnings from work for that business in the previous tax year were at least the amount in section 211(b)(2) of the Social Security Act**. That section sets the self-employment income floor at **$400**.

For a working owner that is a low bar. It matters for the owner who has stepped back: the business is still open, the group plan still covers them, but they drew no self-employment earnings from it last year, or the business lost money. On paper they have a company plan. For Medicare's purposes, that year it may not be coverage based on current employment, and the delay they were counting on is not protected. If that sounds like you, get your accountant's figure for last year's net earnings from the business before you decide anything.

## How CMS counts to 20

The size test is also more specific than "about 20 people". Under 42 CFR 411.170, an employer has 20 or more employees only if it had 20 or more **on each working day in each of 20 or more calendar weeks**, in the current calendar year or the one before.

Two things follow for small owners:

- **A seasonal business can fall under the line.** A landscaping, pool or resort-season business that staffs up to 25 for the winter months and runs at 12 the rest of the year may not reach 20 weeks at 20 or more. Count the weeks, not the peak.
- **A shrinking business can stay over it for a while.** Because the preceding calendar year counts, a firm that had 20 or more through last year still meets the test this year, even after cutting back.

Your broker or the plan's administrator should be able to tell you which side of the line the plan treats you as on. Get that answer in writing. The general rules for people on a large employer's plan are in [do I need Medicare at 65 if I'm still working](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/); this post is about the owner's side of them.

## A policy you bought yourself is not employer coverage

This is the most common mistake. A policy you bought on your own, through a broker or directly from an insurer, can be a good policy. It is still not group coverage based on current employment, so:

- **It does not give you a Special Enrollment Period later.** If you skip Part B at 65 and decide at 67 that you want it, you wait for the General Enrollment Period, January through March, and you pay the penalty from then on. The [late enrollment penalty calculator](/tools/late-enrollment-penalty-calculator/) shows what that adds up to.
- **Its drug coverage may or may not count as creditable for Part D.** Ask the insurer in writing. If it is not creditable, the Part D penalty clock starts when your enrollment window closes.
- **Do not cancel it until Medicare is in force.** Coverage starts on the first of a month. Line up the end date of the old policy with the start date of Medicare so there is no gap. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) shows your exact dates.

## The deduction most self-employed retirees miss

Once you are on Medicare, and still have self-employment income, the premiums may be deductible. The IRS instructions for Form 7206, the self-employed health insurance deduction, say that "Medicare premiums you voluntarily pay to obtain insurance in your name" can be used to figure it. IRS Chief Counsel advice issued in 2012 (CCA 201228037) said premiums for all parts of Medicare qualify, including those for a spouse.

The limits, from the same instructions:

- **It cannot exceed your net earnings from the business.** A year with no profit, or a loss, produces no deduction from that business.
- **It is not allowed for any month you were eligible for a subsidized employer plan**, and that includes your spouse's. If your spouse still works and could have put you on their company plan, those months are out, whether or not you took the coverage.
- **It does not reduce self-employment tax.** It reduces income tax only.

What there is to deduct depends on the route you choose. Under CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, **46 of the 52 general Medicare Advantage plans in Maricopa County charge no plan premium of their own**; the other six run from $16.80 to $127 a month. So on most Advantage plans the deductible amount is mostly your Part B premium, plus any income-related surcharge. On Original Medicare with a supplement and a drug plan, the supplement and drug-plan premiums count too, which makes the deduction larger. That is not a reason to choose one route over the other, but it is worth knowing when you compare costs.

This is tax education, not tax advice. Your tax advisor decides how it applies to your return.

## How the deduction connects to IRMAA

Social Security sets the income-related surcharge, IRMAA, from your modified adjusted gross income: adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest, generally from the tax return two years before the premium year. The self-employed health insurance deduction is taken before adjusted gross income. So a larger deduction lowers the very figure Social Security uses. For a household near one of the bracket lines, which start at {{irmaa.singleStart}} for a single filer and {{irmaa.jointStart}} for a joint return, that can matter.

The other IRMAA point for business owners runs the opposite way. If you sell the business, wind it down or cut your hours, the return Social Security is reading still shows the old income. Stopping or reducing work is one of the life-changing events on form SSA-44, the request to use a more recent year. The steps are in [how do I appeal IRMAA after retirement](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/). The sale itself can raise your income in the year it closes, the same problem covered in [does selling my house affect Medicare premiums](/blog/does-selling-my-house-affect-medicare-premiums/). Plan that timing with your tax advisor.

## What to do before your 65th birthday month

1. **Name your coverage.** Is it a policy you bought yourself, your own company's group plan, or your spouse's employer plan?
2. **If it is a company plan, get the headcount answer in writing,** counted the way 42 CFR 411.170 counts it, and confirm your net earnings from the business last year were at least $400.
3. **If you cannot delay, enroll in Part A and Part B during your seven-month window,** and time the end of your current policy to the day Medicare starts.
4. **Start your six-month Medigap window on purpose.** It opens the month your Part B starts, and in Arizona it is the main time a supplement carrier must accept you without health questions, because Arizona has no birthday rule. Later chances come only from specific guaranteed-issue situations.
5. **Tell your tax advisor you are now on Medicare,** so the premiums are considered for the self-employed health insurance deduction on next year's return.
