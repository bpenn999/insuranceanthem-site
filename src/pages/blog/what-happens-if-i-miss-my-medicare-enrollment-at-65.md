**If you miss your seven-month Medicare window at 65 and had no job-based coverage to protect you, you sign up during the General Enrollment Period, January 1 through March 31, 2027, and Part B starts the first of the month after you file. Whether that costs you a penalty depends on one count: the full months between the end of your first window and the end of the month you enroll. Eleven months or fewer, and Part B carries no surcharge at all.** Twelve or more, and it is 10% of the standard premium for every full year, for as long as you keep Part B.

Most of the "I think I missed it" calls I get from Peoria come in about now, because open enrollment is on the news and people realize the October ads are not talking to them. Usually it is someone in Vistancia or Trilogy who retired from a small firm, moved here, or simply assumed Medicare would show up the way it does for neighbors who already draw Social Security. It didn't. The good news, for a surprising number of them, is that the timing this winter works in their favor if they act in January rather than March.

## First, make sure you actually missed it

Before planning around a penalty, rule out the three situations where you haven't missed anything.

- **Your window is still open.** The Initial Enrollment Period is the three months before your 65th birthday month, that month, and the three months after. If you turned 65 in July 2026 or later, it is still running today. File now; do not wait for January.
- **You had group coverage through current work.** If you, or a spouse whose plan covered you, were on an employer plan based on active employment with 20 or more employees, Part B could wait, and you get eight months after the job or the coverage ends. COBRA and retiree plans do not count. [Do I need Medicare at 65 if I'm still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/) walks through the test.
- **Something outside your control stopped you.** Since 2023, federal rules have created Special Enrollment Periods for exceptional conditions under [42 CFR §406.27](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/406.27): a declared emergency or disaster, an employer or health plan that gave you wrong information about your coverage, release from incarceration, loss of Medicaid, and other cases Medicare reviews one by one. Each lasts at least six months, and coverage starts the month after you enroll. If a benefits office told you in writing that you could wait and it was wrong, keep that letter. It matters.

If none of those fit, the General Enrollment Period is your door.

## When your coverage would start

Since January 2023, a General Enrollment Period signup starts the month after you file, not the following July as it used to. That makes the month you file worth choosing on purpose.

- **File in January 2027:** Part B starts February 1.
- **File in February 2027:** Part B starts March 1.
- **File in March 2027:** Part B starts April 1.

Any day you are uninsured is a day a fall on a trail at Lake Pleasant or an ER visit at Banner Thunderbird is entirely yours to pay. January is the earliest the door opens, and for some birthdays it is also the cheaper month, as the next section shows.

## The penalty count, and why January can matter

The Part B late-enrollment surcharge is set by [42 CFR §408.24(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/408.24): for anyone enrolling from 2023 on, Social Security counts the months from the close of your Initial Enrollment Period to **the close of the month you enroll**. Every full 12 months in that count adds 10% of the standard Part B premium, which is {{partB.premium}} a month in {{year}}. A count of 11 adds nothing.

Two things about that table. First, a 10% surcharge is not a one-time fee. It is recalculated against each year's standard premium and added every month you have Part B. Second, if your 65th birthday was in late 2025, the difference between filing on January 5 and March 5 can be a lifetime surcharge. ## Part A and drug coverage run on different clocks

**Premium-free Part A has no late penalty.** If you have 40 quarters of work, yours or a spouse's, you can apply for Part A at any time, and [42 CFR §406.6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/406.6) lets it reach back up to six months. The one catch: if you have been putting money into a health savings account, backdated Part A turns those months into excess contributions. Stop the HSA deposits before you file, and see [Can I contribute to an HSA while on Medicare?](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) for the look-back.

**Part D is the clock still running right now.** The drug penalty adds 1% of the national base premium for every month you go without creditable drug coverage after a 63-day grace, and it does not wait for the General Enrollment Period. Anyone with Part A or Part B can join a stand-alone drug plan during this fall's open enrollment, October 15 through December 7, for coverage January 1, 2027. If Part A is already on file, or you file for it this week, a drug plan chosen before December 7 stops that count a month before your Part B could even start. The 2027 Arizona drug-plan line-up is in [Medicare Part D plans in Arizona for 2027](/blog/medicare-part-d-plans-arizona-2027/).

**Medicare Advantage is narrower.** It needs both Part A and Part B in force, and the windows for joining right after a General Enrollment Period signup are short and depend on your exact dates. If an Advantage plan is what you want, ask before you file rather than after.

## The Medigap window still opens, even late

This is the part that surprises people. Your six-month Medigap open enrollment does not start at 65. Medicare.gov defines it as the six months that begin the first month you are **65 or older and enrolled in Part B**. A late Part B start still opens it.

So a north Peoria retiree who files in January gets Part B on February 1, 2027, and a Medigap window from February 1 through July 31, 2027. During those months an insurer has to sell any Medigap plan it offers in Arizona and cannot charge more for your health. There is one catch for a late enrollee: if you had no creditable health coverage in the six months before, the policy can make you wait up to six months before it pays for a pre-existing condition. Arizona has no birthday rule and no annual do-over, so this is the one guaranteed window you get. [Plan G vs. Plan N in Arizona](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/) is the decision most people make in it.

## Your plan for this winter

1. **This week:** confirm which situation you are in, using your 65th birthday month and the first section above. If you had any employer coverage after 65, get the dates in writing.
2. **By December 7:** if you have, or file for, premium-free Part A, choose a Part D plan so drug coverage starts January 1.
3. **Before January 1:** gather what Social Security will ask for. [What documents do I need to apply for Medicare?](/blog/what-documents-do-i-need-to-apply-for-medicare/) has the list.
4. **January 2027, early:** file for Part B through Social Security, online or by phone, and ask for the earliest start.
5. **February onward:** use the six-month Medigap window while it is open.
