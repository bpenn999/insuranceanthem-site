**No law makes a veteran sign up for Medicare, but VA itself tells you to enroll in Part A and Part B at 65, and the reason is the penalty clock.** VA health care is not coverage from a current employer, so it does not let you delay Part B and sign up later for free. Skip it at 65, add it at 70, and you pay an extra 10% of the standard premium for each full year you went without it, for as long as you have Part B. Part D is the opposite: VA drug coverage counts as creditable, so that part can wait. Medicare does not pay a cent at a VA clinic, and VA does not pay for a non-VA hospital it did not authorize. Having both is how you avoid finding out which one you needed.

I meet a lot of veterans in [Peoria](/service-area/peoria-az/), more of them every year in Vistancia and along the Lake Pleasant Parkway and Happy Valley corridor, plenty who came to Arizona from somewhere colder with a VA card in their wallet and a working assumption that it covers everything. For a lot of their care it does. The trouble is the care that happens somewhere other than the VA, and in the north end of 85383 that is most of the emergencies.

## Two programs that never send each other a bill

The rule that drives everything else is simple. Medicare does not pay for services furnished by a federal provider, which is what a VA medical center or clinic is, and VA's own guidance says it does not bill Medicare. It goes further for Advantage members: VA says a Medicare Advantage plan will not pay for care received at a VA health facility either.

Run that the other way. When you walk into a doctor's office or emergency room that is not part of VA, VA pays only when it sent you there through an authorized referral, or when the emergency rules (below) are met. Otherwise the bill goes to whatever insurance you have outside VA, which at 65 is Medicare or nothing.

VA puts it this way: you choose which benefits to use each time you receive care. There is no coordination and no crossover claim, the way there is with TRICARE For Life (a different program for military retirees, which I covered in [Do I need Medicare Part B if I have TRICARE For Life?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-part-b-if-i-have-tricare-for-life/), where Part B is mandatory).

## Why Part B is the decision that matters

Part A is premium-free for anyone with 40 quarters of work, so for most veterans it costs nothing to take and there is no reason to refuse it. Part B is the one with a bill attached: {{partB.premium}} a month in {{year}}, more on higher incomes. That is where veterans hesitate, and where the rules are least forgiving.

Medicare lets you delay Part B without penalty only while you, or your spouse, have group coverage through **current employment**. VA health care is not that. Neither is a military pension, retiree coverage or COBRA. So a veteran who retires, declines Part B because "I have the VA," and changes their mind five years later finds three things waiting:

- **A waiting window.** Without a Special Enrollment Period, the only door is the General Enrollment Period, January 1 to March 31, with coverage starting the month after you sign up.
- **A permanent surcharge.** Medicare adds 10% of the standard premium for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B and did not. Five years late is roughly 50% more, every month, for as long as you have Part B. The [late enrollment penalty calculator](/tools/late-enrollment-penalty-calculator/) works out your number.
- **No Medigap guarantee.** Your six-month Medigap open enrollment starts with Part B, but buying a policy years later in Arizona means health questions, because Arizona has no birthday-month switching rule to fall back on.

VA says the same thing in its own words: missing the deadline can mean permanent Part B penalties, and if you cancel Part B later you cannot get it back until the following January. I go through what to do if the birthday month has already passed in [What happens if I miss my Medicare enrollment at 65?](/blog/what-happens-if-i-miss-my-medicare-enrollment-at-65/)

## The north Peoria map: where your nearest VA actually is

This is the part that makes the decision concrete. VA Phoenix lists these as its west-side and central sites:

- **Northwest VA Clinic**, 13985 West Grand Avenue in Surprise. For most of 85383, this is the closest VA clinic.
- **The Thunderbird clinic** on North 25th Avenue in north Phoenix.
- **Southwest VA Clinic**, West Thomas Road in Phoenix.
- **Carl T. Hayden VA Medical Center**, East Indian School Road in central Phoenix. This is the hospital.

VA Phoenix lists no site in Peoria, Glendale, Sun City or Anthem. From Vistancia or Blackstone, the VA hospital is a long drive down the I-17 or Loop 101. The community hospitals around Arrowhead and Sun City are much closer, and an ambulance takes you to the nearest appropriate emergency room, not the one your card is for.

VA does cover emergency care at a non-VA emergency room in some cases, but with conditions. VA has to be notified within 72 hours. No VA facility can be feasibly available. The situation has to be one a prudent layperson would treat as an emergency. And for a condition that is not service-connected, VA pays only after other insurance has been used, and only if you had VA care in the prior 24 months. Urgent care clinics do not qualify. If the emergency becomes an admission and a week of rehab, Medicare Part A and Part B are what pay for the parts VA does not.

## Part D is the part you can safely leave

Federal rules list VA prescription coverage as creditable coverage, coverage at least as good as standard Part D. That means you can skip a Part D plan at 65 and enroll later with no late penalty, as long as you sign up within 63 days of losing VA drug coverage.

VA's 2026 medication copays for priority groups 2 through 8 run $5, $8 or $11 for a 30-day supply depending on tier, capped at $700 a year, and veterans in priority group 1 pay none. For a veteran getting all of their prescriptions through VA, a Part D premium usually buys little.

The case for adding Part D is convenience. VA generally fills prescriptions written by its own providers or by community doctors it authorized, so a prescription from a cardiologist at Arrowhead you chose yourself usually has to be rewritten by a VA provider first. A Part D plan lets you take it to a Peoria pharmacy that afternoon. If most of your doctors are outside VA, that convenience is worth pricing, and the [Arizona Part D plan landscape for 2027](/blog/medicare-part-d-plans-arizona-2027/) changed enough this fall to check before you buy.

## Medigap or Medicare Advantage, alongside VA

Once you have Part A and Part B, the question is how to cover what Medicare leaves at non-VA providers.

**A Medicare Supplement** pays the deductibles and the 20% coinsurance that Part B leaves, at any doctor in the country who takes Medicare, with no network. Two details are specific to veterans. VA cannot bill Medicare, but it can bill a Medicare supplemental policy for care that is not service-connected, and VA says that payment can offset part or all of your VA copay. And your guaranteed-issue window, the six months starting the month you are 65 and in Part B, is the only time an insurer must sell you a policy without health questions. I laid out how [Plan G and Plan N compare in Arizona](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/) for anyone weighing the two.

**A Medicare Advantage plan** covers non-VA care through a network. VA notes that some veterans keep one for dental, vision or hearing benefits VA may not provide in their priority group. Some Maricopa plans also reduce the Part B premium, which I covered in [Is the Medicare Part B giveback worth it?](/blog/is-the-medicare-part-b-giveback-worth-it-peoria-az/) To keep the plan you keep paying Part B. And the plan pays nothing at the VA, so it works best for a veteran who wants extras the VA does not provide, not for one who gets most of their care at a VA clinic.

Which suits you depends on how much of your care already happens at the VA, and that is the first question I ask any veteran who books a call.

## Two veterans, two different answers

**The VA-first veteran.** A retired machinist in the older 85345 part of Peoria, rated 70% service-connected, priority group 1, all primary care and prescriptions at the Thunderbird clinic. VA covers nearly everything at no copay. Part A, free. Part B, still yes, because it is what pays if he collapses at a grandchild's game in Vistancia and the ambulance goes to the nearest hospital. Part D, no. Medigap or Advantage is a smaller decision, sized to how much non-VA care he expects.

**The VA-occasionally veteran.** A retired executive in Trilogy at Vistancia who enrolled in VA years ago for a hearing claim and otherwise uses a concierge internist and the Arrowhead specialists. VA is a backstop for her, not her health plan. She needs Part A, Part B and either a Medigap policy or an Advantage plan built around the doctors she actually sees, and possibly Part D. Her Part B premium will likely carry an IRMAA surcharge on her retirement income; [the IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) shows where she lands, and VA eligibility does nothing to change it.

## What to do in the months before 65

1. **Find your Initial Enrollment Period.** It runs from three months before the month you turn 65 to three months after. Sign up early in it so Part B starts on your birthday month. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) puts the dates on a calendar.
2. **Take Part A.** For nearly everyone it costs nothing.
3. **Take Part B**, unless you or your spouse are still covered through current work at an employer. VA coverage is not that exception.
4. **Confirm your VA priority group and copays** before deciding on Part D. If VA fills everything you take, Part D can wait without penalty.
5. **Decide Medigap or Advantage inside your six-month Medigap window**, not after it.
6. **Keep both cards in your wallet**, and tell whoever answers the phone at a non-VA hospital that you have Medicare.
