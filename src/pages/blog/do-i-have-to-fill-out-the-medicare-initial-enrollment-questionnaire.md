**Yes, fill it out. The Medicare Initial Enrollment Questionnaire (IEQ) is a real form, and it decides who pays your bills first.** Medicare sends it about three months before your coverage starts, and your answers go to the Benefits Coordination & Recovery Center (BCRC), the contractor CMS uses to track other insurance. It asks whether you have coverage that should pay before Medicare: an employer group plan through your job or a family member's, workers' compensation, or liability insurance. It takes a few minutes, it does not enroll you in anything, and skipping it does not cost you your Medicare. What it costs you is clean claims.

The rule the questionnaire is really asking about is short. CMS's [Medicare Secondary Payer page](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coordination-benefits-recovery/overview/secondary-payer) says that at 65 or older, if you are covered by a group health plan through **current** employment, yours or your spouse's, the employer plan pays first when the employer has **20 or more employees**, and Medicare pays first when it has fewer than 20. Retiree coverage pays after Medicare. Get that one line right on the form and most of the trouble goes away.

## What the questionnaire actually asks

The IEQ is not a health questionnaire and has nothing to do with Medigap underwriting. It asks about other coverage, one type at a time:

- **Group health coverage through current employment**, yours or a spouse's, and whether it continues once you have Medicare.
- **Whether that employer has 20 or more employees.** This is the question that decides the payment order.
- **Retiree coverage** from a former employer or union.
- **Workers' compensation or liability coverage**, for example an open claim from a car accident or a work injury.

You can answer on paper or online through your Medicare.gov account. Medicare's older guidance said the online version opens 90 days before your Medicare start date and closes 180 days after it, so don't let the letter sit through the holidays.

Your answers set up the coordination-of-benefits record that every Medicare claim is checked against. When a doctor in Peoria bills Medicare, the system looks at that record to see whether another plan should have paid first. If the record says no other insurance, Medicare pays as primary. If your employer plan should have paid first, Medicare can recover that money later, and that is the letter nobody wants in their second year.

## Is it a scam? How to tell the real one

The real questionnaire is about other insurance and nothing else. It does not ask for a bank account, a credit card or a premium payment, and it does not ask you to "claim" or "elect" a benefit.

What arrives in the same months, and looks official, is marketing. The usual pattern is a pull-tab or window envelope saying you have an "initial election" or a "Medicare benefit update" and asking for your signature, age and phone number. That card goes to a lead buyer, and the calls start a week later. Our post on [how to stop Medicare junk mail](/blog/how-to-stop-medicare-junk-mail/) walks through the opt-outs and the envelopes you must not throw away.

If you are unsure about a letter, don't use any phone number printed on it. Call the BCRC directly at 1-855-798-2627 (TTY 1-855-797-2627), Monday through Friday, 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. Eastern. That number is published on both [CMS's contacts page](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coordination-benefits-recovery/overview/contacts) and [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/supplements-other-insurance/how-medicare-works-with-other-insurance). In Arizona, Eastern time means 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. in winter and 5 a.m. to 5 p.m. in summer, because Arizona never changes its clocks.

The employer count is the one people guess at. It is the total number of employees, not the number in your office or on the plan, and it includes every location. A spouse who works for a national company out of a small Peoria branch is almost always on a 20-plus plan. Someone who runs a small practice off Lake Pleasant Parkway with six staff is not, and Medicare pays first for them even though they are still working. If you are not sure, ask HR the number before you answer the questionnaire, not after a claim bounces.

## Your answer is the lowest-ranked source on the file

This part almost nobody tells you. In a July 2025 training deck on its "hierarchy rules," CMS explains how the BCRC decides between conflicting reports about the same coverage. Sources are ranked in five tiers. The BCRC's own analysts are first. The BCRC call center and 1-800-MEDICARE are second. Insurers reporting under Section 111, along with Medicare Advantage plans, are third. Employer data-sharing agreements and the IRS/SSA/CMS employer data match are fourth. **Your answer on the IEQ is in the fifth tier, with "all others."**

In practice that means your questionnaire answer starts your record, but a later report from your employer's insurer can overwrite it, and your IEQ answer cannot overwrite theirs. CMS says the most common conflict is the end date of the employer coverage. That is exactly the field that goes wrong when someone retires, the employer's insurer is slow to report the end date, and Medicare keeps treating the old plan as primary.

Two things follow. Answer the IEQ accurately, because it is still the first record. And when your coverage changes later, call the BCRC instead of mailing a correction. A phone update is recorded at a higher tier than the questionnaire.

## What happens if you skip it or get it wrong

Your Medicare stays in place. There is no penalty for not returning the IEQ. The problems show up in claims:

- **Medicare pays first when your employer plan should have.** CMS later finds the employer coverage through its data match and recovers the payment. The doctor rebills the employer plan, and you get confusing statements from both sides.
- **The employer plan expects Medicare to pay first and Medicare's record says otherwise.** Claims stall between the two. This is common for spouses on a small employer's plan.
- **A retiree plan denies its share because Part B is missing.** Medicare.gov says plainly that when retiree coverage is the secondary payer, you may need Part B before it will pay.

Medicare.gov notes that if the primary insurer does not pay promptly, usually within 120 days, the provider may bill Medicare, and Medicare may make a "conditional payment" and recover it later. That arrangement keeps the doctor paid. It does not keep your paperwork simple.

## Three things the IEQ is not

It is easy to mix the IEQ up with the other Medicare mail that arrives in the same months.

1. **It is not your Part B decision.** Signing up for Part B, or turning it down while you keep working, goes through Social Security. The questionnaire only records what you have.
2. **It is not the CMS-L564.** That is the employer's "Request for Employment Information" form, used later to prove you had job-based coverage when you [sign up for Part B after 65](/blog/how-do-i-sign-up-for-medicare-part-b-when-i-retire-after-65/). Different form, different time.
3. **It is not a plan choice.** Nothing on it picks a Medicare Supplement, an Advantage plan or a drug plan. Those decisions are made separately and run on their own deadlines. For a Supplement, the clock that matters is the six-month Medigap open enrollment that starts with your Part B, and Arizona has no birthday rule to give you a second chance later.

## How this plays out in north Peoria

The questionnaire matters most for the households that dominate the 85383 corridor around Vistancia and Trilogy at Vistancia: one spouse turning 65 and retired, the other still working, often for a large employer with a Valley office. In that house the right answers are different for each spouse. The working spouse's plan pays first for both of them if the employer has 20 or more employees. When that spouse retires, the order flips, and that change needs a call to the BCRC the same month.

The other common case on the [Peoria](/service-area/peoria-az/) side is the recent arrival still on a former employer's retiree plan from another state. They answer the questionnaire with "yes, I have other coverage" and assume the retiree plan pays first. It does not. Medicare pays first, and the retiree plan expects Part B to be in place.
