**Yes. A green card holder can get Medicare at 65.** If you or your spouse have 40 quarters of work in jobs that paid Medicare tax, you get premium-free Part A and can sign up for Part B exactly as a citizen would, in the seven months around your 65th birthday. If you do not have 40 quarters, federal law lets a lawful permanent resident aged 65 or older enroll once they have lived in the United States continuously for the five years immediately before applying, and then Part A comes with a monthly premium.

Two other points matter this year. First, a federal law signed July 4, 2025 narrowed who can have Medicare at all, and green card holders are on the list of people who keep it. Second, the five-year rule means the Medicare calendar for someone who arrived later in life does not start at their birthday. I get this question from north Peoria families more than you might guess: a mother or father who moved here from abroad to live with a son or daughter in Vistancia or off Lake Pleasant Parkway, now in their late sixties, and nobody is sure when Medicare begins.

## The two ways in

Which route applies depends on work history, not on how long you have had the card.

**The work route.** Forty quarters is roughly ten years of covered work. Someone who came to the Valley in their forties, worked, and got a green card is usually on this route and may never hear about a five-year rule. A spouse's record counts too: a wife who never worked here can get premium-free Part A on her husband's record once he is 62 or older. The regulations put the residence test on the people buying Part A or taking Part B without it, not on people who earned Part A, which is how 42 CFR §407.10 is laid out.

**The residence route.** This is the route for a parent who came over late. The federal statute, [42 U.S.C. §1395o](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1395o), opens Part B to "an alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence who has resided in the United States continuously during the 5 years immediately preceding the month in which he applies." The same test governs buying Part A under [42 CFR §406.20](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/406.20). Long trips back home can raise a question about whether residence was continuous, so keep travel records handy when you apply.

## What the 2025 law changed, and why it is not about you

Section 71201 of the 2025 reconciliation law, [Public Law 119-21](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ21/html/PLAW-119publ21.htm), added a new section to the Social Security Act. Medicare is now limited to:

1. U.S. citizens and nationals,
2. lawful permanent residents,
3. Cuban and Haitian entrants, and
4. people living here lawfully under a Compact of Free Association.

People outside those four groups who were already on Medicare on July 4, 2025 keep it for 18 months. The law sets the end at 18 months after enactment, which is **January 4, 2027**, and told Social Security to review its rolls within a year and send each affected person a notice. In practice that mostly means people here lawfully in other categories, for example a refugee or asylee who never adjusted to permanent residence, even when they earned the work credits. When Medicare ends, any Medicare Advantage or Part D plan built on it ends with it.

If you hold a green card, none of that removes your eligibility. The one practical step: if you became a permanent resident after you first enrolled in Medicare, make sure Social Security has your current status on file. If a termination letter arrives anyway, answer it with your card. Don't ignore it.

## When your sign-up window opens

For most green card holders on the work route, nothing is different. The seven-month window starts three months before your 65th birthday month. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) will lay out your own months.

On the residence route, the window is set by the month you first become eligible, which may be years after 65. [42 CFR §407.14](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/407.14) defines the initial enrollment period as the seven months beginning three months before "the month an individual first meets the eligibility requirements." So a parent who settled in 85383 at 67 gets a seven-month window around the month they complete five continuous years of residence, at 72.

That later start has two good consequences:

- **No Part B penalty for the years you could not enroll.** The late penalty counts only the months you could have had Part B and went without it. Sign up inside your own seven-month window and there is nothing to count. Our [late enrollment penalties explainer](/learn/late-enrollment-penalties/) has the full formula.
- **Your Medigap window starts then, too.** The six-month Medigap open enrollment period begins the first month you have Part B and are 65 or older, according to [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/medigap/ready-to-buy/when). For a 72-year-old who just qualified, that is the one stretch when a company selling Medicare Supplement plans must offer them any plan it sells, without health questions. Arizona has no birthday rule and no later do-over, so mark that date.

## The Part A decision on the residence route

Without 40 quarters, you can take Part B alone and skip the Part A premium. Plenty of families consider it when they first see {{partA.premiumIfUninsured}} a month. Before choosing, know that Part B alone leaves hospital stays uncovered, and that both Medicare Advantage and Medicare Supplement plans require Part A and Part B. Our post on [buying Part A without 40 quarters](/blog/do-i-have-to-buy-medicare-part-a-without-40-quarters/) runs through that choice, including the rule that buying Part A means also taking Part B.

Quarters can still be earned after 65. A parent who works part-time in Peoria and pays Medicare tax adds quarters, and reaching 30 or 40 lowers or removes the Part A premium.

## Once you have Parts A and B in Peoria

From there a green card holder shops the same menu as every other Maricopa County resident. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files there are 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage in Maricopa County for {{nextYear}}, and Medicare Supplement plans are sold to any eligible resident during their open enrollment. What changes the choice for these families is usually not status but geography. A parent who spends months a year back in their home country needs to know that Medicare generally does not pay for care outside the United States, so an HMO built around Arrowhead-area doctors and a Supplement with a foreign travel emergency benefit are very different fits.
