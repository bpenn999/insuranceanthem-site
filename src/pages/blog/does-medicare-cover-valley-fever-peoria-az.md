**Yes. Medicare covers Valley fever the way it covers any other infection. Part B pays for the doctor visits, the blood test and the chest X-ray, Part D pays for the antifungal prescription, and Part A pays for a hospital stay if it comes to that. On Original Medicare in {{figYear}}, the Valley fever blood test usually costs you nothing. Office visits and imaging cost {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount after the {{partB.deductible}} Part B deductible.**

There's no separate Valley fever benefit and no special rule. What makes it worth a post is the local picture. Valley fever is concentrated in this county, it falls hardest on people in their late sixties and older, and its season peaks right now. If you live in Peoria, or you've just arrived for the winter, it's one of the few Medicare questions that depends on where you live.

## Why this is a Maricopa County question

According to the Arizona Department of Health Services' 2024 annual report, Arizona recorded **14,763 cases of Valley fever** in 2024. **10,697 of them, about 72%, were in Maricopa County**, and 93% came from Maricopa, Pima and Pinal together. (ADHS notes that the 2024 count is partly inflated by a faulty batch of test kits used from January to March.)

The age figures matter more for anyone on Medicare. The same report breaks cases down by age:

Add the three oldest rows together and **5,450 of the 14,763 cases, about 37%, were people 65 or older**, at roughly twice the statewide rate. The 75 to 84 group had the highest rate of any age. Maricopa County's public health department lists people 60 and older among those more likely to get severe Valley fever, along with people with diabetes or a weakened immune system.

Timing matters too. A CDC analysis of Arizona cases published in February found that the state's peak usually runs **October through January**, and the share of each year's cases peaks in **November**. Maricopa County's own count shows 6,918 cases reported from January 1 through October 31 this year, and the busiest stretch is still ahead.

## What Medicare pays for, step by step

The CDC says about 40% of people who breathe in the spores get symptoms. They usually start one to four weeks later and look like a chest cold or pneumonia that won't clear up: cough, fever, fatigue, night sweats, aching joints, sometimes a rash. A Valley fever case usually goes through the same few steps, and each one falls under a different part of Medicare.

A few notes on that table.

**The blood test.** The CDC recommends starting with an antibody test (an EIA, usually confirmed with an immunodiffusion or complement-fixation test). These are standard clinical lab tests. Medicare.gov says that on Original Medicare you usually pay nothing for a Medicare-approved diagnostic lab test your doctor orders. Plenty of pneumonia cases in the Valley are never tested, so if a chest infection is dragging on, it's fair to ask your doctor whether a Valley fever test makes sense.

**The prescription.** The CDC says Valley fever is usually treated with fluconazole or amphotericin B, and that some infections clear up without any antifungal. When fluconazole is prescribed, the CDC says a course often runs **three to six months**, and some people with severe or disseminated infections stay on it for life. Generic fluconazole is a Part D drug. I checked two {{figYear}} Maricopa County Medicare Advantage formularies in CMS's Part D files, and both list generic fluconazole on a generic tier (tier 1 on one, tier 2 on the other) with no prior authorization, no step therapy and no quantity limit. That is a spot check, not a guarantee for every plan, so look the drug up on your own plan's formulary. Some plans apply the drug deductible to generic tiers and some don't, and over a six-month course that difference shows up. The [{{partD.cap}} Part D cap](/blog/medicare-part-d-out-of-pocket-cap-2027/) limits what you can spend on covered drugs in a year.

**The hospital.** ADHS counted 986 hospitalizations with a primary diagnosis of Valley fever in 2024, with a median hospital charge of $64,552. That's the hospital's billed charge, not what you pay. On Original Medicare, your share of an inpatient stay is the Part A deductible of {{partA.deductible}} per benefit period. The intravenous antifungal, the lab work and the room are all included for the first 60 days. A [Medigap](/medicare-supplement/) plan such as Plan G picks up that deductible and the {{partB.coinsurance}} on the doctor bills. Our post on the [Part A deductible](/blog/medicare-part-a-deductible-every-hospital-stay-2027/) explains how benefit periods work if you're admitted more than once.

## On a Medicare Advantage plan

A [Medicare Advantage](/medicare-advantage/) plan has to cover everything above, but it sets its own copays and usually limits you to its network. Three things matter for Valley fever:

- **The lab.** Network plans usually send blood work to a contracted lab. If urgent care draws the sample, it normally goes to the right lab, but it's worth confirming before you have a follow-up test done somewhere else.
- **The specialist.** Persistent or complicated cases often end up with an infectious disease or pulmonary specialist. On an HMO that usually takes a referral from your primary care doctor first. See [do I need a referral on a Medicare Advantage plan](/blog/do-i-need-a-referral-medicare-advantage-peoria-az/).
- **The out-of-pocket maximum.** Your plan's in-network maximum limits what a hospital stay plus months of follow-up visits can cost you in a year. In Maricopa County those maximums vary by thousands of dollars from plan to plan.

The Annual Enrollment Period closes **December 7**, eleven days from today. If you're already being treated for Valley fever, or you have a lung condition that makes it riskier, check your specialist's network status and the out-of-pocket maximum before you choose a {{nextYear}} plan.

## Winter visitors with an out-of-state plan

Many people who move to north Peoria for the winter, to Vistancia or the Lake Pleasant Parkway corridor, are still on the plan they bought back home. If that plan is Original Medicare, with or without a Medigap policy, it works at any Arizona doctor or lab that takes Medicare, and Valley fever care is no different.

A Medicare Advantage plan from another state is different. Outside its service area it generally covers only emergency and urgent care. The first urgent care visit for a bad cough is probably fine. The follow-up blood test, the repeat chest X-ray a month later and the specialist visits usually aren't urgent care, and an out-of-area HMO may not pay for them. [Can I use my Medicare Advantage plan in another state](/blog/can-i-use-my-medicare-advantage-plan-in-another-state/) covers the details.

The opposite also happens. Because symptoms can take one to four weeks to appear, someone can breathe in the spores in Peoria in March and get sick after flying home to Minnesota. A doctor there may not think of Valley fever. If you've spent time in Arizona, say so.

## The Peoria angle

Peoria's newer north end, from Vistancia and Blackstone up toward Lake Pleasant, is still being built out of open desert. The CDC links Valley fever to spores in disturbed soil, and dust from construction or a monsoon haboob can carry them. Even so, this is a whole-Valley risk. Living in 85383 instead of the older 85345 core doesn't make you safer or less safe, and the [Peoria service area page](/service-area/peoria-az/) has more on the rest of the north–south split. What does vary across the Peoria, Glendale and Sun City ZIP seam is which Medicare Advantage networks reach which specialists. If you're on an HMO and live near that line, [Medicare in Glendale](/service-area/glendale-az/) covers the other side.

## The short version

- **Medicare covers Valley fever** under the normal rules. There's no separate benefit.
- **The blood test is usually $0** on Original Medicare. Visits and chest X-rays cost {{partB.coinsurance}} after the {{partB.deductible}} deductible in {{figYear}}.
- **Fluconazole is a Part D drug.** It's a generic, but courses run for months, so your plan's deductible and tier matter.
- **A hospital stay costs the {{partA.deductible}} Part A deductible** on Original Medicare. On an Advantage plan you pay its copays, up to its out-of-pocket maximum.
- **People 65 and older account for about 37% of Arizona's cases**, and Maricopa County for about 72%.
- **Out-of-state Advantage plans** usually won't pay for follow-up care here that isn't urgent.
