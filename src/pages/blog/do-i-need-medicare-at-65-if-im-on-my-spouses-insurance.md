**Not yet, if your spouse is still working and the employer has 20 or more employees. That plan pays first, and you can wait to sign up for Part B with no late penalty. Your sign-up window then opens when your spouse stops working or the coverage ends, whichever comes first, and it lasts eight months.** If the employer has fewer than 20 employees, the answer flips: Medicare pays first at 65, and you should enroll on time.

That is Medicare's standard rule, and it has one feature people miss. When you are the *dependent* on the plan, every date that matters is set by your spouse's job, not by your birthday. You might be 67 when the window opens, or 72. The rest of this article is about reading that clock correctly.

I see this pattern a lot in north Peoria. A couple buys in Vistancia or off Happy Valley Road, one of them is turning 65, and the other is five or eight years younger and still on a payroll, often a hospital system, a school district, or a national company they work for remotely. The older spouse is on the younger spouse's plan, and the turning-65 mail tells them to enroll now. For many of them it is the wrong advice.

## The test is your spouse's job, not your age

Three questions decide whether you can leave Part B alone for now. All three are about your spouse:

1. **Is your spouse actively working?** The coverage has to come from a job somebody is doing now. A plan your spouse keeps after retiring is retiree coverage, and it does not count, even if the card looks identical.
2. **Does that employer have 20 or more employees?** That number decides who pays first for anyone 65 or older on the plan. Part-time staff count toward it.
3. **Are you legally married to the employee?** The age-65 rule is written for a spouse. If your coverage comes through a partner you are not married to, get the answer from Social Security in writing before you rely on it.

Three yeses and the work plan stays primary for you, as it was at 64. A large employer also cannot treat you differently because you turned 65. It has to offer you the same coverage on the same terms as a younger spouse, and it cannot reward you for leaving the plan for Medicare. CMS spells this out in its working-aged rules, linked below.

A "no" to the second question is the expensive one. At an employer with fewer than 20 employees, Medicare pays first for you at 65 and the group plan pays second. If you never enrolled, nobody pays first, and the plan can fairly pay a small slice of a hospital bill. A spouse who works for a small medical practice, a family business, or a five-person firm in Scottsdale is the common case. Ask the benefits office for the headcount in writing. I covered the full 20-employee rule, from the employee's side, in [Do I need to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I'm still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/)

## Three moments that start your eight months

Your Special Enrollment Period for Part A and Part B is eight months long. It starts the month after your spouse's employment ends or the month after the coverage ends, whichever comes first. In practice that happens in one of three ways.

**Your spouse retires.** This is the planned version, and the one to schedule for. If the job ends June 30 and the plan runs through July 31, your eight months count from July, because the job ended first. Remember that the coverage ending is the hard stop. If you sign up while the plan is still in force, or in the first full month after it ends, you can choose the month Part B starts, up to three months out. That lets you line Part B up with the plan's last day, so there is no gap.

**Your spouse goes on Medicare and drops the work plan.** This is the one that catches couples. If your spouse turns 65, is still working, and keeps the employer plan, nothing changes for you. But if your spouse decides Medicare is cheaper for them and leaves the group plan, your dependent coverage usually ends the same day. Medicare is one person at a time. Your spouse's new card does nothing for you. Before either of you leaves a work plan, settle which of you needs what.

**The coverage ends some other way.** Your spouse is laid off, the employer drops spouses from the plan at open enrollment, or your spouse dies. Each one starts your eight months. If the worst happens, [What happens to my Medicare when my spouse dies](/blog/what-happens-to-my-medicare-when-my-spouse-dies/) walks through the paperwork.

**COBRA does not stop the clock.** Neither does a retiree plan. If the two of you elect COBRA when your spouse retires, your eight months still started when the job ended. COBRA is a bridge for a month or two while Part B begins, not a reason to wait. Miss the window and Part B costs an extra 10% for every full 12 months you went without it, for as long as you have Part B. You might also have to wait for the January 1 to March 31 General Enrollment Period to sign up at all.

## The form your spouse's employer has to fill out

When you finally sign up, Social Security needs proof that you had coverage from current employment the whole time you delayed. That proof is **Form CMS-L564, Request for Employment Information**, and the part that matters is filled out by your spouse's employer, not yours. It lists the employee's name, dates of employment and the dates you were covered on the plan.

That makes the timing awkward in one specific way. Benefits offices are easy to reach while your spouse works there and slower once the badge is turned in. Get the L564 completed **before** your spouse's last day, and keep a copy. If the employer will not or cannot complete it, Social Security will accept other proof, such as pay stubs showing health premiums deducted, W-2s, or the plan's own coverage letters. Start collecting those now if your spouse's company has been through a merger or switched carriers.

You file the L564 together with **Form CMS-40B**, the Part B application, either online through Social Security or by mail. If you have not signed up for Part A yet, you can apply for both at once.

## Part A, Part D and the HSA while you are still a dependent

**Part A.** Most people can take Part A at 65 for no premium. That is the usual move while you stay on the work plan, because it can pick up hospital costs the group plan leaves behind. The exception is a Health Savings Account. Once you have Part A you cannot put money into *your own* HSA, and Part A is backdated up to six months when you apply after 65. If the family plan is a high-deductible plan and you have been funding an HSA, read [Can I contribute to an HSA while on Medicare?](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) first. Your working spouse's own HSA eligibility is a separate question that turns on their coverage, not yours. How much they can put in is one for your tax advisor.

**Drug coverage.** Your spouse's plan has to tell you each year whether its drug coverage is "creditable" for Medicare, meaning at least as good as standard Part D. Keep that notice. As long as it says creditable, you can skip Part D without a penalty. When the plan ends you get two full months after the month it ends to join a drug plan. Going 63 days or more without creditable drug coverage starts a penalty that lasts as long as you have Part D. For the drug plans you would be choosing from, see [Medicare Part D plans in Arizona 2027](/blog/medicare-part-d-plans-arizona-2027/).

## Why waiting can work in your favour in Arizona

Your one-time Medigap open enrollment does not start at 65. It starts the first month you are 65 or older **and** have Part B, and it lasts six months. During those six months any Medicare Supplement company has to sell you any plan it offers in Arizona, at the same price it charges people in good health, with no health questions.

So if you correctly delay Part B while on your spouse's plan, you also delay that window. It is waiting for you on the far side of your spouse's retirement, whether that is two years away or eight. That matters more here than in some states. **Arizona has no Medigap birthday rule** or yearly switching right, so after your six months, changing supplements usually means passing medical underwriting. [When is open enrollment for Medicare Supplement plans in Arizona?](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/) has the details.

The flip side: if you sign up for Part B "just to be safe" while you still have a large-employer plan, your six months start running then. Most people do not buy a supplement while they still have the work plan, so the window can close before they need it. Unless the work plan is small-employer, or you have a particular reason, taking Part B early tends to cost twice: the {{partB.premium}} a month premium in {{year}}, and the supplement window.

If you choose Medicare Advantage instead when the work plan ends, the Maricopa menu is large. CMS's 2027 plan landscape files list 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage in Maricopa County from 13 organizations. Losing the employer plan gives you two full months after the month it ends to join one, once Part A and Part B are in place.

## What to do this month if you are turning 65 on your spouse's plan

- Put your own dates into the [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) so you know when your seven-month window around 65 opens and closes.
- Ask your spouse's benefits office two questions in writing: how many employees the plan counts for Medicare secondary payer purposes, and whether the drug coverage is creditable.
- If the answer is under 20 employees, plan to enroll in Part A and Part B during your seven-month window around 65.
- If there is an HSA, stop and check the six-month look-back before you apply for anything, including Part A.
- Agree as a couple on the order of events before either of you leaves the work plan, and get the L564 completed before the last day of work.
