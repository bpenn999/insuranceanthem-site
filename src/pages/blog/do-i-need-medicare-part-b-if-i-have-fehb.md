**No. If you are a federal retiree, your Federal Employees Health Benefits plan keeps covering you whether or not you enroll in Medicare Part B, so Part B is a choice, not a condition of coverage.** The catch is that the choice is hard to undo: take Part B later and the premium carries a 10% penalty for every full 12 months you went without it, for life. Postal retirees are the exception. Under the Postal Service Health Benefits program, anyone who retired after January 1, 2025 has to be in Part B to keep PSHB, with only narrow exceptions.

Almost no other retiree in the country gets to make this call. A private-sector retiree plan pays after Medicare and assumes Part B is there. FEHB was built to stand on its own, which is why the people I meet in north Peoria who spent a career at Luke Air Force Base, the Phoenix VA or a federal office downtown ask me this more than anyone else does. The honest answer is that it depends on three things: whether you are still working, what your particular plan does when Medicare pays first, and your income.

## What is the rule while I am still a federal employee?

If you are 65 and still on the federal payroll, your FEHB plan pays first and Medicare pays second. OPM tells active employees they can defer Part B for exactly that reason. You lose nothing by waiting, because coverage from **current** employment protects you from the penalty.

When you retire, you get a Special Enrollment Period to pick up Part B. Medicare.gov sets it at eight months from the month the job or the employer coverage ends, whichever comes first. OPM's instruction is to ask your employing office to complete form CMS-L564 when you retire, which is the proof Social Security needs that you had employer coverage all along. Get it before you leave, while the people who can sign it still work there.

Part A is the easier call. Most people pay no premium for it, and taking it at 65 costs nothing. The one reason to think twice is a health savings account: if you are on an FEHB high-deductible plan and still contributing to an HSA, Part A ends your eligibility to contribute, and I explained the look-back in [can I contribute to an HSA while on Medicare?](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) The general working-at-65 rules are in [do I have to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I am still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/)

## What changes when I retire?

Two things, and both matter.

**Who pays first flips.** OPM's who-pays-first rules say Medicare is primary for an annuitant who has Medicare, and FEHB pays second. With Part A and Part B, a doctor's bill goes to Medicare first, which pays 80% of the approved amount after the {{partB.deductible}} deductible, and your FEHB plan then works on the rest. If you decline Part B, FEHB simply keeps paying doctor and outpatient bills on its own terms, the way it did when you were working.

**The penalty clock starts.** FEHB in retirement is not coverage from current employment. So if you pass up Part B at retirement, or at 65 if you were already retired, there is no Special Enrollment Period waiting later. A change of heart means the General Enrollment Period, January 1 through March 31, with Part B starting the month after you sign up, plus the 10%-per-year penalty described in the [late-enrollment penalties explainer](/learn/late-enrollment-penalties/). OPM's own example on its annuitant page walks through exactly that cost.

## When is Part B worth paying for on top of FEHB?

Here is the arithmetic, plainly. With Part B, you pay a second premium, and in exchange your FEHB plan usually stops charging you much of anything for doctor and outpatient care. OPM's annuitant page says your plan "may waive its copayments, coinsurance, and deductibles for services covered by Part B," and that some FEHB plans offer reimbursement for Part B premiums. You also get Medicare's reach: any doctor in the country who takes Medicare, which matters to anyone splitting the year between Peoria and somewhere else.

Without Part B, you keep the premium, and you keep your plan's cost sharing and its network rules.

In my experience the people who come out ahead with Part B are the ones who use a lot of outpatient care, travel, or are on a plan with real cost sharing. The people who often do fine without it are healthy, on a plan with modest copays, and paying an income surcharge that makes Part B expensive. That second group should still go in with open eyes: a serious diagnosis at 72 does not reopen the window.

## How does IRMAA change the math for a federal retiree?

A lot, and it is the part federal households underestimate. A full pension plus required minimum distributions from the Thrift Savings Plan can put a couple over the {{irmaa.jointStart}} line without much else going on. IRMAA is based on the tax return from two years earlier, so for {{year}} it looks at {{irmaaTaxYear}} income, and it raises the Part B premium for as long as income stays up.

That surcharge goes to Medicare, not to your FEHB plan, so when a plan says it reimburses part of the Part B premium, ask whether that covers only the standard amount. If your last working year is what pushed you into a bracket, retirement is a life-changing event Social Security accepts for a reduction; I walk through it in [how do I appeal IRMAA after retirement?](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/) and the [IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) will show where you land. Bring your tax advisor into any decision that turns on income.

## What about a Medicare Advantage plan instead?

There are two very different doors here, and federal retirees confuse them constantly.

**Through your FEHB plan.** Several FEHB carriers offer a Medicare Advantage plan to their own Medicare-enrolled annuitants. You need Part A and Part B, and you do not suspend FEHB. OPM says many of these plans reimburse some or all of the Part B premium, which is why they have become popular. The details live in your FEHB plan's brochure, not on the Medicare Plan Finder.

**An individual plan on the open market.** In Maricopa County that is a long menu. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, there are 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage here for 2027, 39 of them with a $0 premium, and out-of-pocket maximums running from $2,000 to $8,900. A $0 premium is not the same as no cost: those plans have copays, networks and that maximum. To use one you would suspend FEHB. OPM says that if you suspend, you can come back during open season or after a qualifying life event. **Canceling FEHB as an annuitant is permanent.** If you ever fill out paperwork that says "cancel," stop and call OPM first.

If you are in Peoria, the network question is the whole question. A plan that works around Arrowhead may look different a few miles away, and I covered the north-versus-south split on the [Peoria service-area page](/service-area/peoria-az/). For how the private plans differ from Medigap and from each other, the [Medicare Advantage vs. Medigap explainer](/learn/medicare-advantage-vs-medigap/) is the long version.

## Two deadlines in the same month: what should I do now?

This is the part that makes November tricky for federal households. The Federal Benefits Open Season runs **November 9 through December 14, 2026**, and OPM's figures put the average enrollee share of FEHB premiums up about 10.9% for 2027. Medicare's Annual Enrollment Period ends a week earlier, on **December 7**. If your plan for 2027 involves an individual Medicare Advantage plan, the Medicare deadline is the one that binds.

1. **This week: read your plan's 2027 brochure, the Medicare section.** Look for three things: what it waives when Medicare is primary, whether it reimburses any of the Part B premium, and whether it offers its own Medicare Advantage plan.
2. **If you are turning 65 and still working:** do nothing about Part B yet. Put a note in your retirement file to get form CMS-L564 signed before your last day.
3. **If you are turning 65 and retired:** your seven-month Initial Enrollment Period is the window. Decide on Part B inside it, not after. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) will lay out your dates.
4. **If you are a postal retiree:** check whether you retired after January 1, 2025. If so, Part B is not optional for PSHB, and a covered spouse who skips it can be dropped from family coverage.
5. **By December 7:** make any individual Medicare Advantage choice for January 1.
6. **By December 14:** make any FEHB plan change, including a switch to a plan whose Medicare features suit you better.

One more piece for anyone who skips Part B now and might want a Medicare Supplement later: your six-month Medigap open enrollment starts only when Part B does, and Arizona has no annual birthday window to reopen it. With FEHB behind you most people never need one, but it is worth knowing [how that window works](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/) before you decide.
