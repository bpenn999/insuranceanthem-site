**You sign up in one state: the one where you actually live.** Part A and Part B are federal, so the enrollment itself is identical whether you do it from a kitchen table in Peoria or one in Minnesota. What your home state decides is everything you add afterward. A Medicare Advantage or Part D plan follows one permanent address, and a Medicare Supplement is sold under the rules and prices of the state you live in on the day you apply.

I have this conversation every fall, usually with someone who drove down in October, turns 65 in February, and has just realized the mail about Medicare is going to a house with nobody in it.

## Does it matter which state I am in when I enroll in Part A and Part B?

No. You enroll through Social Security, online or by phone, and there is no Arizona version or Wisconsin version of the application. The premium is the same too: {{partB.premium}} a month for Part B in {{year}}, with the same income surcharge above {{irmaa.singleStart}} for a single filer or {{irmaa.jointStart}} for a couple, wherever you file.

What Social Security does want is your address, and that field matters more than the rest of the form. It is the address Medicare uses to decide which plans you may join, and it is where your card goes. So before you enroll, settle two things:

- **Your residence address** is the home that is really your permanent one. It should agree with your tax return, your driver's license and your voter registration.
- **Your mailing address** can be different. If you are in Arizona when your card and your plan notices go out, ask Social Security to send mail here for now. That is a mailing change. It does not move where you live.

Mixing those two up is the common mistake. People change their residence to the Peoria address so the card will find them, and in doing so tell Medicare they have moved to Maricopa County.

For someone who has not chosen anything yet, the short version is that the bottom row of that table is the one that punishes a two-state life, and the second row is the one built for it.

## Why your home state matters most for a Medicare Supplement

Every state gives you the same federal starting point. For six months, beginning the month you are 65 or older and have Part B, any insurer selling supplements in your state has to accept you at its standard price regardless of your health. That window comes once.

After that, the states go their own way, and this is where your choice of home state has consequences that last for decades:

- **Three states do not use the lettered plans.** Minnesota, Wisconsin and Massachusetts standardize their supplements differently. If you are a Minnesota resident you will not be buying a "Plan G" at all, and comparisons you read online may not match what you are offered.
- **Some states let you switch later without health questions.** California, Oregon and Illinois are among those with a birthday rule. Washington lets people who already hold a supplement move to another one. New York and Connecticut require insurers to accept applicants all year.
- **Arizona has none of those.** There is no birthday rule and no annual switching window here. Once your six months close, an Arizona insurer can ask health questions and turn you down. I covered the dates in [when Medicare Supplement open enrollment is in Arizona](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/).

Put those together and the question becomes more than paperwork. A couple who keep their legal home in a state with switching rights and winter in Peoria hold a policy that works at Arizona doctors and can still be changed later. The same couple, if they make Arizona home before 65, buy under Arizona's rules: a wide choice of insurers during the six months, and underwriting after it.

That is not a reason to pick a state for its insurance law. Where you live is a bigger decision than that, and it has tax consequences your tax advisor should weigh in on. It is a reason to know which set of rules you are buying under before the window opens.

## What if my 65th birthday falls while I am in Arizona?

Then you enroll from here, on time, at your home address. Your seven-month window is set by your birthday month and it does not wait for you to drive north.

- **Turning 65 in January 2027:** October 1, 2026 through April 30, 2027.
- **Turning 65 in February 2027:** November 1, 2026 through May 31, 2027.
- **Turning 65 in March 2027:** December 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027.

Enroll in one of the three months before your birthday month and coverage begins on the first of that month. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) will give you your own dates.

Two things trip up winter residents in particular. The first is the fall advertising. October 15 through December 7 is for people who already have Medicare and want to change plans. It is not your deadline, and it is not your window. The second is the supplement clock. Your six months start with Part B whether or not you are home to shop, so a February birthday means the window closes at the end of July. ## Two Peoria winters, two different answers

Here are the two situations I see most at the north end of the city.

**Home is still up north.** A couple from Wisconsin spend November through April in a house off Happy Valley Road and go back for the summer. They file, vote and hold licenses in Wisconsin. Their plan choices are Wisconsin's, and a Maricopa County Advantage plan is not available to them at all. For a five-or-six-month absence every year, Original Medicare with a supplement is usually the arrangement with the fewest moving parts, because nothing about it depends on being in one county. A PPO with out-of-network benefits can work as well, if they are comfortable checking the network each year.

**Home is now Peoria.** A couple sold the house in Illinois, changed their licenses, and keep a lake cabin for July and August. They live in 85383, so they choose from the Maricopa County menu, which for 2027 has 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage along with the full range of supplements. I listed what changed on that menu in [the Arizona plans ending for 2027](/blog/medicare-advantage-plans-ending-arizona-2027/). Two months at the cabin is well inside the six-month limit, so either route is open to them. The question for this couple is which Arrowhead and Sun City doctors a given network reaches, which is the subject of the [Peoria page](/service-area/peoria-az/).

The household that gets into trouble is the one in between: Arizona address on the Medicare record, real life mostly somewhere else. If the center of your life has moved, [you have moved](/blog/do-i-have-to-change-my-medicare-if-i-move-to-arizona/), and Medicare gives you a window to change plans that runs two full months past the move. If it has not, the home address stays where it is.

## What to do before your window opens

1. **Decide which state is home**, and make sure your tax return, license and voter registration already say so. Talk to your tax advisor if that is an open question.
2. **Check the address Social Security has for you**, and add a separate mailing address if you will be in Arizona when your card is sent.
3. **Enroll in Part A and Part B in the three months before your birthday month**, unless you are covered by an employer plan through current work. [Still working at 65](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/) covers that case.
4. **Find out what your home state allows for supplements** after the first six months. Your state insurance department publishes it.
5. **List the doctors and pharmacies you use in both places.** Any plan you consider has to work in both, and that list is how you check.
