**No, not safely. COBRA is not coverage from a current employer, so it does not let you delay Medicare Part B without a penalty, and it does not extend your sign-up window by a single day.** Your eight-month Special Enrollment Period starts the month the job or the employer plan ends, even if you elect COBRA that same week. Once you are 65, Medicare pays first whether you enrolled or not, and a COBRA plan is allowed to pay as though Medicare had already covered its share. Keeping COBRA is fine. Keeping COBRA *instead of* Medicare is how someone ends up with a bigger Part B premium for the rest of their life and a stack of claims nobody paid.

I see this most in north Peoria, and for an unremarkable reason: a lot of the households arriving in Vistancia and the 85383 corridor retired a year or two early from an employer in another state, took the 18 months of COBRA that came with the package, and are turning 65 somewhere in the middle of it. COBRA feels like coverage, so Medicare feels like something to deal with when the COBRA runs out. That instinct is exactly backwards, and this is the plain-English version of why.

## Why COBRA does not let you wait

Medicare lets you delay Part B without a penalty for one reason only: you or your spouse are *actively working* and covered by that employer's group health plan. Medicare.gov's own working-past-65 page says it in one sentence: you can delay Part B without a penalty until you or your spouse stop working or lose that coverage.

COBRA fails that test by definition. It is continuation coverage you buy after the job ends, which is the moment the "current employment" protection stops. The same page then says the part that catches people: the Special Enrollment Period starts when you stop working or lose the insurance, *even if you choose COBRA or other coverage that is not Medicare*. The eight months run from the job, not from the COBRA.

So the two situations look like this:

- **You are on COBRA when you turn 65.** Your seven-month Initial Enrollment Period is your window, three months before your birthday month through three months after. COBRA is not a reason to skip it. The [turning-65 explainer](/learn/turning-65-in-anthem/) walks through that window in full.
- **You worked past 65, delayed Part B, then retired and took COBRA.** You have eight months from the month the job ended to enroll in Part B without a penalty. COBRA running for 18 months does not move that date.

There is no third option where COBRA carries you to a later Medicare start. If somebody at a former employer's benefits desk told you otherwise, they were describing their plan's rules, not Medicare's.

The penalty is the smaller of the two problems. In this example the gap between the end of the window and the General Enrollment Period is long enough to count as one full 12-month period, which means a 10% surcharge on {{partB.premium}} a month, every month, for life, and it grows if the standard premium does. The [late-enrollment penalties explainer](/learn/late-enrollment-penalties/) has the arithmetic for longer gaps.

The larger problem is the months in between. Once you are 65, Medicare is the primary payer. A COBRA plan that is written to pay second can process a hospital bill as if Medicare had paid its {{partB.coinsurance}} and then cover only the remainder, or decline to pay at all until you show a Part B enrollment. Medicare.gov says this plainly: if your group health plan or retiree coverage is the secondary payer, you may need to sign up for Part B before they will pay. People do not discover this at the pharmacy. They discover it after an emergency room visit, which in north Peoria usually means a bill from Arrowhead or Sonoran Crossing arriving with a number on it that the COBRA premium was supposed to have prevented.

## Does COBRA end when I sign up for Medicare?

It can, and this is the part that surprises people in the other direction.

The Department of Labor's COBRA rules list "you become entitled to Medicare after electing COBRA" as one of the reasons a plan may end your continuation coverage early. "Entitled" means enrolled, not merely eligible: turning 65 by itself does not trigger it, but your Part A and Part B becoming effective does. Medicare.gov puts it less formally and says your COBRA coverage will probably end once you sign up.

Three things follow from that:

1. **If you were already on Medicare when the job ended**, your COBRA is not cut short for this reason. You elected it as a Medicare beneficiary and it runs its course, paying second.
2. **If you enroll in Medicare while on COBRA**, expect the plan to end your own coverage the month Medicare starts. Some employers let it continue; the plan document decides, and it is worth asking before you assume either way.
3. **Your spouse and dependents are not affected.** Their COBRA continues on its own clock, which brings up the one genuinely useful COBRA rule for couples.

## The 36-month rule for a younger spouse

If you enrolled in Medicare while you were still working, and then the job ended, the Department of Labor's rules give your spouse and dependents COBRA for up to **36 months from the date you became entitled to Medicare**, or 18 months from the date coverage was lost, whichever is longer. Your own COBRA, if you take it at all, is the usual 18 months.

That matters in a city where the spouse is often several years younger. I meet couples in Westbrook Village and in the newer Vistancia streets where the husband took Part A at 65 while still working, retired at 66, and the wife is 62. The automatic assumption is 18 months of COBRA for her and then a problem. The actual answer, if his Medicare entitlement date was less than 18 months before the retirement, is longer, and in the right fact pattern it reaches most of the way to her own 65th birthday.

Two cautions. The rule hinges on the Medicare entitlement happening *before* the job ended, so it does not apply if you both lost coverage first and you enrolled in Medicare afterwards. And benefits administrators do not always know it. Ask for the end date in writing and, if it says 18 months, ask them to check the Medicare-entitlement provision specifically.

## Which pays first, and why that decides everything

The order of payment is the reason none of the above is optional.

- **You have Medicare because you are 65 or older, or because of a disability:** Medicare pays first, COBRA pays second.
- **You have Medicare because of End-Stage Renal Disease:** COBRA pays first for a coordination period of up to 30 months, then the order flips.

That second case is rare. The first is everyone else, and it is why a COBRA plan is not a substitute for Part B even on the days it looks like one. Your [Medicare Supplement](/medicare-supplement/) or Advantage decision is downstream of this too: both assume Part A and Part B are in place, and the six-month Medigap open enrollment that gives you a policy without health questions starts the month your Part B begins. Arizona has no birthday rule and no annual re-opening of that window, so delaying Part B to ride out COBRA also delays the one period in which a supplement is yours for the asking. For the seasonal households in Trilogy at Vistancia who are gone four months a year, that window is usually the most valuable thing on the calendar, and it is tied to the Part B date, not the COBRA date.

## Part D: the one place COBRA can count

Drug coverage runs on a different rule. COBRA prescription coverage *can* be creditable for Part D, meaning it is expected to pay on average at least as much as standard Medicare drug coverage. If it is, you can stay on it past 65 without a Part D penalty, as long as you join a Part D plan within 63 days of it ending.

The plan has to tell you. Every employer plan sends an annual notice of creditable coverage, and that single page is the evidence Medicare accepts. Keep it. If the notice says the coverage is *not* creditable, or you cannot find one, treat the drug clock as running from the end of your Initial Enrollment Period. The Part D penalty is 1% of the national base beneficiary premium for every month without creditable coverage, added to your premium for as long as you have Medicare drug coverage, and CMS resets that base premium each year.

So the practical split for someone on COBRA at 65 is: enroll in Part B now, and make a separate, documented decision about Part D based on the creditable-coverage notice.

## When keeping COBRA alongside Medicare makes sense

COBRA is rarely worth its full premium as a secondary policy for the person on Medicare. There are a few cases where it earns its keep for a while:

- **A spouse or dependents still on it.** This is the main one. Your enrollment in Medicare does not end their COBRA, and the 36-month rule above may lengthen it.
- **Dental, vision or other benefits Medicare does not cover.** Some plans let you continue just those pieces after Medicare starts. Worth asking.
- **Finishing a course of treatment** with a specialist who is in the employer plan's network and would not be in the Medicare Advantage network you are considering. A Medicare Supplement usually solves this more cleanly than COBRA does, because it follows Original Medicare to any provider who accepts it.
- **A severance package that subsidises the COBRA premium** for a set number of months. If the employer is paying most of it, keeping it as secondary coverage for those months costs little. It still does not replace Part B.

If you have a Health Savings Account from the job, COBRA premiums are among the few insurance premiums an HSA can reimburse tax-free, and so are Medicare premiums once you are enrolled. The contribution side is a separate trap with its own six-month look-back; [can I contribute to an HSA while on Medicare?](/blog/can-i-contribute-to-an-hsa-while-on-medicare/) covers it. Run the reimbursement question past your tax advisor rather than your benefits desk.

## Three situations I actually see in Peoria

**The early retiree on COBRA at 65.** Moved to Vistancia from a Midwest employer at 63, took 18 months of COBRA, turns 65 in month eleven. The right move is to enroll in Part A and Part B during the Initial Enrollment Period, expect COBRA to end for them when Medicare starts, and use the Medigap window while it is open. The remaining COBRA months, if the plan allows them, cover the spouse.

**The post-65 retiree who delayed Part B.** Worked remotely past 65 for a large employer, retired in the spring, elected COBRA, and has eight months from the retirement date to pick up Part B. The COBRA will outlast the window by ten months, which is precisely the trap. The fix is to put the eighth month on the calendar the day the COBRA paperwork arrives.

**The small-business owner.** Federal COBRA applies to employers with 20 or more employees. An owner of a six-person company in the 85345 core who turns 65 does not have federal COBRA to lean on, and because the employer is under 20 employees, Medicare becomes primary at 65 regardless. Here there is no decision to defer: Part B at 65, then the Supplement-or-Advantage question. [Do I need to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I'm still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/) covers the 20-employee rule in detail.

In all three the plan menu is set by Maricopa County and it is the same list at both ends of Peoria. What differs is the timing, and timing is the whole article.

## What to do this month if you are on COBRA and near 65

1. **Find two dates.** The month your employment ended, and the month your COBRA ends. Only the first one matters to Medicare. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) turns your birthday and your job-end date into the actual deadlines.
2. **Enroll in Part A and Part B** in your Initial Enrollment Period if you are turning 65, or inside the eight-month Special Enrollment Period if you are past it. Do it early in the window so coverage starts on the first of the month you want.
3. **Pull the creditable-coverage notice** for the drug side and decide Part D on that evidence, not on a guess.
4. **Ask the plan administrator, in writing,** whether your own COBRA ends when Medicare starts, and whether your spouse's runs 18 months or 36.
5. **Open the Medigap question the month Part B starts.** The six-month window is the one that does not come back, and in Arizona there is no birthday rule to reopen it later.
