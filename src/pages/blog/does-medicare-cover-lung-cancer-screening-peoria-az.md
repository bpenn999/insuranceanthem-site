**Yes. Medicare Part B covers one low-dose CT lung cancer screening a year, and you pay nothing for it if the provider accepts assignment. To qualify you must be 50 to 77, have no symptoms of lung cancer, have smoked at least 20 pack-years, and either still smoke or have quit within the last 15 years. The Part B deductible doesn't apply, and every Medicare Advantage plan has to cover the scan in network with no cost sharing.**

That's the standard answer, and it's the same in Peoria as anywhere else. The part that's different here comes after the scan. In the Valley, a lot of screening CTs find a spot on the lung, and many of those spots are old Valley fever, not cancer. Sorting that out takes more scans, sometimes a biopsy, and those are billed as diagnostic care with ordinary cost sharing. So I'd plan around the follow-up, not just the free first scan.

## Who qualifies for the free scan

Medicare's rules come from its national coverage decision for low-dose CT screening, NCD 210.14, which CMS widened on February 10, 2022. Before that the starting age was 55 and the smoking history was 30 pack-years. Now you need all five of these:

The counseling visit happens once, before your first scan. Your provider confirms you qualify, walks through the benefits and risks with a decision aid, talks about coming back every year, and talks about quitting if you still smoke. Medicare covers that visit too. After that the scan repeats once a year for as long as you still qualify.

The quit-date rule surprises a lot of retirees. The American Cancer Society changed its guideline in 2023 to recommend screening for former smokers no matter how long ago they quit, ages 50 to 80. Medicare hasn't followed. If you quit in 2008, your doctor may recommend the scan, but Medicare won't pay for it as screening. Ask for the self-pay price first, or ask whether anything in your history makes the scan diagnostic.

## What the free scan does not include

The $0 applies to the yearly screening scan and the counseling visit. It does not cover what happens if the scan finds something.

Most scans that find something end with a radiologist recommending a repeat CT in a few months to see whether a nodule changes. That repeat is ordered to check a known finding, so it's a diagnostic test. Diagnostic imaging under Part B costs {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount after the {{partB.deductible}} deductible in {{figYear}}. A PET scan, a lung specialist visit or a needle biopsy is diagnostic too. If the work happens in a hospital outpatient department rather than a freestanding imaging center, the facility charge adds to your share.

None of that is unusual. It's just that the free scan is the start of the process, and how much of the rest you pay depends on your coverage.

## Why Valley fever matters for a Peoria scan

Valley fever, the fungal infection that's common in Maricopa County, often leaves small scars in the lungs called nodules, even in people who never felt very sick. On a CT, those nodules can look like early lung cancer.

The research on this comes mostly from the Southwest:

- A 2023 study from Fresno, in California's Central Valley where Valley fever is also common, found that **up to a third of patients sent for a biopsy because of suspected lung cancer had Valley fever instead.** The authors cite other work estimating that one-third to one-half of high-risk-looking nodules in endemic areas are Valley fever.
- A 2014 meta-analysis, summarized in a 2017 JAMA Surgery paper that included a Phoenix nodule clinic, found PET/CT scans were **61% specific in regions where fungal infections are common, against 77% elsewhere.** Put simply, in those regions PET wrongly flagged about 39% of the nodules that were not cancer as suspicious.
- John Galgiani of the University of Arizona's Valley Fever Center for Excellence has said that where Valley fever is common, it should very often be considered when a mass is thought to be cancer.

For you, that means a Peoria screening is more likely than a Midwest one to lead to follow-up, and more of that follow-up turns out to be nothing serious. That's reassuring medically, but you still pay the diagnostic cost sharing. If you've had Valley fever, or lived here a long time, say so at the counseling visit and when you get results. Your doctor decides what to do with a nodule. Telling them your history helps them decide. I covered how Medicare pays for the infection itself in [Does Medicare cover Valley fever testing and treatment?](/blog/does-medicare-cover-valley-fever-peoria-az/)

**On a Medicare Supplement**, a nodule workup is mostly a scheduling question. Any provider who takes Medicare will do, with no referral and no prior authorization, and Plan G leaves you with the {{partB.deductible}} deductible for the year. That's one reason retirees who expect a lot of imaging tend to pick a supplement. The trade-off is the monthly premium.

**On a Medicare Advantage plan**, the screening is still $0 in network. The questions start with the follow-up: whether the plan needs approval before a PET scan or biopsy, whether the pulmonologist you're referred to is in network, and what the imaging copay is. Every plan has an out-of-pocket maximum that caps the total, and I've explained [how the 2027 limits work in Peoria](/blog/medicare-advantage-out-of-pocket-maximum-peoria-az-2027/).

## The Peoria picture

- **Where the scans happen.** North Peoria residents in Vistancia, Trilogy and the 85383 corridor usually get imaging at an independent center near Lake Pleasant Parkway or at the hospital outpatient departments toward Arrowhead and Sun City. For the free screening scan, either is fine on Original Medicare. For a follow-up, a freestanding center often costs you less than a hospital department.
- **On an HMO, check the radiology group.** Some Advantage HMOs contract with specific imaging centers. If your primary care doctor is in Peoria and the plan sends imaging to Glendale or Sun City, that's worth knowing before the first appointment, not after.
- **Snowbirds.** If you spend summers out of state, schedule the yearly scan in the same place each year so the radiologist can compare images. On a supplement, any Medicare provider in either state works. On an Advantage HMO, routine screening generally has to happen in the plan's area.
- **Turning 78.** Screening coverage ends after 77. If a nodule is still being watched, follow-up scans continue as diagnostic care. The age limit only stops new screening.

## The short version

- **Medicare covers one low-dose CT lung screening a year at $0**, ages 50 to 77, 20 pack-years, still smoking or quit within 15 years, with no symptoms.
- **The first scan needs a counseling visit**, which Medicare also covers.
- **Quit more than 15 years ago? Medicare won't pay for screening**, even though the American Cancer Society now recommends it.
- **Follow-up scans and biopsies are diagnostic** and cost {{partB.coinsurance}} after the deductible on Original Medicare, nothing extra after the deductible on Plan G, and the plan's copays on Advantage.
- **In the Valley, expect more follow-up.** Valley fever scars look like nodules. Tell your doctor your history.
