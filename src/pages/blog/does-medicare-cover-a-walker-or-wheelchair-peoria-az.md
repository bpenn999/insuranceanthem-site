**Yes. Medicare Part B covers a walker, a rollator and a wheelchair as durable medical equipment when your doctor orders it for use in your home. In {{figYear}} you pay the {{partB.deductible}} Part B deductible and then {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount, as long as the supplier is enrolled in Medicare and accepts assignment.** Power wheelchairs and scooters are covered too, but only after a face-to-face exam with a treating provider, and many models need Medicare's approval before the supplier can deliver them.

That is the standard answer, and Medicare.gov gives it in nearly those words. What it leaves out is the part that trips people up in practice: who you buy from, whether you rent or own, and what happens when the brakes on a three-year-old rollator give out in July. I'll take those in order, with the Peoria detail at the end.

The {{partB.coinsurance}} applies to the **Medicare-approved amount**, not to the price on the showroom tag. A supplier that accepts assignment can charge you only the deductible and coinsurance on that approved amount. A non-participating supplier doesn't have to accept assignment and can bill you more, so it's worth asking this before anything is delivered.

## The three things Medicare checks before it pays

**1. An order from your doctor.** The equipment has to be medically necessary, and the order has to come from the doctor or other provider treating you. A walker you pick up yourself at a pharmacy without one is a retail purchase, not a Medicare claim.

**2. "For use in your home."** This is Medicare's wording, and it's literal. The need has to show up inside the house, not only on the trail or at the airport. Under CMS's benefit manual, an apartment in an assisted-living community generally counts as home. A hospital or a skilled nursing facility does not, because the facility supplies the equipment as part of the stay. If you are coming home from a rehab stay, the order should be written for the house before you're discharged.

**3. A supplier enrolled in Medicare.** Medicare pays only for equipment from enrolled suppliers. Medicare.gov runs a [supplier search](https://www.medicare.gov/medical-equipment-suppliers/) by ZIP code. Use it rather than the first name that comes up in a map search, because enrolled and nearby are not always the same supplier.

One warning Medicare repeats often: if someone calls you out of the blue offering a "free" brace, scooter or walker billed to Medicare, hang up. Legitimate equipment starts with your own doctor, not with a phone call.

## Rent or own: the 13-month rule

Cheap items such as a standard walker or a cane are usually bought outright on the first claim. Many wheelchairs, and some other equipment, fall under what Medicare calls **capped rental**. The rule is set by regulation, 42 CFR § 414.229:

- Medicare pays a monthly rental for **no longer than 13 months of continuous use**.
- The supplier must then **transfer ownership to you** on the first day after the 13th paid month.
- Your {{partB.coinsurance}} applies to each monthly rental payment, not to a single purchase price.

The practical risk is a supplier that accepts assignment for the first month but not for the later ones. Medicare.gov is blunt about it: if the supplier won't accept assignment for every rental month, you can end up paying the full cost up front. The regulation requires the supplier to tell you before delivery whether it will accept assignment for the whole rental period. Get that answer in writing.

A snowbird detail: once the chair is yours, it travels with you. If you spend summers out of state, any Medicare-approved supplier there can repair it.

## Power wheelchairs and scooters need more paperwork

Medicare.gov says Part B covers power wheelchairs and scooters "only when they're medically necessary". Before it pays, it requires a **face-to-face examination and a written prescription from a treating provider**. In practice the notes from that visit need to explain why a cane, a walker or a manual chair won't get you around your own home, and that you can handle the power device safely.

For a long list of power wheelchair models, identified by billing code on [Medicare.gov's prior authorization page](https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/wheelchairs-scooters/power-wheelchairs-prior-authorization), the supplier has to request **prior authorization** from Medicare and send the supporting records. Medicare lists two common reasons for a denial: it decides you don't medically need a power chair, or it didn't receive enough information to decide. The second is a paperwork problem, and it's the one you can usually fix by making sure the exam notes say what the need actually is.

Standard power wheelchairs are rented under the 13-month rule above. An up-front purchase option exists only for complex rehabilitative models.

## What Medicare will not pay for

Durable medical equipment means equipment that serves a medical purpose, withstands repeated use and is used in the home. Several things families buy after a fall or a surgery miss that definition. Medicare generally treats them as comfort, convenience or home-modification items, not medical equipment:

- grab bars, shower chairs and tub transfer benches
- raised toilet seats (a bedside commode used in place of the toilet is a different matter)
- stair lifts, ramps and widened doorways
- a second walker or wheelchair kept in the car or at a second home

Some Medicare Advantage plans offer an over-the-counter allowance or a supplemental home-safety benefit that helps with a few of these. Medigap does not, because it only pays toward bills Medicare has already approved.

## Repairs, and the five-year replacement clock

Medicare pays to repair equipment you own, including replacement parts, through any Medicare-approved supplier. It doesn't have to be the one that sold it to you.

Replacement follows 42 CFR § 414.210(f). Medicare will pay for a new item once the old one has been in continuous use for its **reasonable useful lifetime, which can be no less than five years**, counted from the day it was delivered to you. Inside five years, Medicare will replace an item only if it is lost, stolen or damaged beyond repair, which is where a police or insurance report helps. When repair bills climb, the contractor may compare them with the cost of replacing the item. The regulation uses 60 percent of the replacement cost as the yardstick.

## On a Medicare Advantage plan in Maricopa County

According to CMS's {{figYear}} plan landscape files, Maricopa County has 85 Medicare Advantage plans for {{figYear}}, up from 82 this year, special needs plans included. Every one of them has to cover walkers and wheelchairs at least as broadly as Original Medicare. They differ on three things, and all three are in the plan's Evidence of Coverage under "durable medical equipment":

- **Your share.** Many plans charge a DME coinsurance, often in the same range as Original Medicare's {{partB.coinsurance}}, and it counts toward the plan's out-of-pocket maximum.
- **The supplier.** Most plans contract with a short list of DME vendors. A rollator from an out-of-network showroom on Bell Road can be denied outright on an HMO, even when the same item from the plan's vendor would have been covered.
- **Prior authorization.** Common for power mobility devices, and on some plans for anything above a set price.

If a power chair is likely in the next year and you're deciding between plans now, the DME line and the supplier list belong next to the drug list in your comparison. The Annual Enrollment Period closes **December 7**, two weeks from today.

## The short version

- **A walker, rollator or manual wheelchair is covered under Part B**: the {{partB.deductible}} deductible, then {{partB.coinsurance}} of the approved amount in {{figYear}}.
- **You need a doctor's order for use in the home and a Medicare-enrolled supplier.** Ask whether it accepts assignment.
- **Many wheelchairs are rented for up to 13 months**, and then they're yours.
- **Power chairs and scooters need a face-to-face exam**, and many models need prior authorization.
- **Grab bars, shower chairs, stair lifts and ramps are not covered.**
- **Replacement comes after five years** of use, or sooner if the item is lost, stolen or beyond repair.
- **On Medicare Advantage, use the plan's supplier** and read the DME line in the Evidence of Coverage.
