**A C-SNP, or Chronic Condition Special Needs Plan, is a Medicare Advantage plan that only accepts people with a particular chronic diagnosis and is built around treating it. Maricopa County will have 25 of them in {{nextYear}}, up from 20 this year. Twenty are for diabetes, heart failure or cardiovascular disease, four are for kidney disease and one is for chronic lung disease. Every one is an HMO, and 21 of the 25 carry a $0 plan premium.**

That growth runs against the rest of the shelf. The ordinary Medicare Advantage menu in the county slipped from 46 plans to 45 for {{nextYear}} and [eight general plans ended outright](/blog/medicare-advantage-plans-ending-arizona-2027/). Carriers trimmed the plans that take everybody and added plans that take a defined group. If you live in Peoria and manage one of these conditions, that shift is worth ten minutes before December 7, whether or not you ever enroll in one.

## What a C-SNP is, in plain terms

Three kinds of Special Needs Plan exist. One is for people who have both Medicare and Medicaid, one is for people living in a nursing facility, and the third is for people with a chronic condition. Only the third is the subject here, and it has **no income test**. The plan asks three things: do you have Parts A and B, do you live in its service area, and has a provider diagnosed you with the condition it serves.

In exchange for that narrow door, the plan is allowed to design everything around one kind of patient. Medicare.gov's description is that every SNP must include Part D drug coverage and must provide care coordination. In practice a heart-and-diabetes plan tends to put insulin, test supplies and the common cardiac drugs on favorable tiers, set specialist copays with frequent cardiology and endocrinology visits in mind, and assign a care manager. How well a given plan does that is in its Evidence of Coverage and drug list. The label alone does not tell you.

What does not change: you still pay the Part B premium, {{partB.premium}} a month in {{year}}, and you are still in a network.

## Which conditions the Maricopa County plans accept

The federal definition of a qualifying condition has 22 categories, including cancer, dementia, stroke, autoimmune disorders and chronic mental health conditions. A carrier chooses which of them to build a plan for, and in the Valley they have chosen four groups.

- **Diabetes, chronic heart failure and cardiovascular disorders: 20 plans.** These are sold as one combined group, so any one of the three qualifies. The cardiovascular category covers cardiac arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease and valvular heart disease.
- **Chronic kidney disease: 3 plans**, including dialysis.
- **Kidney disease or post-kidney-transplant care: 1 plan**, new for {{nextYear}}.
- **Chronic lung disorders: 1 plan**, the category that includes COPD, emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis and asthma.

There is no Maricopa County C-SNP for cancer, dementia, stroke or an autoimmune condition in {{nextYear}}. A reader with one of those is choosing among the general plans and Original Medicare, the same as before.

Four things stand out.

**The label does not mean a low cap.** Ten of the 25 plans cap in-network spending under $3,000 a year. Six cap it at $9,250 or more, higher than any general plan in the county, where the top is $8,900. Two plans from the same carrier with nearly the same name can sit at opposite ends, as the Humana and Alignment rows show. For someone who expects a hospital stay or regular specialist care, the cap matters more than the premium.

**Every one is an HMO.** Seventeen are plain HMOs and eight are HMO-POS, which allows some out-of-network use for certain services at higher cost. There is no PPO among the 25. A household that chose a PPO to keep a wide choice of specialists gives that up to move here.

**Drug deductibles went up on most of them.** Five plans have no Part D deductible; many of the rest sit at or near $700. That is separate from the [{{nextYear}} out-of-pocket cap on covered drugs](/blog/medicare-part-d-out-of-pocket-cap-2027/), which applies to all of them.

**Ratings are by contract and are not a measure of your own doctors.** They range from 2.5 to 4.5 here. [What the 2027 Star Ratings do and do not tell you](/blog/are-there-any-5-star-medicare-advantage-plans-in-arizona-2027/) has the detail.

## The two that ended, and the seven that are new

Two chronic-condition plans sold in Maricopa County this year are absent from the entire {{nextYear}} file: **SCAN Strive (HMO C-SNP), H1822-006**, and **Aetna Medicare Prime Chronic Care (HMO C-SNP), H3931-188**. Members of either should have a non-renewal letter dated October 2.

Aetna has a new plan with a similar name, Aetna Medicare Chronic Care (HMO C-SNP), on a different contract, H8332-010. Whether a member is moved into it automatically or has to choose is stated in the letter and nowhere else. A shared name does not mean a shared network or drug list, so check both before letting anything happen by default. The [week-by-week plan for a discontinued plan](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/) covers the dates.

The seven new plans are three from Devoted Health on a second contract, SCAN Walmart Medicare Complete, SCAN DaVita Dialysis Care Complete, Gold Dialysis Premier and the Aetna plan above. Two of the seven are kidney plans, which doubles the kidney choices from two to four.

Statewide the count went from 32 chronic-condition plans to 42. Pima County went from 19 to 24, Pinal from 10 to 14, Coconino from 3 to 6 and Yavapai from 3 to 5. Mohave County lost its only one. That matters to a Peoria household with a summer address up north, because a C-SNP, like any Advantage plan, follows your permanent county.

## When you can join one

There are three ways in, and the middle one is specific to these plans.

1. **Annual Enrollment, October 15 through December 7**, for a January 1 start. Anyone on Medicare can use it.
2. **The chronic-condition special enrollment period.** If you have a qualifying condition you can join a C-SNP that serves it in any month of the year. The rule at 42 CFR §422.62(b) says this period ends once you are enrolled, so it is a single move, not a revolving door. Someone diagnosed with heart failure in April does not have to wait for October.
3. **A discontinued plan.** If your {{year}} plan ended, you have through February 28, {{nextYear}} to choose, though choosing by December 31 avoids a gap in drug coverage.

Before or just after you enroll, the plan has to confirm the diagnosis with your provider. If it enrolls you on a questionnaire and cannot verify by the end of the second month, it is required to disenroll you. Call your doctor's office and tell them the form is coming.

## How a Peoria household should weigh it

Most of the north Peoria people I talk with about this are in 85383, in Vistancia, Trilogy or Blackstone, and their cardiologist, endocrinologist or nephrologist is in Sun City or near Arrowhead. Some moved here with a plan they bought in another state. A few steps keep the decision honest.

**Start with the specialist, not the plan.** A chronic-condition plan is only useful if the specialist who manages that condition is in it. Ask the practice directly whether it will be in network with that exact plan on January 1. The Peoria, Glendale and Sun City line is drawn tightly enough that [quotes differ by ZIP](/blog/medicare-plans-by-zip-code-peoria-az/), and the hospital-based groups contract separately from the building, which [how Advantage networks work](/learn/medicare-advantage-networks/) explains.

**Price your own drugs on the plan's list.** The point of the design is a formulary tuned to the condition. Run your actual prescriptions, including insulin type and any brand-name cardiac or kidney drugs, and compare the result with the general plan you have now.

**Read the cap against a bad year.** With a chronic condition, the bad year is not hypothetical. A $0 plan with a $9,850 cap and a $0 plan with a $2,000 cap are very different promises.

**Know what you give up.** HMO rules, referrals where the plan requires them, and a service area that stops at the county line. Households who spend four months of the year elsewhere should read [how Advantage plans work in another state](/blog/can-i-use-my-medicare-advantage-plan-in-another-state/) first.

**Then look at the Medicare Supplement door, because the timing is unusual.** Arizona has no birthday rule and no annual Medigap window. Outside a protected period, a supplement carrier can ask health questions, and diabetes with complications, heart failure and kidney disease are the answers that most often lead to a decline. That is why a chronic condition makes the protected periods valuable: the [six months around your Part B start](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/), and the guaranteed-issue window from **November 1, 2026 through March 4, 2027** for anyone whose plan was discontinued. In those windows the diagnosis does not count against you. For a household that would rather pay a known premium than manage a network and a cap, that may be the one chance. [Switching from Advantage to Medigap in Arizona](/blog/can-i-switch-from-medicare-advantage-to-medigap-in-arizona/) lays out the rules, and [the Advantage and Medigap comparison](/learn/medicare-advantage-vs-medigap/) covers the trade.

Neither route is right for everyone. A well-matched C-SNP with your specialists in network and a low cap can cost far less in a year than a supplement. A poorly matched one is an ordinary HMO with a narrower door.
