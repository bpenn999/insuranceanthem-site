**You do not need heart attack and stroke insurance to be covered for a heart attack or a stroke. Medicare pays for the ambulance, the hospital stay, inpatient rehab and cardiac rehab. With Original Medicare and Medigap Plan G, your share of all of that comes down to the Part B deductible ({{partB.deductible}} in {{year}}). On a Medicare Advantage plan, your share is capped by the plan's in-network out-of-pocket limit, and in Maricopa County 19 of the 45 general Advantage plans with drug coverage set that limit at $5,000 or more for 2027.** A heart and stroke policy pays you cash on diagnosis. It earns its premium only when there is a real gap for that cash to fill.

I hear this one around north Peoria, often from a Vistancia or Trilogy couple right after a mailer or a neighbor's scare. It is a fair worry. CDC's county estimates put about 3 in 100 Maricopa County adults as having had a stroke and about 1 in 20 as living with coronary heart disease, and both rise with age. The useful question is not whether a heart attack is expensive. It is how much of the expense is already yours under the Medicare you have.

Two details in that table catch people. The first is the **three-day rule** for a skilled nursing facility: Original Medicare covers it only after three days as an admitted inpatient, and time spent under observation does not count. The [observation stay post](/blog/does-medicare-cover-hospital-observation-stay/) explains how to ask which one you are. The second is the **20% with no ceiling**. A stroke that leads to weeks of outpatient therapy produces a Part B bill that keeps growing until something closes it.

The rest of the event is covered in more depth elsewhere on this site: [ambulance rides](/blog/does-medicare-cover-ambulance-rides-peoria-az/), [rehab after a hospital stay](/blog/how-long-does-medicare-pay-for-rehab-after-a-hospital-stay-peoria-az/) and [cardiac rehab in Peoria](/blog/does-medicare-cover-cardiac-rehab-peoria-az/).

## On a Medigap plan, most of the bill is already spoken for

Medigap Plan G pays the Part A hospital deductible, the skilled nursing coinsurance for days 21 to 100, the {{partB.coinsurance}} Part B coinsurance and any Part B excess charges. What it leaves you is the Part B deductible, once a year. Plan N leaves you that deductible plus small copays: up to $20 at some office visits and up to $50 at the emergency room unless you are admitted.

So for a household in Blackstone or along the Happy Valley corridor carrying Plan G, a heart attack produces a medical bill close to the same {{partB.deductible}} as a quiet year, plus prescriptions under the Part D cap. A heart and stroke policy layered on top would mostly be paying you for a bill you do not get. If that cash is still appealing, be honest that you are buying it for the costs Medicare never pays, which come two sections down, and price it on that basis.

## On a Medicare Advantage plan, check the number on page one of your plan summary

An Advantage plan replaces the Part A deductible and the open-ended 20% with its own copays: a set amount per hospital day for the first several days, a daily copay in a skilled nursing facility, a copay per rehab session. Every one of those counts toward the plan's **in-network maximum out-of-pocket limit**, and once you hit it the plan pays 100% of covered in-network care for the rest of the calendar year. A heart attack in March can easily reach the limit by summer.

That makes the limit the number to look at. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, here is how the 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage in Maricopa County line up:

- **11 plans** have an in-network limit of $3,000 or less. The lowest is $2,000.
- **15 plans** fall between $3,000 and $5,000.
- **19 plans** are at $5,000 or more. The highest is $8,900.

Prescription costs sit outside that limit and are capped separately by Part D at $2,400 in {{nextYear}}. On a PPO, the limit for out-of-network care is higher again, which matters if the hospital that takes you is not one your plan contracts with. In the West Valley, that is a live question: north Peoria ambulances generally head toward Arrowhead, Sun City or Glendale, and those hospitals are not carried identically by every plan.

On a $2,000 or $2,500 plan, a lump-sum policy is covering a modest, known number. On an $8,900 plan, the gap is large enough that some households want cash set aside for it, whether in a savings account or an insurance policy. The [Maricopa out-of-pocket maximum post](/blog/medicare-advantage-out-of-pocket-maximum-peoria-az-2027/) lists every plan by its 2027 limit.

## The cost Medicare never pays, and the one a lump sum is suited to

Here is where heart attack and stroke differ from most of the other "extra" policies people get mailed. A serious stroke can leave someone needing help with bathing, dressing or getting safely from bed to chair for months. Medicare covers skilled care: therapy, nursing, rehab. It does **not** cover custodial care, the day-to-day help with ordinary activities, when that is the only care you need, at home or in a facility.

In Arizona, that help is priced by the hour. The {{care.surveyYear}} CareScout Cost of Care Survey puts a non-medical caregiver at about {{care.az.homeCareHourly}} an hour here, and assisted living at around {{care.az.assistedLiving}} a month. None of it counts toward a Medicare Advantage out-of-pocket limit and none of it is paid by Medigap, because none of it is a Medicare-covered service.

There are smaller unpaid costs too: a walk-in shower or grab bars, a ramp at the front door, a spouse driving to a rehab facility in Sun City every day for three weeks. A heart and stroke policy pays cash that you can spend on any of this. That is its strongest case, and it is a case about long-term care, not about medical bills. If the worry is really months of help at home, a [long-term care policy](/blog/is-long-term-care-insurance-worth-it-arizona/) or [short-term care coverage](/blog/is-short-term-care-insurance-worth-it-with-medicare/) is built for that job, and it is worth comparing before you buy a policy that pays once on diagnosis.

## What to read in a heart and stroke policy before you buy

These are not Medicare products, and they are not standardized the way Medigap plans are. Two policies with the same name can pay very differently. Before comparing premiums, read:

1. **The definitions.** Each policy defines what counts as a heart attack and what counts as a stroke, and the definitions are narrower than the everyday words. Check whether a procedure such as a stent or bypass without a confirmed heart attack pays anything, and whether a transient ischemic attack (a "mini-stroke") counts.
2. **Pre-existing conditions.** Most policies exclude or decline a heart or stroke condition you were diagnosed or treated for before you applied, sometimes for a set number of years. If you already have heart disease, the policy may never pay for the event you are most worried about.
3. **Waiting periods.** Many policies pay nothing for a diagnosis in the first weeks or months after purchase.
4. **First occurrence or recurrence.** Some pay once and end. Some pay a reduced benefit for a second event.
5. **Premium changes.** Ask whether the premium can rise as you age or across a class of policyholders, and how often it has.

This is general education. A licensed professional can show you the actual policy language and how it fits your situation, and I would rather you read the definitions page than the brochure.

## When it is worth a closer look, and when it is not

**Probably not needed** if you have Original Medicare with Plan G or Plan N, solid emergency savings and a separate plan for long-term care, or if you are on an Advantage plan with a limit near the bottom of the Maricopa range and could pay that limit from savings.

**Worth pricing** if you are on an Advantage plan near the top of the range, if a medical bill of several thousand dollars would genuinely strain the household budget, or if you want cash for help at home and long-term care coverage is no longer available to you.

**Worth fixing at the source first** if the real problem is the Medicare choice itself. Arizona has no Medigap birthday rule, so outside your first [Medigap open enrollment](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/) and a few guaranteed-issue situations, moving from Advantage to Medigap means answering health questions, and a recent heart attack or stroke can mean being declined. For someone still healthy and wondering whether to switch, the question to answer before December 7 is the Medicare one, not the add-on.
