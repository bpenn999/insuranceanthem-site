  - q: How long will Medicare pay for hospice care?
    a: For as long as a hospice doctor keeps certifying that you are terminally ill. Eligibility starts with a prognosis of six months or less if the illness runs its normal course, but there is no six-month cutoff. Medicare pays in benefit periods, two of 90 days and then an unlimited number of 60-day periods, and at the start of each new period the hospice doctor recertifies. People do live longer than expected on hospice, and the coverage continues as long as the certification does.
  - q: Does Medicare pay for 24-hour hospice care at home?
    a: Not as a standing arrangement. A hospice nurse and doctor are on call 24 hours a day, and the hospice team visits on a schedule, but day-to-day hands-on care between visits is expected to come from family or paid caregivers. The exception is a crisis. When symptoms cannot be controlled any other way, hospice can provide continuous home care, predominantly nursing, for up to 24 hours a day until the crisis passes. Federal rules require at least eight hours of care on a day for it to count as continuous home care.
  - q: Who pays for hospice if you have a Medicare Advantage plan?
    a: Original Medicare does. Once you elect hospice, Original Medicare covers everything related to the terminal illness, even if you stay in your Advantage plan. The plan keeps covering its extras, such as dental and vision, care for unrelated health problems, and its drug coverage for drugs that are not part of the hospice plan, as long as you keep paying the plan premium. A short-lived experiment that let some Advantage plans run hospice themselves ended on December 31, 2024.
  - q: What is not covered under hospice?
    a: Four things. Treatment meant to cure the terminal illness, because electing hospice means choosing comfort care instead. Prescription drugs for unrelated conditions, which go to your Part D or Advantage drug coverage. Care from a hospice or provider the hospice team did not arrange. And room and board, whether that is your own home, an assisted living apartment or a nursing home. Ambulance trips and emergency room visits for the terminal illness also need to go through the hospice, or the bill can land on you.
  - q: Does Medicare cover hospice in a nursing home or assisted living?
    a: It covers the hospice care, yes. The hospice team visits the resident in the facility just as it would at home, and the medications, equipment and supplies for the terminal illness are covered. It does not pay the facility's monthly rent, meals or personal care charges, which keep going as before. The exception is a short inpatient or respite stay that the hospice arranges, which Medicare does cover.
  - q: Can I keep my own doctor on hospice?

**Yes. Medicare Part A covers hospice care at home, there is no deductible, and for most families the bill comes to a copay of up to $5 per prescription for comfort medications and 5% of the cost of any inpatient respite stay.** The nurse visits, the aide, the social worker, the chaplain, the hospital bed, the oxygen and the grief counseling afterwards are all part of it. What hospice does not pay for is round-the-clock help in the house, or the rent and meals if the person lives in a care facility. And if you are on a Medicare Advantage plan, Original Medicare picks up the hospice bill anyway.

I am writing this one because it is the call nobody plans for. In Peoria it usually comes from an adult son or daughter who has just flown into Sky Harbor, is sitting at a parent's kitchen table in Westbrook Village or out in Vistancia, and has been handed a hospice brochure by a discharge planner at a hospital in Sun City or near Arrowhead. They want to know two things: what is this going to cost, and does the parent's insurance change anything. The answers are short, and they are kinder than most people expect.

## What the hospice benefit actually pays for

Hospice is care for someone whose doctor expects them to live six months or less if the illness runs its usual course, and who has chosen comfort over cure. It is not only for cancer. Heart failure, COPD, dementia and kidney disease are all common reasons, and in an older community like the 85345 core and the Sun City side of the line, they are most of them.

Once hospice is elected, the hospice team writes a plan of care, and Medicare covers everything in it that relates to the terminal illness:

- doctor and nursing visits, with a nurse and doctor on call 24 hours a day;
- hospice aide and homemaker visits;
- medical equipment such as a hospital bed, wheelchair or walker, and supplies such as dressings and catheters;
- drugs for pain and symptom control;
- physical, occupational and speech therapy where it helps comfort;
- social work, dietary counseling and spiritual support;
- short inpatient stays to get symptoms under control, and short respite stays to give a family caregiver a break;
- grief and loss counseling for the family afterwards.

There is also something most families never hear about: before choosing hospice at all, a person can have a one-time consultation with a hospice medical director or hospice doctor to talk through the options and how pain would be managed. Medicare covers it even if they decide not to go ahead.

Two practical notes. First, the $5 is a ceiling, not a fixed charge. Second, respite is meant to be occasional: up to five days at a time in a hospice facility, hospital or nursing home, so that a spouse who has been doing the caregiving for weeks can sleep.

## Why hospice at home is not 24-hour care

This is the part I most want families to hear before they agree to bring someone home, because it is where the plan and the reality part ways.

Federal rules recognise four levels of hospice care. Almost every day on hospice is the first one.

- **Routine home care.** The team visits on a schedule, a few times a week for the nurse and more often for the aide as needs grow. Between visits, the family is the caregiver.
- **Continuous home care.** During a crisis, when pain or breathing or agitation cannot be controlled any other way, the hospice can staff the home with predominantly nursing care for up to 24 hours a day. It has to be at least eight hours on a given day to count, and it ends when the crisis does.
- **General inpatient care.** A short stay in a hospice unit, hospital or skilled nursing facility for symptoms that cannot be managed at home.
- **Inpatient respite.** The caregiver's break, described above.

So a hospice nurse can be at the end of the phone at three in the morning, and can drive out. What Medicare will not do is put an aide in the house on the overnight shift for weeks so that an eighty-four-year-old spouse can sleep. Families who need that hire it privately, usually from a home care agency, and in Arizona that runs at about {{care.az.homeCareHourly}} an hour according to the {{care.surveyYear}} survey the site's [cost of care tool](/tools/cost-of-care/) draws on. Eight hours a night adds up fast.

That gap is where long-term care insurance earns its keep, if someone owns a policy. Many policies pay for home care aides, and some can be claimed alongside hospice rather than instead of it, with hospice covering the medical care and the policy covering the hands. Whether a particular policy does that is a question for its claims department, not for me, and the [long-term care post](/blog/is-long-term-care-insurance-worth-it-arizona/) explains what these policies are built to do. If there is no policy, the money comes from savings, and that is worth saying out loud early.

## Medicare Advantage: the hospice bill moves to Original Medicare

This surprises nearly everyone on an Advantage plan. The day hospice starts, the bill for everything related to the terminal illness goes to Original Medicare, not to the Advantage plan. The hospice does not need to be in the plan's network. No prior authorization from the plan is involved.

The person can stay enrolled in the Advantage plan, and usually should, as long as the plan premium keeps getting paid. The plan continues to cover:

- its extras, such as dental, vision and hearing benefits;
- care for health problems that have nothing to do with the terminal illness;
- drugs that are not part of the hospice plan, if the plan includes drug coverage.

For a few years, some Advantage plans were allowed to run hospice themselves under a federal demonstration. CMS ended that demonstration at 11:59 p.m. on December 31, 2024, so for {{year}} and 2027 the rule is the same everywhere: hospice is Original Medicare's job.

That has a consequence for this year's plan changes. If a parent in hospice is on one of the Maricopa County Advantage plans that are [ending on December 31](/blog/medicare-advantage-plans-ending-arizona-2027/), the hospice care itself does not stop and does not need a new plan to continue, because the plan was never paying for it. What the household has to sort out is the rest: drug coverage for unrelated prescriptions, and coverage for anything that is not part of the hospice plan. The [discontinued-plan calendar for Peoria households](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/) has the dates. A person whose plan ends gets a guaranteed right to buy certain Medigap plans with no health questions, and that right comes with no health questions at all.

## Original Medicare with a Medigap plan

On Original Medicare the hospice benefit is identical. The supplement mostly matters around the edges: it pays the small drug copays and the respite coinsurance, as the table shows, and it keeps paying its usual share of any care that is not hospice-related. For a spouse who is still healthy, it keeps doing what it always did.

The bigger difference is the one before hospice, not during it. Someone on Original Medicare with a supplement can see any doctor who takes Medicare for a second opinion, at Mayo, at Banner or out of state, without asking a plan for permission first. That is often the conversation that comes before the hospice one, and it is one reason affluent households weigh the [Advantage versus Medigap trade](/learn/medicare-advantage-vs-medigap/) the way they do.

## Snowbirds, out-of-state family and the summer

Three Peoria-specific wrinkles come up often enough to mention.

**A winter resident goes on hospice here, then wants to go home.** Hospice is delivered by a specific Medicare-approved provider that serves a specific area. A patient can change hospice providers once in each benefit period, and that is how a move back to Minnesota or Illinois is usually handled: the Peoria hospice and the hospice back home arrange a transfer. Ask the Peoria team to start that early, because the receiving hospice has to accept the patient before the flight, not after.

**Family flies in from out of state.** The hospice's social worker is there for the family as well as the patient, and grief support continues after a death, typically for about a year. Out-of-town children often do not realise this is part of what Medicare is paying for, and they should use it.

**The Arizona summer.** Room and board includes keeping the house livable. Medicare does not pay to run or repair an air conditioner, and a home hospice patient in a Peoria July cannot do without one. If a unit is old, deal with it in the spring.

## Choosing a hospice in north Peoria

Families are often handed one name at discharge and assume it is the only option. It usually is not. You are entitled to pick any Medicare-approved hospice that serves your address, whether that is 85383, 85382 or 85345. Medicare's [Care Compare](https://www.medicare.gov/care-compare/) lists them by ZIP code with family survey results, including how often families said they got help as soon as they needed it. That last measure tells you more about three-in-the-morning reality than any brochure.

If the patient is on an Advantage plan, the plan must give you a list of approved hospices in its area, but you are not limited to its network for the hospice care itself.

Three questions worth asking any hospice before you sign: how quickly a nurse actually arrives after a night call, how many aide visits a week they expect to provide at the start, and whether they waive the drug copay. The answers vary more than people expect.
