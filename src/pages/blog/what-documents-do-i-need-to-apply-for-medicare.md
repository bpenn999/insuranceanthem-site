**Often none at all. If Social Security already has your age on record, your Social Security number, your birth details and the dates of any employer health coverage are enough to apply. When proof is needed, it is a birth certificate recorded before age 5, proof of citizenship or lawful residence if Social Security cannot confirm it, and possibly a marriage certificate if you are claiming Part A on a spouse's work record.** People who are already drawing Social Security four months before their 65th birthday month need nothing; their card comes on its own.

I get this question most from people who moved to north Peoria in the last few years. The house in Vistancia is unpacked, but the birth certificate is in a fireproof box that is either in the hall closet or in a storage unit off Lake Pleasant Parkway, and nobody is sure which. They want to know whether to go looking before they apply. Usually the answer is: apply first, and let Social Security tell you what it is missing.

## Proof of age, and why you may never be asked for it

Medicare at 65 depends on being 65, so Social Security has to be satisfied about your date of birth. Its manual for Medicare claims simply points staff to the ordinary proof-of-age rules. The first step under those rules is to check your own record. If you established your age with Social Security before, on an earlier claim or when you applied for your number with a birth record, a code on your file says so, and you are generally not asked again unless something about the date looks wrong.

That is why two neighbors on the same street in 85383 can have opposite experiences. One applies online and is approved without sending a thing. The other, who got a Social Security number as a teenager with no birth record attached, gets a letter asking for proof.

When proof is needed, the federal rule ranks it. The preferred evidence is a birth certificate or hospital birth record recorded before age 5, or a religious record of birth made before age 5. If none exists, Social Security will look at other evidence that shows your age at some point in your life:

- school or census records;
- an insurance policy, a marriage record or a passport;
- employment or military records;
- a delayed birth certificate or your child's birth certificate;
- immigration or naturalization papers;
- an old family Bible entry or a signed statement from the doctor or midwife at your birth.

Without the preferred kind, expect to need at least two. Most north Peoria arrivals were born somewhere else, so a replacement birth certificate means ordering a certified copy from your birth state's vital records office, which can take weeks. If you know yours is missing, order it the month your seven-month window opens, not the month you apply.

## Originals, certified copies and what you get back

This is the part that catches careful people. A clear photocopy from your home printer is not acceptable evidence. Social Security can ask to see the original, and it accepts a copy only when it is certified as a true copy by one of a short list of people: the official custodian of the record (in practice, the vital records office that issued it), an authorized Social Security employee, a VA employee for records already given to the VA, a U.S. consular officer for records held abroad, or a state agency employee for records in that agency's own files.

Originals come back. The regulation says Social Security photocopies them and returns them to you. Still, you have two sensible choices:

1. **Order an extra certified copy** from the issuing office and mail that one, so the only original you own never travels.
2. **Take the original to a Social Security office** and have it copied and certified while you wait. It leaves your hand for a few minutes rather than a few weeks.

Do not mail a green card or a naturalization certificate you cannot easily replace without thinking through which of the two you prefer.

## If you were born outside the United States

Part B has a citizenship rule written into it. You must be a U.S. citizen, or a lawful permanent resident who has lived in the United States continuously for the five years before the month you apply. Social Security usually confirms U.S.-born citizens from its own records. A naturalized citizen may be asked for a naturalization certificate or a U.S. passport. A permanent resident should expect to show the green card and something that proves the five years.

Plenty of Peoria households fit here: a spouse who naturalized decades ago and has not seen the certificate since, or a parent who moved in from abroad to be near grandchildren and is now approaching 65. For the parent, the five-year clock is the question to settle before anything else, because no document fixes a residence period that has not been completed.

## Part A on a spouse's record

Premium-free Part A normally comes from 40 quarters of work. If you do not have them yourself, you may get it on a current spouse's record, a deceased spouse's record or, after a marriage of at least ten years, a former spouse's record. Without that, Part A costs up to {{partA.premiumIfUninsured}} a month in {{year}}. That makes the marriage certificate the single most valuable piece of paper in some households.

This comes up more often than people expect among higher-earning couples where one spouse spent years running the household or a family business that did not pay them a wage. For a current spouse, Social Security's evidence rule often settles for signed statements from both of you about when and where you married. Where there is any doubt, or the claim is on a former or late spouse's record, it wants a certified copy of the public or religious marriage record, or the original certificate. For a former spouse, add the divorce decree so the ten years can be counted. [Can my spouse get Medicare when I turn 65?](/blog/can-my-spouse-get-medicare-when-i-turn-65/) covers the age-gap side of the same question.

## Before you start online

Medicare.gov is plain about it: to sign up online you need a my Social Security account first. Create it a week or two before you plan to apply, because the identity check is the step most likely to stall, and you do not want to discover that in the last week of your window.

The application asks for:

- your Social Security number and date and place of birth;
- your citizenship status;
- whether you or your spouse have employer or union health coverage, and its start and end dates;
- whether you want Part B now or are delaying it because you are still covered through current work.

The last item is the one with consequences. If you are still working, read [Do I need to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I'm still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/) before you answer it. If you are also applying for retirement benefits on the same application, Social Security may ask for more, such as your most recent earnings, and those questions are about the benefit, not about Medicare.

## What you will not need

Nothing medical. Medicare does not ask about your health. It does not ask for tax returns either: if your income is high enough for IRMAA, Social Security receives that figure from the IRS on its own, about two years in arrears, as [How much does Medicare cost at 65?](/blog/how-much-does-medicare-cost-at-65/) explains.

The coverage you choose after the card arrives needs even less. A Medigap, Medicare Advantage or Part D application asks for your Medicare number and your Part A and Part B effective dates, and both are printed on the card. During your six-month Medigap open enrollment window, a Supplement cannot be refused or priced up for your health, and Arizona has no later window that does the same, so that card is worth acting on promptly. [When is Medicare Supplement open enrollment in Arizona?](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/) has the dates.

## A paperwork checklist for a spring 2027 birthday

1. **Find your window.** The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) gives you the first and last day for your birthday month.
2. **Two weeks before it opens:** create the my Social Security account, and look for the birth certificate, the marriage certificate and, if it applies, the naturalization certificate or green card.
3. **If any are missing:** order certified copies now from the issuing state. Out-of-state offices are the slow part.
4. **Write down your employer coverage dates** for yourself and your spouse, to the month.
5. **Apply early in the window,** online, and answer the Part B question deliberately.
6. **If a letter asks for evidence,** decide between mailing a certified copy and taking the original to an office.
7. **When the card arrives,** put it in front of the Medigap-or-Advantage decision while your six months are fresh.
