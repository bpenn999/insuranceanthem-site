**If you retire after 65 and start Part B then, your Medicare Advantage window opens three months before Part B starts and closes on the last day of the second month after it starts.** That is the Initial Coverage Election Period in [42 CFR §422.62(a)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-422/subpart-B/section-422.62). Part B starting January 1, 2027 means you can choose a plan any time from October 1, 2026 through March 31, 2027. Most guides say "two months after your employer coverage ends." That is a separate window, and it is the shorter of the two.

The longer window does not settle it, though. The date that matters more is your drug-coverage gap, which is covered further down.

I get this question most from north Peoria households where one spouse is still drawing a paycheck at 66 or 67. They planned the Part B paperwork carefully. Then the plan choice turned out to run on its own calendar. Here is how that calendar works.

## Why your window is set by Part B, not by your 65th birthday

Medicare Advantage needs both Part A and Part B. Most people who keep working past 65 take Part A at 65, because it is premium-free for most people with 40 quarters of work. They put Part B off until the job, and the job's health plan, ends. Until Part B begins you are not eligible for Medicare Advantage, so the federal rules treat the month Part B starts as your starting line.

The rule gives three things:

- **It opens three months before** the first month you have both Part A and Part B.
- **It closes on the later of** the last day of the second month after that month, or the last day of your Part B Initial Enrollment Period. If you are signing up at 67, the Initial Enrollment Period ended two years ago, so the first date is yours.
- **The plan starts the same day as Part B** if you apply beforehand ([42 CFR §422.68(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/422.68)). Apply in a month Part B is already running and the plan starts the 1st of the next month.

You need your Part B start date settled first. The forms (CMS-40B, plus the employer's CMS-L564) are covered in [how to sign up for Part B when you retire after 65](/blog/how-do-i-sign-up-for-medicare-part-b-when-i-retire-after-65/). Once Social Security confirms the start date, the plan application can go in.

## The two windows side by side

A retiree coming off employer coverage usually qualifies for two windows at once:

1. **The Initial Coverage Election Period** above, which you get the first time you have both parts.
2. **The employer-coverage Special Enrollment Period** at [42 CFR §422.62(b)(4)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-422/subpart-B/section-422.62). It ends two months after the month your group coverage ends. It works the same way for COBRA when COBRA ends.

You only need one of them. For a first-time enrollee the first one runs a month longer. Here is how it works out for three common retirement dates, assuming Part A is already active and Part B starts the day after your group coverage ends:

## The deadline that actually binds: 63 days without drug coverage

The last column is the one I build plans around. Medicare charges a [Part D late enrollment penalty](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/part-d/basics/costs) if you go **63 days or more in a row** without creditable drug coverage after you first become eligible. That covers a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage, a standalone Part D plan, or other coverage at least as good as Part D. The penalty is added to your premium every month for as long as you have Part D.

Take employer coverage that ends December 31:

- A plan starting **March 1** leaves a 59-day gap. No penalty.
- A plan starting **April 1** leaves a 90-day gap. Penalty. That is true even though March was still inside your election window.

So the window on paper runs to March 31, but in practice you should decide by the end of February. The exception is COBRA. If you took COBRA and it includes drug coverage, it usually counts as creditable for Part D, even though it does not protect your Part B enrollment ([more on that split](/blog/can-i-keep-cobra-instead-of-medicare-at-65/)). Ask the COBRA administrator for the creditable-coverage notice and keep it.

## Your one change, and why joining late uses it up

People new to Medicare Advantage get one do-over. Under [42 CFR §422.62(a)(3)(ii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-422/subpart-B/section-422.62), you can make one change from the month you have both Part A and Part B through the last day of the third month. You can switch to a different Medicare Advantage plan, or go back to Original Medicare and add a drug plan.

That clock starts with **Part B**, not with your plan. Part B on January 1 means the change has to happen by March 31. Pick a plan that starts January 1 and you get three months to find out whether your Arrowhead-area specialists are really in network. Pick one that starts March 1 and you get one month. That is one more reason to decide before Part B starts, not at the end of the window.

## What north Peoria retirees are choosing from in 2027

For the Maricopa County part of Peoria, CMS's 2027 plan landscape files list **45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage from 13 companies**, not counting special needs plans. That is one fewer plan than 2026, with the same number of companies. Eight 2026 plans ended and new ones arrived, so a colleague's advice from last year may point you to a plan that no longer exists. Part of Peoria north of Lake Pleasant sits in Yavapai County, which has a much shorter list. Check the county on your tax bill, not just your ZIP ([the Yavapai difference is laid out here](/blog/are-there-ppo-medicare-advantage-plans-in-peoria-az-2027/)).

The question I start with for someone coming off a large employer's plan is not the premium. 39 of those 45 plans have a $0 premium on top of Part B, which is {{partB.premium}} a month in {{figYear}}. The question is the network. Employer plans in the Valley tend to be broad PPOs, and most Maricopa Medicare Advantage plans are HMOs. Some network changes also take effect January 1. You are not limited to Medicare Advantage. Part B starting also opens your six-month Medicare Supplement open enrollment, so the comparison between the two paths is open at the same time ([how that choice usually breaks down](/blog/medigap-or-medicare-advantage-at-65-arizona/)).

## One more thing retirement changes: IRMAA

If your salary pushed you into an income-related premium, your first Medicare bills may be based on a working year's tax return. Medicare looks back two years. Retirement counts as a life-changing event, and Form SSA-44 asks Social Security to use your lower income instead ([how the appeal works](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/)). The plan you pick does not change IRMAA either way. It is billed the same on Medicare Advantage as on Original Medicare. Your tax advisor should run the income side.

## The order I would do it in

1. **Confirm the end date** of your group coverage, in writing, from HR.
2. **File for Part B** so it starts the 1st of the month after that date, and get the confirmation from Social Security.
3. **Get the creditable-coverage notices** for your employer's drug coverage, and for COBRA if you take it.
4. **Shortlist plans by network first.** List every doctor, hospital and prescription you use.
5. **Apply in the three months before Part B starts**, so the plan starts the same day and your one-change clock has its full three months.
6. **Diary the outside dates:** the end of the second month after Part B (the window closes), and day 62 without drug coverage (the penalty starts).
