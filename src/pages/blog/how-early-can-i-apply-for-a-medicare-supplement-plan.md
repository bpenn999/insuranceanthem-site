**You can apply for a Medicare Supplement before your six-month open enrollment window even begins, and federal law protects you when you do. The statute bars an insurer from turning you down or charging more for health reasons when an application is submitted "prior to or during" the six months that start the first day of the month you are 65 or older and enrolled in Part B.** There is no federal earliest date. Each company sets its own lead time, and the policy itself still cannot start before Part B does.

That last sentence is the whole point of applying early. Most people I talk to in north Peoria assume they have to wait until their birthday month, sign up, and then go looking. The ones who apply a few months ahead get a Supplement that begins on the same morning as Part B, and a decision made while nothing is rushing them.

## What the law actually says about timing

The rule sits in the Social Security Act, at 42 U.S.C. § 1395ss(s)(2)(A). Paraphrased only lightly: an issuer may not deny or condition a Medigap policy, or discriminate in its price, because of health status, claims experience, receipt of health care or a medical condition, for anyone "for whom an application is submitted prior to or during the 6 month period" that begins with the first month they are 65 or older and enrolled in Part B.

Two words in that sentence do the work. "During" is the six-month window most articles describe. "Prior to" is the part that gets skipped, and it is why you are not stuck waiting. An application made in December for a policy that starts with your March Part B is inside the protection, even though your window will not open until March 1.

Medicare.gov describes the same window from the other side: the best time to buy is the six months that start once you are 65 and have Part B, because during it a company cannot use medical underwriting. Nothing in that guidance says you have to wait for day one.

## How far ahead the companies will take it

This is where the law stops and company practice starts. The statute protects an early application but does not force any insurer to accept one on a particular date. Each company decides how far ahead of your Part B start it will take your paperwork.

In my own work the range runs from roughly three months to roughly six months before the Part B date, and it changes from company to company and occasionally from year to year. So the useful question to ask is not "when does my window open" but "what is the earliest date you will take my application for a policy starting on my Part B date." Get the answer from each company you are comparing, because the one with the shortest lead time sets your calendar.

What the company will usually want from you:

- **Your Medicare number** and your Part A and Part B effective dates. The number often shows up in your online Medicare account before the card reaches your mailbox, which [When will I get my Medicare card?](/blog/when-will-i-get-my-medicare-card/) explains.
- **The effective date you want**, which should be the first day Part B is in force.
- **Proof of earlier coverage**, if you are coming off an employer plan. That matters for the pre-existing condition question below.

If you apply before Social Security has finished processing your Part B enrollment, expect the company to hold the application until it can confirm Part B, or to ask you for the confirmation letter. That is normal and does not cost you the protection.

## If you are still working and delaying Part B

The six months do not open at 65. They open the first month you are both 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. If you are holding off on Part B because you have group coverage from a current job at a large employer, your window has not started, and nothing is being used up while you wait.

That makes the early application more valuable, not less, because the retirement date is usually known months in advance. The order I suggest:

1. **Settle the date your job coverage ends**, in writing from HR.
2. **Set Part B to start the first of the following month.** You will use the special enrollment period for people leaving work, which [How do I sign up for Medicare Part B when I retire after 65?](/blog/how-do-i-sign-up-for-medicare-part-b-when-i-retire-after-65/) walks through, form by form.
3. **Apply for the Supplement a few months ahead** with that Part B date as the effective date.
4. **Keep the employer plan until the new coverage is in force.** Never cancel first and apply second.

Whether you can delay Part B at all depends on the size of the employer and on whose job the coverage comes from. [Do I need to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I'm still working?](/blog/do-i-need-medicare-at-65-if-still-working/) covers that test.

**The pre-existing condition question usually disappears here.** The same statute lets a Supplement exclude a pre-existing condition for up to its first six months, but only if you lacked creditable coverage. If you had at least six months of continuous creditable coverage as of the date you apply, the policy cannot exclude anything for a pre-existing condition. If you had less, the exclusion has to shrink month for month by the coverage you did have. Someone moving straight from a group plan to Medicare almost always has no waiting period at all. Bring the proof.

## What an early application locks in, and what it does not

**It locks in your acceptance.** Under the "prior to" language, a diagnosis or a hospital stay between the day you apply and the day the policy starts cannot be used to decline you or price you up. For a 64-year-old with a heart workup on the calendar or a knee that is clearly heading for surgery, that is the real value of going early.

**It does not necessarily lock in the price.** A Supplement's premium is set by the company's filed rates for your age, ZIP and plan letter, and companies file rate changes. If one takes effect between your application and your start date, you may see a different first bill than the quote. Ask the company directly whether the quoted premium is guaranteed to your effective date. How the premium moves after that depends on how the policy is rated, which [Attained-age vs. issue-age Medicare Supplement pricing in Arizona](/blog/attained-age-vs-issue-age-medicare-supplement-arizona/) explains.

**It does not commit you forever.** Federal law, at § 1395ss(c)(4), requires that a Medigap policy can be returned within at least 30 days after it is issued, with a full refund of any premium paid, however it was sold. Before the policy is issued you can generally withdraw the application.

And one thing nobody's application can change: there is no birthday rule in Arizona and no yearly switching right. Once your six months close, a later application is a health application. That is why the window, and getting the application in on time, matters more here than in states that have added extra chances.

## The drug plan has to be timed separately

A Medicare Supplement does not cover prescriptions. If you take the Supplement route, you also need a stand-alone Part D plan, and that enrollment runs on its own clock: your initial enrollment period for Medicare, the seven months around your 65th birthday month, or a special enrollment period if you are leaving job coverage.

The price picture is different this year. **According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, Arizona has nine stand-alone Part D plans for 2027 and none of them has a $0 premium, where four did for 2026.** The lowest is $5.30 a month before any IRMAA surcharge. Line the drug plan up to start the same day as Part B and the Supplement. Three start dates on one morning is the goal.

## Applying early from north Peoria, or from somewhere else

A lot of households in 85383 apply from two addresses at once. They are closing on a house in Vistancia or Blackstone, finishing a last winter between two states, or still getting mail forwarded from the Midwest. Supplement premiums are filed by area, so **apply using the Arizona ZIP you will live in on the effective date**, not the address you happen to be at when you fill out the form. Plan prices can differ along the Peoria, Glendale and Sun City seam, which [Do Medicare plans change by ZIP code in Peoria?](/blog/medicare-plans-by-zip-code-peoria-az/) maps out.

The reason those households tend to want a Supplement at all is the same reason timing matters: it pays wherever Original Medicare pays, so the specialist at Arrowhead and the clinic near a summer place in Minnesota are on equal footing. That choice is only fully yours inside the six months. ## A short checklist, starting this month

1. **Pin down your Part B start date.** Birthday month, sign-up date, or retirement date, whichever applies.
2. **Write down the last day of your six months.** Count six months from the first day Part B is in force.
3. **Ask each company you are comparing for its earliest application date** for a policy starting on your Part B date.
4. **Gather your Medicare number, your Part A and B dates and proof of prior coverage.**
5. **Ask whether the quoted premium is guaranteed to the effective date.**
6. **Enroll in a Part D plan** with the same start date.
7. **Keep every current policy in force** until the new ones are.
