**Yes, under Original Medicare, when the Botox is given in a hospital outpatient department. Botox for chronic migraine and for eyelid or facial spasm has needed prior authorization there since July 1, 2020. A final rule CMS published on November 6, 2026 adds eight more Botox injection codes to that list, for injections given on or after July 1, 2027.** The new codes cover the neck, the arms and legs, the trunk, the voice box and the salivary glands. Injections given in a doctor's own office, a critical access hospital or an ambulatory surgery center are outside the program, and so is every Medicare Advantage plan, which runs its own authorization rules instead.

That is the national rule, and it applies in Arizona the same way it applies everywhere else. What it means for a particular patient depends on two things: where the injection is billed, and which kind of Medicare the patient has. In Peoria, those two answers can change at the same time this winter. The new code list starts six months after the 2027 plan year does, and the people most likely to be affected are already getting Botox on a schedule.

## What changes on July 1, 2027

Since 2020, CMS has run a prior authorization program for a short list of hospital outpatient services it considers prone to overuse: eyelid surgery, nose surgery, removing excess skin, vein ablation and Botox. Cervical spine fusion and spinal cord stimulators joined in 2021, and facet joint injections in 2023. The Botox entry has always covered two procedures and four drug codes:

- **64612:** injections into muscles controlled by the facial nerve on one side, the code for blepharospasm (eyelid spasm) and hemifacial spasm
- **64615:** injections across both sides of the face, head and neck, the code used for chronic migraine
- **J0585 to J0588:** the drug itself, whichever of the four botulinum toxin brands is used

The new rule leaves those in place and adds eight codes. In explaining why, CMS cited claim counts for seven injection procedures:

The final list of eight is published as a table in the rule. The seven above are the procedures CMS analysed. Across those codes, hospital outpatient claims rose 42.8% between 2017 and 2024, from about 43,500 to about 62,103, while total hospital outpatient claims fell 8.3% over the same period. CMS puts the codes at about $102 million a year in Medicare payments. It acknowledged that new FDA-approved uses explain some of the growth but said they did not explain all of it, and it finalized the change over objections from physician groups.

One thing CMS specifically left out: Botox injected into the bladder for overactive bladder is not among the new codes.

## Who this touches, and who it does not

The program is narrower than "Medicare needs prior authorization for Botox" makes it sound. CMS's own FAQ draws the lines:

- **Hospital outpatient departments only.** That includes clinics owned by a hospital system that bill Medicare as hospital outpatient departments, which is common with hospital-system specialty practices. A neurologist billing from a private office is not in the program, and neither are critical access hospitals or ambulatory surgery centers.
- **Original Medicare only.** CMS's FAQ answers the Advantage question in one word: no. This process applies only to fee-for-service claims.
- **Emergency department care is excluded.**
- **Medicare as secondary payer is included.** If you are still working and your employer plan pays first, the hospital still needs the approval before it bills Medicare for the balance.

The quickest way to find out which side of the line your clinic is on is to ask whether the visit will be billed as a hospital outpatient visit. If your statements show a separate facility charge from a hospital, it almost certainly is.

## How the approval works and how long it takes

The hospital, or the physician on its behalf, sends the request and medical records to the Medicare contractor before the injection. For Arizona that is Noridian, which publishes a checklist of what the records have to show. Since January 1, 2025, a standard request has to be decided within 7 calendar days. An expedited review, for cases where waiting could seriously jeopardize your health or recovery, takes 2 business days. CMS says the average decision over the past year took 3.6 days.

Three details matter to a patient on a repeat schedule:

- **An approval lasts 120 days.** After that the hospital has to ask again, so someone getting injections every three months should expect the paperwork to come up again and again, not once.
- **A denial can be resubmitted as often as needed** before the claim is billed. It cannot itself be appealed, but if the hospital bills anyway and the claim is denied, that denial can be appealed.
- **Skipping the step gets the claim denied.** CMS makes the approval the hospital's job and a condition of payment. If a clinic hands you an Advance Beneficiary Notice before a Botox injection, it is telling you it expects Medicare may not pay, and you could be the one billed. Ask why before you sign it.

The Medigap row is where people are most often surprised. A supplement is the cleanest way to avoid an insurer's own approval process, and that is still true. What it cannot do is override Medicare. From July 1, 2027, a Plan G member getting neck or limb injections at a hospital-based clinic goes through the same prior authorization as anyone else on Original Medicare. The difference is that once Medicare says yes, the bill is close to zero, and the clinic can be any one in the country that takes Medicare.

On the Advantage side, nothing in this rule changes anything, because the plans were never in the program. They decide for themselves. According to CMS's 2027 plan landscape files, Maricopa County has 45 general Advantage plans with drug coverage next year, and each publishes its own list of Part B drugs that need approval. A plan can also require you to try a cheaper treatment first. If Botox is part of your routine, look it up on the plan's prior authorization list before December 7, not after the first denial in January.

## What a Peoria patient should do before December 7

Most of the Botox patients I talk to are not shopping for Botox coverage. They are shopping for a plan, and the injections come up halfway through the conversation. For a north Peoria household, the drive matters as much as the plan. From Vistancia or the Lake Pleasant Parkway and Happy Valley corridor, neurology usually means Arrowhead, Sun City or further into the Valley, and the clinic you end up at is often a hospital-system practice.

- **Ask the clinic how it bills.** Hospital outpatient or physician office decides whether the July change touches you at all on Original Medicare.
- **If you are on an Advantage plan that ends December 31,** your new plan will want its own approval for the next injection. Have the neurologist's office request it in December, and check that the clinic is in the new plan's network. My post on [what to do if your plan is discontinued](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/) has the dates.
- **If you are weighing a move from Advantage to a supplement,** remember that Arizona has no Medigap birthday rule, so outside a guaranteed-issue window the supplement application is medically underwritten, and a neurological condition treated with Botox can come up. [Can I switch from Medicare Advantage to Medigap in Arizona?](/blog/can-i-switch-from-medicare-advantage-to-medigap-in-arizona/) covers when that door is open.
- **If you are on a supplement already,** nothing changes until July. After that, the clinic files the request and you wait up to a week. Ask the office to file early for your first appointment after July 1.

## The short version

- Original Medicare already requires prior authorization for Botox for chronic migraine and eyelid or facial spasm in hospital outpatient departments, and has since July 2020.
- From July 1, 2027, eight more Botox codes join the list: neck, arms and legs, trunk, voice box and salivary glands. Bladder injections are not included.
- Doctor's offices, ambulatory surgery centers, critical access hospitals and emergency care are outside the program. So is Medicare Advantage, which sets its own rules.
- Decisions take up to 7 calendar days, or 2 business days if expedited, and last 120 days. A Medigap plan pays only after Medicare approves.
