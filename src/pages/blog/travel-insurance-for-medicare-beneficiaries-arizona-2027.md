**If the trip leaves the United States, almost certainly yes.** Medicare was built to pay American hospitals and American doctors, and outside the country it pays for very little. What you may have on top of it — a Medicare Supplement, or a Medicare Advantage plan with a worldwide emergency extra — helps with an emergency and then stops, with a cap, a clock, or both. Nothing in Medicare pays to fly you home.

This comes up every autumn in Anthem and across the north Valley, because the people asking are the people booking: a river cruise next spring, a month in Portugal, the grandkids' wedding in Cabo, the standing Rocky Point weekend, the dental run to Los Algodones. The question is always some version of "I have Medicare, so I'm covered, right?" The honest answer is that it depends which layer you are standing on, so here they are one at a time.

## What Original Medicare pays outside the U.S.

Start with what counts as "the U.S." Medicare.gov's definition is the 50 states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands and American Samoa. A cruise that stops in San Juan or St. Thomas is still inside Medicare. Cozumel, Vancouver and Ensenada are not.

Outside that line, Original Medicare pays for hospital care in exactly three situations:

1. **You are in the U.S. when a medical emergency happens, and the foreign hospital is closer** than the nearest U.S. hospital that can treat you.
2. **You are travelling through Canada between Alaska and another state**, by the most direct route and without unreasonable delay, and a Canadian hospital is closest.
3. **You live in the U.S. and a foreign hospital is closer to your home** than the nearest U.S. hospital that can treat your condition, emergency or not.

None of those is a vacation. If the emergency begins after you have landed in Lisbon or crossed at Lukeville, Medicare's answer is no.

Two more rules round it out. On a **cruise ship**, Part B can pay for medically necessary care from the ship's doctor only while the ship is in a U.S. port or **no more than six hours** from one. And **Part D does not cover prescriptions bought abroad** — not from a stand-alone drug plan and not from the drug coverage inside an Advantage plan — and the money you spend at a foreign pharmacy does not count toward the {{partD.cap}} cap. The one travel item Part D does pay for is vaccines: every vaccine the CDC's advisory committee recommends, including travel vaccines, at no cost before you go.

## What a Medigap plan adds

Medicare Supplement **Plans C, D, F, G, M and N** carry a standardised foreign travel emergency benefit, identical from every carrier because the benefit is set by federal rule. After a **$250 annual deductible**, the plan pays **80 percent** of the billed charges for medically necessary emergency care outside the U.S., provided the emergency **begins within the first 60 days of the trip**, up to a **lifetime limit of $50,000**. Plans A, B, K and L do not include it.

That benefit is worth having, and for a short trip it may be all you need. But read the three limits again as a traveler rather than as a policyholder:

- **80 percent means you pay 20 percent**, and foreign hospitals generally want payment up front. Twenty percent of a cardiac admission in a private European hospital is a real number.
- **$50,000 is for your lifetime**, not per trip and not per year. One serious event can use most of it.
- **Day 61 and the benefit is gone.** A snowbird winter in Baja, or a long stay with family abroad, outruns it.

And it is emergency care only. No evacuation, no repatriation, no trip interruption, no routine visit for a prescription refill. [Plan G and Plan N](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/) carry the identical benefit, so travel is not a reason to pick one over the other.

## What a Medicare Advantage plan may add

Medicare requires every Advantage plan to cover emergency and urgent care anywhere inside the United States, in network or not. Outside the country the plan only has to follow Medicare's rules, which means the three narrow situations above. Anything beyond that is a **supplemental benefit** the plan chose to add, and many plans sold in Maricopa County do add a worldwide emergency and urgent care benefit.

How it is written varies from plan to plan, which is the whole point of checking:

- **A copay**, often the same as or higher than the plan's U.S. emergency room copay.
- **An annual dollar cap** on what the plan will pay abroad. The figure is in the Evidence of Coverage, and it differs across plans.
- **Emergency and urgent care only.** No routine care, no follow-up, no prescriptions.
- **Usually no transportation.** A few plans include emergency transport abroad; most do not, and none is an evacuation policy.
- **It can change every January 1.** The benefit is one row on the Annual Notice of Change your plan mailed by September 30, and a plan may trim or remove it for {{nextYear}}. [Here is how to find that row and what to do if it changed](/blog/medicare-advantage-worldwide-emergency-coverage-arizona-2027/).

The pattern in that table is the answer to the headline. Medicare and the things built on it will handle an emergency up to a point and then hand the bill back to you. A travel medical policy is written for the part they hand back: the 20 percent, the amount over the cap, the day-61 problem, and the flight home.

## What a travel medical policy actually does

The product worth knowing about is **travel medical insurance**, which is different from the trip-cancellation coverage a cruise line or airline tries to sell at checkout. Trip cancellation refunds a fare. Travel medical pays a hospital, and the better plans pay it directly rather than reimbursing you later.

Three things in that table matter for someone on Medicare:

**"Primary health plan required" is the Medicare question.** GeoBlue's own FAQ defines a primary plan as a group, individual or **governmental health plan such as Medicare**, and says Medicaid and VA coverage do not qualify. So Original Medicare, with or without a supplement, and a Medicare Advantage plan generally satisfy it. Confirm it on the application rather than assuming.

**Pre-existing conditions are the dividing line between plans.** The Gold single-trip plan skips the primary-plan requirement and skips pre-existing conditions with it. For most people past 65 that trade is the wrong one — the diagnosis you already have is the one most likely to send you to a hospital abroad.

**Age changes the maximum, not the eligibility.** On the annual plans the medical maximum drops to $200,000 from age 70. That is still four times the Medigap lifetime figure, and the evacuation benefit sits on top of it.

A multi-trip plan is the shape that fits the Rocky Point household and the couple who sees family abroad twice a year. A single-trip plan fits the one big trip, and the $0 deductible option is the version that pairs most cleanly with a Medigap plan, because the Medigap 20 percent is exactly the kind of bill a $0-deductible travel policy is there to absorb.

## A pre-trip checklist for the Medicare traveler

1. **Decide which layer you are on.** Original Medicare alone, Medigap, or Advantage — and if Advantage, open the Evidence of Coverage to the worldwide emergency row and write down the copay and the cap.
2. **Check the clock.** More than 60 days out of the country on a Medigap plan, or past your Advantage plan's consecutive-day limit, means the Medicare-side benefit is gone before you come home.
3. **Add up what is not covered.** The 20 percent, anything over the cap, and the flight home. That is the number a travel medical policy is quoting against.
4. **Confirm pre-existing conditions are covered** on whatever travel policy you consider, and that Medicare satisfies its primary-plan requirement.
5. **Carry the documents.** Your Medicare card does nothing abroad, but the travel policy's card and 24-hour assistance number do. Keep every itemised receipt in case you file a Medigap claim afterward.
6. **Fill prescriptions before you leave.** Part D will not pay for anything bought abroad, and most plans allow an early vacation fill if you ask.
7. **Read the {{nextYear}} Annual Notice of Change** before the trip if you are on an Advantage plan. [The worldwide emergency row](/blog/medicare-advantage-worldwide-emergency-coverage-arizona-2027/) is the one that changes quietly.

## The short version

- **Original Medicare pays for care abroad in three situations**, none of which is a vacation. It pays on a cruise ship only within six hours of a U.S. port, and Part D pays for no prescription bought abroad.
- **Medigap Plans C, D, F, G, M and N pay 80 percent of emergency care** after a $250 deductible, in the first 60 days of a trip, up to $50,000 in a lifetime. No evacuation.
- **Some Medicare Advantage plans add a worldwide emergency benefit** with a copay and an annual cap. It can change every January 1.
- **A travel medical policy covers what those leave behind** — the 20 percent, the amount over the cap, long trips, and medical evacuation — and the right ones cover pre-existing conditions and treat Medicare as a qualifying primary plan.
- **Buy travel medical, not just trip cancellation.** One refunds a fare. The other pays a hospital.
