**Most people on Medicare do not need cancer insurance, and the state regulators who write the consumer guides say so directly: "If you have Medicare and want more insurance, a comprehensive Medicare supplement policy is what you need."** Medicare already pays for chemotherapy, radiation, surgery and cancer drugs. With a Medigap Plan G on top, your share of a cancer year comes down to the Part B deductible ({{partB.deductible}} in {{year}}) and your drug costs, which Part D caps at {{partD.cap}} this year and $2,400 in 2027. The case for a separate cancer policy is narrower: a Medicare Advantage plan with a high out-of-pocket limit, a Medigap door that has already closed, or a household that wants cash for the costs no health plan touches.

I get this question most from north Peoria, usually from someone in Vistancia or along the Happy Valley corridor who just watched a neighbor go through treatment. The worry is fair. The answer depends almost entirely on which kind of Medicare you have, so that is where to start.

## What Medicare already pays when the diagnosis is cancer

Medicare has no separate cancer benefit and no cancer exclusion. Cancer care is paid the same way as any other covered care:

- **Part A** covers inpatient hospital stays, including surgery that requires an admission.
- **Part B** covers chemotherapy given in a clinic, infusion center or doctor's office, radiation therapy, imaging, office visits and most outpatient surgery. You pay {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount after the Part B deductible.
- **Part D** covers most oral cancer drugs taken at home. A few oral drugs that have an intravenous equivalent are paid under Part B instead.

The weak point in Original Medicare alone is that the {{partB.coinsurance}} share has no annual ceiling. A year of infusions or daily radiation can produce a coinsurance bill that keeps growing as long as treatment does. Everything below is about how that open-ended share gets closed, and whether a cancer policy has any job left to do once it is. The [Peoria cancer treatment post](/blog/does-medicare-cover-cancer-treatment-peoria-az/) walks through the coverage itself in more detail.

## With Medigap Plan G, a cancer policy mostly duplicates what you have

Medigap Plan G pays the Part A deductible, the hospital coinsurance and the {{partB.coinsurance}} Part B coinsurance on every Medicare-covered service, with no network. It pays the same whether the claim is a knee, a heart or a tumor. What you are left with is short:

1. The Part B deductible, once a year.
2. Your Part D costs, capped at $2,400 in 2027.
3. Your premiums.

That is a known number before anything happens. A cancer policy layered on top would pay benefits for bills that are already paid. That is why both the Wisconsin guide (prepared by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners) and the Alabama Department of Insurance tell Medicare beneficiaries to buy a comprehensive supplement before anything disease-specific. Plan N works almost the same way, with small office and emergency room copays.

There is one honest exception for a Medigap household: **money for the costs that are not medical bills.** Medicare does not pay for gas, parking, a hotel near a treatment center, a spouse's lost wages, or help around the house during recovery. From north Peoria, specialist cancer care can mean regular trips across the Valley. If the reason you are asking is "I want a cushion for all of that," a lump-sum policy can do that. It is a want, not a gap in your health coverage, and it should be priced as one.

## With Medicare Advantage, your out-of-pocket limit decides it

An Advantage plan replaces the open-ended {{partB.coinsurance}} with copays and coinsurance up to an **in-network maximum out-of-pocket limit** for the year. Once your medical costs reach that limit, the plan pays 100% of covered in-network care until January 1. Part B chemotherapy drugs count toward it. Part D drugs do not; they run against the separate drug cap.

So the worst-case medical year on an Advantage plan is the out-of-pocket limit plus up to $2,400 in drugs. And that limit is getting higher in Maricopa County. The middle of the menu did not move. The top end did: more plans now sit at $5,000 and above, and the highest limit went up $1,000. A Peoria household on one of those plans has a worst-case medical year in the high four figures before a single prescription. That is the household for whom a cancer policy's lump sum lines up with a real, measurable exposure. The [out-of-pocket maximum post](/blog/medicare-advantage-out-of-pocket-maximum-peoria-az-2027/) shows how to find your plan's number.

### The calendar catch nobody mentions

The out-of-pocket limit resets every January 1. Cancer treatment does not follow the calendar. A diagnosis in September, with surgery in October and chemotherapy through March, can hit the limit once in the first year and then start counting again in January. The drug cap resets on the same date. On a plan with a high limit, a single course of treatment can cost close to two years' worth. A Medigap household facing the same dates pays a second Part B deductible and nothing else on the medical side.

## The Medigap door, and why timing matters in Arizona

The usual reason an Advantage member asks about cancer insurance, instead of switching to Medigap, is that switching is no longer simple. Outside your first six months on Part B and a few protected situations, a Medigap company in Arizona can ask health questions and decline you. Arizona has no birthday rule or annual switching window for supplements. A recent or current cancer diagnosis is one of the clearest reasons an application gets declined.

That leaves three groups:

- **Turning 65, or within six months of starting Part B.** Your Medigap open enrollment is guaranteed. Compare Plan G against the Advantage menu before you look at any disease-specific policy, and use the [enrollment timeline](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) to see your window.
- **On an Advantage plan that is being discontinued for 2027.** You have guaranteed-issue rights to certain Medigap plans for a limited time. The [discontinued-plan post](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/) has the dates. If cancer is the worry, that window matters more than any cancer policy.
- **Healthy, on an Advantage plan, and past those windows.** You can still apply for Medigap with underwriting. [Switching from Advantage to Medigap in Arizona](/blog/can-i-switch-from-medicare-advantage-to-medigap-in-arizona/) explains how that works. If you would not pass, or the premium does not make sense, this is the group where a cancer policy is a reasonable way to cover part of a high out-of-pocket limit.

## What to read in a cancer policy before you buy one

Cancer insurance is not Medicare and not a Medicare Supplement. It is a limited-benefit policy that pays only when cancer is diagnosed. The state guides list the same warnings, and in my experience they are the right ones:

- **It will not cover a cancer you already have.** No policy pays for a cancer diagnosed before you applied.
- **There is usually a waiting period.** Some policies pay nothing for a diagnosis in the first 30 days, or the first several months, after the policy starts.
- **Lump sum or schedule.** Some pay a single benefit on first diagnosis. Others pay set dollar amounts per hospital day, per treatment or per procedure. A per-day hospital benefit is worth much less when most cancer care is outpatient, as it usually is now.
- **Fixed dollars do not grow.** The benefit you buy at 66 is the benefit you have at 80, while out-of-pocket limits and drug caps tend to rise.
- **Check coordination language.** Many policies advertise that they pay no matter what other insurance pays. The guides warn that some contain coordination-of-benefits clauses anyway. Read the definitions page.

The broader category includes cancer, heart attack and stroke policies, which pay a lump sum on one of several named diagnoses. For an Advantage member with a high out-of-pocket limit, a policy that responds to all three often fits better than one built around cancer alone. I am not going to quote premiums here; they depend on age, benefit amount and the company, and the only useful comparison is against your own plan's limit.

## A quick way to decide

- **On Medigap Plan G or Plan N:** you very likely do not need it. Buy it only if you want cash for travel, lodging or lost income, and know that is what you are buying.
- **On an Advantage plan with a limit near the $2,000 to $4,150 end:** your worst case is already modest. Building savings may do the same job.
- **On an Advantage plan with a limit of $5,000 or more, past your Medigap window:** this is where a cancer, or cancer-heart-stroke, policy most often earns its premium. Compare it against the cost of switching to a lower-limit plan before December 7.
- **Turning 65:** settle Medigap or Advantage first. The cancer question is much smaller once that decision is made.
