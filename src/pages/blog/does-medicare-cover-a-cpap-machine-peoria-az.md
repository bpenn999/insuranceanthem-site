**Yes. Medicare Part B covers a CPAP machine for obstructive sleep apnea, along with the mask, tubing and supplies, and you pay {{partB.coinsurance}} of the Medicare-approved amount after the {{partB.deductible}} Part B deductible.** The catch is that coverage comes in stages. Medicare pays for a 12-week trial first, keeps paying only if the machine's own usage data shows you wear it, rents it for 13 months, and then hands it to you.

The phone call I get about this is rarely "will Medicare pay?" It is "my supplier just told me Medicare stopped paying, and I don't know why." In north Peoria it usually comes from someone who moved into Vistancia or the 85383 corridor with a machine from an employer plan back in Illinois, or who started therapy last spring and quietly gave up during a hot, restless July. Both situations have a rule behind them, and both are fixable if you know which rule you hit.

## What Medicare needs before it pays for a CPAP

Four things have to line up, and the supplier will check each of them before the first box ships:

1. **A sleep study that shows obstructive sleep apnea.** Medicare accepts an overnight study in a sleep lab or a home sleep test using an approved device. The result has to show 15 or more breathing events an hour, or 5 to 14 an hour along with a documented problem such as daytime sleepiness, insomnia, mood or memory trouble, high blood pressure, heart disease or a past stroke.
2. **A clinical evaluation and an order** from your treating doctor, nurse practitioner or physician assistant.
3. **A doctor and a supplier who are both enrolled in Medicare.** If either one is not, Medicare may not pay its share at all.
4. **A supplier that accepts assignment for every month of the rental.** If it does not, you can be asked to pay the full cost up front and wait for Medicare to reimburse its part.

Point four is the one worth asking about by name. A participating supplier can bill you only the deductible and coinsurance. Ask "do you accept Medicare assignment on CPAP rentals?" before anything is delivered.

## The 90-day usage test that ends most CPAP coverage

This is where coverage actually stops for most people, and it has nothing to do with the plan you chose.

Modern machines record every night you use them. Medicare's rule is that during **some 30-day stretch in your first three months**, you used the machine **at least four hours a night on at least 70% of nights**. That works out to 21 nights out of 30. Your treating practitioner then has to see you in person, **no sooner than day 31 and no later than day 91**, review that usage report and note that your symptoms have improved.

If you fail the trial, Medicare treats the machine as not medically necessary and stops paying for it and its supplies. Starting again is possible, but it takes a new in-person evaluation to work out why therapy did not take, plus a **repeat sleep study in a sleep lab**, not at home. That is a real cost and a real wait, so if the mask is miserable in week two, call the supplier and the doctor in week two. A mask change is routine. A failed trial is not.

## What you pay for the machine and the supplies

On Original Medicare, the arithmetic is simple even if the rental is not:

- Each month of the 13-month rental, Medicare pays 80% of the approved amount and you pay {{partB.coinsurance}}, once you have met the {{partB.deductible}} Part B deductible for the year.
- The mask, cushions, tubing, filters and humidifier parts are covered on the same terms, on a fixed replacement schedule.
- After the 13th payment the machine belongs to you, and only the supplies keep billing.

With a **Medicare Supplement**, most of that {{partB.coinsurance}} disappears. Plan G pays the Part B coinsurance in full, leaving you the {{partB.deductible}} deductible, which most people meet early in the year anyway. Plan N also pays it, and its office-visit copays do not apply to equipment. Plans K and L pay half and three-quarters of it.

The supply schedule is the part people rarely see written down. These are the usual maximums under Medicare's coverage policy:

Desert air matters here. Plenty of people who sleep fine in a humid Midwestern bedroom wake up with a dry mouth and nose in Peoria, and the heated humidifier and heated tubing are both on the covered list when your practitioner orders them. Ask at the start rather than putting up with it.

## Moving to Peoria with a CPAP you already own

This is the north Peoria version of the question, and Medicare has a separate rule for it.

If you got your machine before you joined Original Medicare, through an employer plan or an individual policy, Medicare can still pay for supplies, a rental or a replacement. It does **not** make you redo the 12-week trial or the usage download. It needs two things:

- **The old sleep study.** It has to be from before you enrolled, and it has to meet Medicare's current breathing-event criteria. Get the actual report from the sleep center or your old doctor, not just a note saying you have sleep apnea. Request it before you lose touch with the practice back home.
- **A new in-person visit after you enroll.** Your treating practitioner here records that you have obstructive sleep apnea and that you still use the machine.

If either piece is missing, the supplier's claim is denied, and the supplier usually finds out first. So the order of operations for someone just arrived is: request the old study, book a new primary care or sleep visit in the Arrowhead or Sun City area, and only then call a Medicare-enrolled supplier for masks. ## CPAP on a Medicare Advantage plan in Maricopa County

An Advantage plan has to cover CPAP, because it has to cover everything Original Medicare does. It just gets there differently:

- **Prior authorization** is common, for the machine and sometimes for the sleep study.
- **Contracted suppliers.** HMOs in particular will cover the machine only from the plan's equipment vendor. The friendly supplier your neighbor in Trilogy uses may not be in your network.
- **Cost-sharing** can be a flat copay or a percentage, and it may count toward the plan's out-of-pocket maximum. The plan's Evidence of Coverage spells it out under durable medical equipment.

Two situations deserve a phone call before December 7:

**Your plan is ending on December 31.** Several Maricopa County plans were discontinued for 2027. If you are in the middle of a 13-month rental on one of them, ask the new plan, or the supplier if you are moving to Original Medicare, how it handles a rental already in progress, and whether you will need to switch suppliers. Before the old plan ends, download or request your usage report and a copy of your sleep study. The [discontinued-plan guide for Peoria](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/) walks through the dates.

**You winter here and summer somewhere else.** On Original Medicare, any Medicare-enrolled supplier in any state can ship your supplies. On an HMO, supplies ordered outside the service area may not be covered at all. If you split the year, read [whether your Advantage plan works in another state](/blog/can-i-use-my-medicare-advantage-plan-in-another-state/) before you pick a plan for 2027.

## Where the weight-loss drugs fit

Zepbound is FDA-approved for moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity, which is the one door that lets a Part D plan cover it. That does not replace the CPAP rules above. ## Questions to ask before your sleep study

If you are 64 or 65 and a doctor has just mentioned a sleep study, a few questions now save a denial later:

- Is the doctor ordering the test enrolled in Medicare, and is the home test device one Medicare accepts?
- Which supplier will the doctor send the order to, and does that supplier accept assignment?
- Who books the follow-up visit, and is it on the calendar between day 31 and day 91?
- If I am on an Advantage plan, does the study or the machine need prior authorization, and which supplier does the plan use?
