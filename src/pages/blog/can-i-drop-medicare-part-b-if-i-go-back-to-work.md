**Yes. You can end Medicare Part B at any time by sending Social Security a written request, and CMS Form CMS-1763 says coverage stops the last day of the month after the month you submit it.** File on April 5 and Part B ends May 31. If a group plan through your own or your spouse's current job takes over with no gap, you can come back later with an 8-month special enrollment period and no late penalty. Whether you should is a separate question. Part B goes back on easily. A Medicare Supplement may not.

The question fits a common story in Vistancia and Trilogy. Someone retires at 65, does everything right, and at 67 takes a consulting contract or a part-time job with benefits along Lake Pleasant Parkway. Now they are paying {{partB.premium}} a month in {{year}} for coverage that sits behind a group plan, and they want to know if they can stop. Here is how I would work through it, as education and not as advice for your situation.

## Check these two things before you touch the form

**How many people work there?** With 20 or more employees, the group plan generally pays first and Medicare second. Under 20, it is usually the reverse: Medicare is primary, and some small-employer plans pay as though you have Part B whether you do or not. Drop it there and you may be paying for the portion nobody covers. A spouse's employer counts by the same test, based on the size of the company the spouse works for.

**Is the coverage from current work?** The federal rule, [42 CFR 407.20](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/407.20), requires coverage "by reason of the current employment status" of you or your spouse. Retiree benefits and COBRA do not qualify, however generous they look. I go through those in [Can I keep COBRA instead of Medicare at 65?](/blog/can-i-keep-cobra-instead-of-medicare-at-65/)

There is one more wrinkle in that rule. It describes people who either had group coverage when they were first eligible or took Part B during their initial enrollment period, and who then held one or the other every month after. If your Part B started through the General Enrollment Period, ask Social Security how the rule applies to you before you cancel anything.

## Get the timing right, because one gap costs you

The same rule is why the date matters so much. Every month has to be covered by Part B or by the group plan. A plan with a 60- or 90-day waiting period for new hires can leave you exposed, so get the first day of coverage in writing from HR first.

Because the end date is set by the month you file, the arithmetic is simple:

An overlap costs you one month's premium, while a gap can cost you the special enrollment period. When in doubt, file a month late rather than a month early.

The current form, revised in January 2026, tells you to mail or fax it to your local Social Security office. Older descriptions of the process mention an in-person interview, so go by the form itself. It also says that if you change your mind, you must contact Social Security before the end date. Keep the fax confirmation or a certified-mail receipt, and check your next statement to see that the premium stopped.

If your Advantage plan included drug coverage, your drug coverage goes with it. Ask HR for the plan's creditable coverage notice, because going more than 63 days without creditable drug coverage can start a separate Part D penalty.

The HSA line is worth reading twice, since a high-deductible plan with an employer deposit is often what makes dropping Part B look attractive. IRS Publication 969 says that beginning with the first month you are enrolled in Medicare, "your contribution limit is zero." The form says Part A can be terminated only if you pay a premium for it, so for most people there is no way back to contributing. Your tax advisor should see this before any money goes in.

## The Medigap question decides most of these

If you are on a Medigap policy, this is the real decision. The law protects you from health questions only "prior to or during the 6 month period" that began when you were first 65 and on Part B, under [42 U.S.C. 1395ss(s)(2)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1395ss). That window runs once. Dropping Part B does not stop the clock, and taking Part B again does not restart it.

Federal law has a pause button, but not for you. Subsection (q)(6) lets a policyholder suspend a Medigap policy while on a group plan only if they are entitled to Medicare through disability. Someone on Medicare by age has three choices:

1. **Keep Part B and the Medigap policy through the job.** You pay both premiums on top of your share of the group plan, and nothing is at risk.
2. **Drop Part B and keep paying for Medigap.** You hold your place at the price of a policy that does little in the meantime. This suits a short contract, though it is not cheap.
3. **Drop both.** This saves the most today. To buy Medigap again in Arizona you would usually pass health questions, because the state has no birthday rule. The federal guaranteed-issue list does cover an employer plan that supplements Medicare and then ends, but a primary plan at an active job may not be read that way, so do not plan on it without an insurer's answer in writing.

I would think hard before choosing the third at 67 with a blood pressure prescription and a cardiologist. I would think less hard at 66 with a clean history, a job you expect to keep for years and an Advantage plan you were happy with. Without Medigap you are not stranded: all 52 general Medicare Advantage plans in Maricopa County for 2027, by CMS's landscape files, take applicants without health questions when you have an enrollment period, and 45 of them include drug coverage. That route brings networks and a yearly out-of-pocket limit, which is the trade you would be accepting.

If you carried an Advantage plan and no Medigap, this section barely applies, and dropping Part B is a much simpler call.

## Coming back when the job ends

The return is the easy half, if you did the earlier steps properly:

- **The window.** Eight months, beginning the month after the job or the group coverage ends, whichever comes first. COBRA does not extend it.
- **The start date.** Sign up while still covered, or in the first full month after, and you can pick a start date that month or in any of the next three. Wait longer and Part B begins the month after you enroll, which leaves a stretch uninsured. Filing before your last day is the clean way to do it.
- **The paperwork.** Form CMS-L564 is where your employer confirms the coverage dates. Get it signed before you leave, since HR is easier to find while you still work there. The steps are in [How do I sign up for Part B when I retire after 65?](/blog/how-do-i-sign-up-for-medicare-part-b-when-i-retire-after-65/)
- **The penalty.** Group-plan months from current work are not counted against you under section 1839(b) of the Social Security Act. If something did go wrong, the [late enrollment penalty calculator](/tools/late-enrollment-penalty-calculator/) shows what it would cost.
- **Your plans.** You have two months after the employer coverage ends to join an Advantage or Part D plan through that special election. Otherwise it is the October 15 to December 7 period. In Arizona's 2027 stand-alone drug plan list, premiums run from $5.30 to $191.50 and none is $0, according to CMS's landscape files, so price that before you assume last time's choice still holds.

Missing the eight months sends you to the General Enrollment Period, January 1 through March 31, with a possible surcharge of 10% for each full 12 months.

## The order I would do it in

1. Ask HR for the employee count, the first day of coverage and the creditable drug notice, in writing.
2. Price both premiums, and your share of the deductible, against {{partB.annual}} a year for Part B at the {{year}} rate. Higher-income households should use their actual IRMAA figure instead.
3. Decide about Medigap first. It is the only step you may not be able to reverse.
4. File CMS-1763 in the month that lines up with the group plan's start date, and keep your proof.
5. Save a reminder for the day the job ends. The [enrollment timeline tool](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) lays out the dates.
