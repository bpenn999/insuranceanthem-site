**Your own Medicare does not change when your spouse dies.** Part A, Part B and whatever plan you are on, a Medigap policy, a Medicare Advantage plan or a Part D plan, are in your name alone. Your card, your doctors and your premium carry on as they were. The coverage that ends is coverage you had *through* your spouse, such as a plan from their employer. The cost that changes, a couple of years later, is the income-related surcharge called IRMAA. Its single-filer threshold is {{irmaa.singleStart}} in {{year}}, half the {{irmaa.jointStart}} joint line.

I sit down with widows and widowers in [Peoria](/service-area/peoria-az/) more often than with anyone else, mostly in Trilogy at Vistancia, Sun City West and the older streets around Westbrook Village. The conversation nearly always starts the same way: "I don't even know what I'm supposed to do with Medicare now." The honest answer is: less than you fear in the first month, and one thing worth planning for in the third year.

## What changes right away, and what does not

Medicare covers each person individually. Couples share no Medicare policy, so a death doesn't touch the survivor's enrollment. That is also why a husband and wife [can be on different plans](/blog/can-husband-and-wife-have-different-medicare-plans/) to begin with.

Social Security is normally told about a death by the funeral home. CMS then passes it to the plans. Under CMS's enrollment rules, your spouse's Advantage or Part D enrollment ends on the first day of the month after the death. The plan is not allowed to end it until CMS's records show the death. If a premium bill or a plan letter arrives in your spouse's name a few weeks later, that is the system catching up, not something you have to fix.

## If you were on your spouse's employer plan

This is the one situation where waiting costs money, and it comes up more often in north Peoria than people expect. Plenty of couples moved to the 85383 corridor while one spouse was still working remotely or commuting to the Valley's big employers. The other spouse turned 65, stayed on the working spouse's group plan, and put off Part B.

When the working spouse dies, three clocks start:

1. **Part B.** You get an 8-month Special Enrollment Period to sign up for Part B without the late penalty. It runs from when the job or the group coverage ended, whichever came first. Medicare.gov is explicit that the clock runs from the earlier of the two.
2. **COBRA does not stop that clock.** A surviving spouse can keep the group plan for up to 36 months under COBRA, but COBRA is not coverage from current employment. Staying on it past the 8 months while skipping Part B earns a lifetime penalty. I explain why in [the COBRA post](/blog/can-i-keep-cobra-instead-of-medicare-at-65/).
3. **Medigap and Part D.** When group coverage that paid alongside Medicare ends, you generally have a guaranteed right to buy certain Medigap plans, with no health questions, if you apply within 63 days. You also have a special period to join a Part D or Advantage plan, which runs until two months after the employer coverage ends.

If you are under 65 when your spouse dies, COBRA's 36 months may carry you all the way to Medicare. Write down the date it ends, and line up your [enrollment timeline](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) against it.

A retiree plan is a different animal. Some employers keep a surviving spouse on for life, some for a set period, some not at all, and the plan document decides. If it does end, the same 63-day Medigap right generally applies. Get the answer in writing before you cancel anything.

## The IRMAA change, and why it takes three years to arrive

This is the part almost nobody warns you about. IRMAA is the surcharge on top of Part B and Part D for higher incomes. Social Security sets it from your tax return two years back, and the brackets depend on how you filed that return.

Married couples filing jointly don't pay any surcharge until their modified adjusted gross income passes {{irmaa.jointStart}}. For single filers the line is {{irmaa.singleStart}}. In the year your spouse dies, the IRS still lets you file a joint return. After that you file single. Social Security's own table puts a "qualifying surviving spouse with dependent child" on the single-filer brackets too, so that status doesn't help with IRMAA.

The thresholds for 2027 and later haven't been published yet, but they always keep the same shape, with single at half of joint. Using {{year}}'s numbers: a couple with $200,000 of income pays no surcharge. The survivor often keeps far more than half of that, through the larger of the two Social Security checks, a survivor pension, and required distributions from an IRA rolled over in their own name. At $150,000 of single income, that survivor lands in the second tier. Part B becomes {{irmaa.t2.partB}} a month plus {{irmaa.t2.partD}} on top of the Part D premium. The surcharge isn't tied to the death; it shows up the first year Social Security uses a single return, and the letter arrives in late 2028.

You can't appeal your way out of that version, because nothing is wrong with the number. It's what single filers at that income pay. What does help is time. A survivor who sees it coming has two-plus years to think about which accounts to draw from. That is a conversation for your tax advisor, and the same logic I go through in [the Roth conversion post](/blog/does-a-roth-conversion-affect-medicare-premiums/). If the house in Vistancia or Westbrook Village is going to be sold, the timing of the sale matters too, for the reason in [the home-sale post](/blog/does-selling-my-house-affect-medicare-premiums/).

## When Form SSA-44 does help a widow or widower

The opposite case is just as common. One spouse had the pension or the paycheck, and that income stopped at the death. Social Security, though, is still billing the survivor a surcharge based on a joint return from two years back.

Death of a spouse is one of the eight life-changing events Social Security accepts on Form SSA-44. With it you can ask them to use a more recent year's income instead of the old return. Bring a copy of the death certificate and a reasonable estimate of this year's income. Before you file, work out your premium both ways, under the surcharge you have now and under your new income at the brackets you'll actually file under. The request only helps if income really fell. The [IRMAA appeal post](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/) walks through the form, and the [IRMAA estimator](/tools/irmaa-estimator/) shows which tier a given income lands in.

## Moving after a spouse dies

A lot of survivors leave Arizona within a year or two to be near family. Others move from a house in 85383 to something smaller across town. Your Medicare goes with you either way, but a plan may not:

- **A Medicare Advantage plan** only works in its service area. A permanent move out of it opens a special period to choose a new plan where you're going. A move inside Maricopa County that brings new plans within reach also qualifies. You can't keep the old plan in another state.
- **A Medigap policy** stays with you. It covers you anywhere that takes Medicare, and you don't need to change it to move. The premium may be repriced to your new address.
- **Part D** stand-alone plans are regional, so a move out of state means choosing a new one.

If a move is likely, it's worth knowing that before December 7, rather than locking in an Advantage plan for 2027 that you'll have to replace in the spring.

## A short checklist for the first few months

1. **Confirm Social Security has the death recorded.** The funeral home usually reports it. Ask Social Security about switching to the larger of the two benefits and the $255 lump-sum death payment.
2. **If you were on your spouse's work or retiree plan, get its end date in writing**, then count 8 months for Part B and 63 days for Medigap from it.
3. **Call your own Medigap carrier** and ask whether a household discount changes at renewal.
4. **Call your spouse's Medigap carrier**, if they had one, about cancelling and about any premium paid past the death.
5. **Put a note on your calendar for late in the second year** to check what your third-year IRMAA will be, and talk to your tax advisor before then if it looks close.
6. **If you are thinking about moving**, decide that before you pick a 2027 plan.

## The short version

Your Medicare stays as it is. If you were covered through your spouse's job, act within months, not years: Part B within 8 months of that coverage ending, Medigap within 63 days. The money question comes later. Most survivors who end up paying IRMAA see it arrive in the third Medicare year after the death, when Social Security first uses a single-filer return. That leaves time to plan, and an SSA-44 is there for the opposite case, when income dropped and the old joint surcharge no longer fits.
