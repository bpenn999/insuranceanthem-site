**Yes. If you were married at least ten years, you are not married now, and your former spouse is 62 or older with about 40 quarters of Medicare-covered work, their record makes your Part A premium-free at 65.** You do not need their permission, your claim takes nothing from their benefits, and they do not even have to be retired. What the record does not do is pay for Part B: that {{partB.premium}} a month in {{year}} is yours, the same as for every other enrollee.

I get this question more than people might guess in [Peoria](/service-area/peoria-az/). A good share of the people I meet in Vistancia, Trilogy and the newer streets off Lake Pleasant Parkway moved here after a divorce, or after a long marriage ended and the house back east was sold. Many of them, more often women, spent years raising children or running a family business that never put them on the payroll. They are the ones who reach 65 assuming they will owe the full Part A premium of {{partA.premiumIfUninsured}} a month, when a marriage that ended fifteen years ago has already paid for it.

## The four conditions for using a former spouse's record

Medicare's hospital side, Part A, is premium-free for anyone entitled, or entitled except for not having applied, to a monthly Social Security benefit at 65. A divorced spouse's benefit counts, and Social Security's rules for that benefit set the test. Every one of these has to be true:

1. **The marriage lasted at least ten years**, counted to the day the divorce became final, not the day you separated. Nine years and eleven months does not qualify. If a divorce is in progress now and you are close to the line, that date is worth knowing before it is set.
2. **You are not married now.** Social Security looks at your status at the time, not at your history.
3. **Your former spouse is at least 62 and insured.** For premium-free Part A that means roughly 40 quarters of work where Medicare tax came out. They do not have to have claimed anything.
4. **If your former spouse has not started Social Security, you have been divorced at least two years.** Once they are drawing benefits, the two-year wait no longer applies.

You also have to be 65, unless you qualify earlier through your own disability. And you do not have to start your own Social Security checks to use any of this. You can apply for Medicare on its own and leave your retirement benefit to grow.

Your Medigap open enrollment still runs six months from the month you are 65 and enrolled in Part B, and since Arizona has no birthday-month rule to fall back on, that window is the one to protect. ## If your ex is under 62, or you have remarried

Two situations break the rule, and both are common.

**Your former spouse is younger than you.** If you turn 65 while your ex is 60, their record is not available yet. It becomes usable in the month they turn 62. In the meantime you use your own record if it is enough, or pay the Part A premium for those months (the reduced rate below may apply), then go back to Social Security when your ex reaches 62 and ask to be moved onto their record. Skipping Part A to wait it out is rarely the answer: a Medicare Supplement or Advantage plan needs you enrolled in both Part A and Part B, and for anyone who has to buy Part A, signing up late adds a penalty. Part B and your Medigap window run on your own birthday regardless.

**You are married again.** While you are married to someone else, the former spouse's record is off the table for premium-free Part A. Your current spouse's record takes its place once you have been married a year, which I cover in [Can my spouse get Medicare when I turn 65?](/blog/can-my-spouse-get-medicare-when-i-turn-65/). If neither works and the later marriage ends, the earlier one can count again, because the test is whether you are married now.

## Widowed, or your former spouse has died

Survivors get a more generous version of the rule:

- **A widow or widower** generally needs a marriage of at least nine months before the death, not ten years.
- **A surviving divorced spouse**, whose former spouse died after the divorce, still needs the ten-year marriage.
- **In both cases, remarrying at 60 or older does not end it.** That exception is why I ask the remarried widows I meet in Sun City West and along the 85383 corridor when the second marriage happened, not just whether it did.

## The reduced-premium rule almost nobody mentions

Sometimes neither record reaches 40 quarters. Your own work stopped at 22, say, and your former spouse had 34 when you divorced. Social Security will not add the two together, but it does have a middle step: if you were married at least ten years and your former spouse had **30 or more quarters at the time of the divorce**, you pay the reduced Part A premium rather than the full one. Social Security's own manual says remarriage does not end that eligibility, which is the opposite of the premium-free rule above.

On {{year}}'s numbers, the reduced rate is roughly 45 percent less than {{partA.premiumIfUninsured}} a month, which adds up to thousands of dollars a year. The [Part A premium checker](/tools/part-a-premium/) runs the quarters test, and it is worth running for each record you might use.

## The IRMAA notice after a recent divorce

This one catches higher-earning households. Social Security sets your Part B and Part D premiums from the tax return two years back. If you divorced in the last two years, that return was probably a joint one, carrying both incomes, and the first Medicare bill can arrive with an IRMAA surcharge built on a household that no longer exists.

Divorce is one of the eight life-changing events Social Security accepts on Form SSA-44. File it with the divorce decree and an estimate of your income this year, and the surcharge is reset from your own numbers. [How do I appeal IRMAA after retirement?](/blog/how-do-i-appeal-irmaa-after-retirement/) shows how the form works, and if a property settlement or a retirement-account split is moving money around this year, have your tax advisor look at it before you put an estimate on the form.

## How to claim it when you turn 65

The online application handles the common case, a person on their own record. A former spouse's record usually goes more smoothly by phone or at a Social Security office, where you can say up front that you are applying on a former spouse's record. Have these ready:

- **Your marriage certificate**, the original or a certified copy.
- **The divorce decree**, so the ten years can be counted to the date it became final.
- **Your former spouse's Social Security number** if you have it. If not, their full name, date of birth and, where you know it, their place of birth.
- **For a late spouse**, the death certificate as well.

[What documents do I need to apply for Medicare?](/blog/what-documents-do-i-need-to-apply-for-medicare/) has the full list, including which copies Social Security accepts. Apply during the three months before your birthday month so the record question is settled before coverage starts. The [enrollment timeline](/tools/enrollment-timeline/) shows your exact dates.

## What to do in the three months before you turn 65

1. **Check your own quarters first.** If your record already has 40, none of this is needed.
2. **Pin down the dates.** Your wedding date, the date the divorce became final, and your former spouse's date of birth decide everything above.
3. **If you remarried, note when.** For a widow or widower, the age at remarriage matters; for a divorced spouse, being married now is what matters.
4. **Gather the certificates and decree** before you call, not after Social Security asks.
5. **Look at the IRMAA year.** If the return two years back was joint and the household is now one person, plan to file SSA-44 as soon as the notice comes.
