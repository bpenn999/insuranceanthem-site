**If you have Medicare because of Social Security disability, turning 65 does not end it and you do not reapply. Your Part A and Part B continue without a gap; Social Security simply switches the reason you are entitled from disability to age.** You also get a fresh set of enrollment windows. Any Part B late-enrollment surcharge is rolled back to the standard premium, {{partB.premium}} a month in {{year}}, starting the month you turn 65. Any Part D late penalty ends, and a six-month Medigap open enrollment period opens.

That last one is the part that matters most in Arizona. Federal law does not require insurers to sell Medigap to anyone under 65, Arizona has never added that requirement, and no insurer here offers it voluntarily. So if you went on Medicare early, you have probably been on Original Medicare alone or in a Medicare Advantage plan for years, with no other choice. At 65, for six months, you can buy any Medigap policy sold here, and the insurer cannot turn you down or charge more because of your health.

I see this most in [Peoria](/service-area/peoria-az/), where plenty of households in 85383 and the older 85345 neighborhoods include one spouse who went on disability in their fifties. The 65th birthday tends to get treated as a non-event because "I already have Medicare." It is the only birthday that reopens things.

The last row confuses people. The check does not change at 65. Federal regulations convert a disability benefit to a retirement benefit automatically at full retirement age, and for anyone turning 65 in 2027 that is two years later. Medicare does not wait for that conversion. The changes in this article are tied to your 65th birthday, not to the check.

## The penalties you may have been paying stop

People on disability often miss a deadline the first time around. The 24-month wait makes the enrollment window easy to overlook, and money is tight. If that left you with a surcharge, 65 clears it.

**Part B.** Social Security's operations manual is plain on this: if a premium surcharge was in effect before age 65, the premium is rolled back to the standard amount effective with the month you turn 65. It also says months before 65 are never counted toward a later surcharge. If you dropped Part B while you were disabled, you get a new initial enrollment period at 65, the same seven months anyone turning 65 gets, with nothing carried over.

**Part D.** CMS's late-penalty guidance says someone who had Medicare before 65 gets a "subsequent" Part D initial enrollment period at 65. It runs from three months before your birthday month through three months after. The penalty ends the day before that window opens, and your record of uncovered months resets to zero. A gap of 63 days or more without drug coverage after the new window closes starts a fresh penalty, so the reset only helps if you keep coverage from then on. The [late enrollment penalty calculator](/tools/late-enrollment-penalty-calculator/) shows what a future gap would cost.

One wrinkle: Medicare treats you as 65 the day before your birthday. If your birthday falls on the 1st, every date in that row moves back one month.

If your birthday is in January or February, your new window overlaps the Annual Enrollment Period that runs through December 7. That gives you a choice of which window to use, but it does not double the decision. Make the Medigap decision first and the drug-plan decision around it, not the other way round.

## Medigap at 65: the decision that does not come back

Medicare.gov defines the Medigap open enrollment period as the six months that start the first day of the month you are 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. It does not matter that you have had Part B for years. If you have kept Part B, your window opens on the first of your birthday month whether you act or not, and it closes six months later whether you act or not.

Here is why it matters more for you than for the typical 64-year-old. Arizona has no birthday rule and no under-65 Medigap access. Even the 2026 bill to change that, HB 2433, was written only for people with ALS or kidney failure, not disability in general. Outside this window, an Arizona Medigap insurer can ask health questions and decline you. For someone whose disability came from a heart condition, a back injury, MS or cancer, the medical history is exactly what underwriting looks at. This six-month window is the only time it cannot be used against you.

Because you have already had Medicare, which counts as prior coverage, a Medigap policy bought in this window generally cannot hold back coverage of a condition you already have.

What it does not decide for you is which plan. [Plan G versus Plan N](/blog/plan-g-vs-plan-n-arizona-which-should-i-choose/) is the usual question. The premium is the cost of the policy; the benefit is that any Arizona doctor or hospital that takes Medicare takes you. That includes the specialists at Arrowhead or in Sun City that a narrow network may have kept out of reach. The [Medigap open enrollment post](/blog/when-is-medicare-supplement-open-enrollment-arizona/) has the mechanics of applying.

## If you are in a Medicare Advantage plan now

Most disabled Arizonans who wanted more than Original Medicare ended up in a Medicare Advantage plan, because it was the only option. In Maricopa County that menu is large: CMS's 2027 plan landscape files list 45 general Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage, before counting special needs plans.

At 65 you get two things. The new Part D window lets you join a different plan with drug coverage. CMS's current enrollment guidance also gives you a coordinating special enrollment period that runs alongside it. That period lets you leave Medicare Advantage for Original Medicare, or move to an Advantage plan without drug coverage. Pair a move to Original Medicare with your Medigap window and a stand-alone drug plan, and you have made a switch that would otherwise mean medical underwriting.

The catch is that this is not a trial. People who turn 65 and join Medicare Advantage for the first time get a 12-month right to change their mind and go back with guaranteed Medigap. CMS's guidance states that people who had Medicare before 65 do not get that period. Whatever you choose at 65, you make it without the safety net a first-time enrollee has. If you stay in Medicare Advantage now and want Medigap in three years, you will be answering health questions.

If you are happy with your plan, keep it. Nothing forces a change. The point is to decide on purpose while the door is open, rather than discover in 2029 that it closed.

## A short checklist for the months before you turn 65

1. **Pull up your Medicare record** at Medicare.gov and confirm Part B is active. If you turned Part B down while disabled, your new seven-month window around 65 is when to take it, without a penalty.
2. **Check your Part D premium** on the first bill after your new drug-plan window opens. If a late penalty is still on it, ask the plan to correct it. CMS's guidance tells plans to stop billing it.
3. **Decide on Medigap before your birthday month.** Get quotes in the month or two before, so the policy can start when your window opens.
4. **If you are in Medicare Advantage, check your doctors and drugs** against Original Medicare plus a drug plan before you decide to stay. The [discontinued-plan checklist](/blog/medicare-advantage-plan-discontinued-what-to-do-peoria-az-2027/) walks through the network check.
5. **Have a conversation, not a sales pitch.** I do these reviews by phone or in person. If you would like the dates and options for your birthday month laid out, you can [book a Turning 65 review](/book/?topic=t65).

None of this changes what Medicare covers. Turning 65 on disability does not add or remove a single benefit. It changes your price, your penalties and, in Arizona, whether a Medigap insurer can say no.
